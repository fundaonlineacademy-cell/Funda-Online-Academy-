# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 1: E-commerce Fundamentals & Business Readiness
### Lesson 5: South African E-commerce Law, Ethics & Consumer Protection Foundations

**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain why e-commerce operations must consider legal and ethical responsibilities.
2. Identify the broad roles of ECTA, the Consumer Protection Act and POPIA in South African e-commerce.
3. Recognise common customer-information, pricing, privacy and marketing risks.
4. Distinguish statutory obligations from voluntary store policies.
5. Escalate legally uncertain decisions instead of inventing rules.

## Why This Lesson Matters
An online store makes commercial promises through product information, pricing, policies, marketing, data collection and transaction processes. In South Africa, those activities can be affected by electronic-transaction, consumer-protection and personal-information rules. Learners do not need to become lawyers, but they must understand that store design and operations have compliance consequences.

This lesson provides operational awareness and should not be treated as individual legal advice. Detailed legal topics are revisited later where they become operationally relevant.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **ECTA** — Electronic Communications and Transactions Act 25 of 2002.
- **CPA** — Consumer Protection Act 68 of 2008.
- **POPIA** — Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013.
- **Consumer** — A person or entity protected under applicable consumer law in the relevant circumstances.
- **Disclosure** — Information made available so a customer can understand material facts about a transaction.
- **Personal information** — Information relating to an identifiable person or juristic person where POPIA applies.
- **Direct marketing** — Promotional communication directed to a person through relevant channels.
- **Statutory right** — A right created by legislation rather than only by a store's voluntary policy.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Law is part of store design
Compliance is not something added after launch. The wording of product pages, checkout, policies, consent controls and customer communications can affect whether customers receive accurate and fair information.

### 2. ECTA and electronic transactions
ECTA contains rules relevant to electronic contracting and online suppliers. Among other matters, it addresses information that certain online suppliers should make available to consumers and creates cooling-off rights for qualifying electronic transactions, subject to important exclusions.

A store should therefore provide transparent supplier and transaction information rather than hiding important terms until after payment.

### 3. Consumer Protection Act foundations
The CPA addresses matters such as fair marketing, product information, consumer rights and the quality of goods in circumstances where the Act applies. Businesses should avoid false or misleading representations and should not assume that a voluntary store policy can remove statutory remedies.

For example, a store's “7-day returns only” statement does not automatically determine the outcome of every defective-goods complaint. The legal category of the complaint matters.

### 4. POPIA foundations
E-commerce businesses commonly process names, contact details, addresses, order information and other personal data. POPIA requires responsible handling of personal information under applicable processing conditions.

Collecting information “because the system allows it” is not a good privacy principle. The business should understand why information is needed, who can access it, how it is protected and how long it should be retained under the relevant purpose and requirements.

### 5. Pricing and marketing ethics
A promotion should not create a false impression about price, scarcity, product performance or delivery. Ethical marketing supports informed choice rather than exploiting confusion.

Examples of risky practices include fabricated reviews, invented reference prices, false countdown timers, unsupported health or performance claims, and deliberately hiding unavoidable costs until the customer is committed.

### 6. Store policies versus legal rights
Store policies can provide useful procedures and may give customers additional benefits. They should not be drafted as if the business can simply contract out of all legal duties. When legislation applies, it may create rights or obligations independent of the store's preferred process.

### 7. Customer data should be treated as an asset and a responsibility
Personal information can help deliver orders and support customers, but misuse can create harm and regulatory risk. Staff should have appropriate access, protect credentials and avoid exposing customer details publicly or using data for unrelated purposes without an appropriate basis.

### 8. Escalate uncertainty
Frontline staff should know when a case requires specialist review. Unusual refund disputes, privacy complaints, data incidents, health or safety allegations, misleading-advertising complaints or questions about statutory rights should be documented and escalated rather than decided from memory.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A store advertises “Free national delivery” but excludes several regions in checkout without disclosing the limitation earlier. Even before considering the exact legal rule, this creates a transparency and trust problem. The business should correct the claim so customers understand the real conditions and review the promotion against applicable consumer requirements.

## Practical Activity
Audit a fictional online store under four headings: supplier information, product/pricing claims, privacy/data handling and returns/customer rights. Identify two risks in each area and state which team member or adviser should own the correction.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why should compliance be considered before launch rather than after complaints occur?
2. What broad area does POPIA regulate?
3. Why can a voluntary store returns policy not decide every customer remedy?
4. Give two examples of potentially misleading marketing practices.
5. What should staff do when they are unsure which legal rule applies?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Store design, data collection, claims and transaction processes can create compliance obligations from the beginning.
2. Responsible processing of personal information.
3. Applicable legislation may provide statutory rights independently of the store policy.
4. Examples include fake reference prices, false scarcity, fabricated reviews and unsupported claims.
5. Record the facts and escalate for authoritative review rather than guessing.

## Key Takeaways
- South African e-commerce can involve ECTA, the CPA and POPIA.
- Compliance affects product pages, checkout, policies, marketing and data handling.
- Store policies cannot simply remove statutory rights.
- Customer information should be collected and used responsibly.
- Ethical marketing and accurate disclosure support both trust and compliance.
- Legal uncertainty should be escalated and verified.
