# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 1: E-commerce Fundamentals & Business Readiness
### Lesson 8: E-commerce Business Planning & Readiness

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Combine customer, commercial, operational and risk considerations into an e-commerce concept.
2. Define essential requirements before platform selection.
3. Identify assumptions that need testing before launch.
4. Prioritise immediate, desirable and future capabilities.
5. Produce a basic e-commerce readiness brief.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A strong online store begins with a clear problem and operating model, not with a theme or technology purchase. Planning helps a business identify what the store must do, who will operate it, what it will cost and what could prevent it from serving customers reliably. This lesson closes Module 1 and prepares learners for the platform and store-setup decisions in Module 2.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Requirement** — A capability or condition the business needs the store or operation to satisfy.
- **Assumption** — A belief treated as true until it is tested or verified.
- **Constraint** — A limit such as budget, staff capacity, time, geography or technology.
- **Readiness** — The degree to which the business can operate the planned e-commerce service reliably.
- **Priority** — The relative importance or urgency of a requirement.
- **Business case** — A reasoned explanation of why an initiative should be undertaken and what resources or outcomes it involves.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Begin with the customer problem
A store concept should explain who the customer is, what is being offered and why online purchase is useful. “We need a website” is a technology statement, not a business requirement.

### 2. Define the offer and catalogue scope
Determine whether the business sells physical goods, services, digital products or a combination. Estimate the number of products and variants, product-data requirements, stock approach and fulfilment complexity.

### 3. Define functional requirements
Common functional requirements include:
- product browsing and search;
- cart and checkout;
- payment options;
- delivery or collection selection;
- order confirmation;
- inventory or availability handling;
- customer support contact;
- reporting and analytics.

Requirements should be specific enough to test. “Easy checkout” is vague. “A customer can choose delivery or collection before order confirmation” is more testable.

### 4. Define operational requirements
The business must know who will:
- add and maintain products;
- verify prices and stock;
- monitor orders;
- handle payment exceptions;
- pick and pack;
- book delivery;
- respond to customers;
- process returns;
- maintain access and security.

Technology cannot compensate for undefined responsibility.

### 5. Understand commercial constraints
Budget should include more than initial setup. Subscription fees, transaction charges, development, applications, marketing, packaging, delivery, staff and returns can all affect total cost.

The business should estimate what level of sales and margin is required to support the model rather than assume digital selling is automatically cheaper than physical retail.

### 6. Identify compliance and risk requirements
Relevant considerations can include customer disclosures, product claims, privacy, direct marketing, payment security, returns, fraud controls and record keeping. Some risks can be reduced through platform features, but responsibility still remains with the business.

### 7. Prioritise requirements
A useful classification is:
- **Essential:** required for safe and viable operation at launch.
- **Desirable:** useful but not required for initial operation.
- **Future:** planned for later growth.

This prevents limited budgets from being consumed by decorative features while critical payment, delivery or security needs remain unresolved.

### 8. Test assumptions
Examples of assumptions include:
- customers are willing to pay the planned delivery charge;
- a supplier can replenish stock quickly;
- the chosen courier serves all target areas;
- staff can process a certain order volume;
- the target market prefers a particular payment method.

Important assumptions should be tested through research, quotations, pilot orders or other evidence before major commitments are made.

### 9. Define readiness criteria
A business may be considered ready to proceed to detailed store setup when it can explain:
1. target customer;
2. offer and value proposition;
3. catalogue scope;
4. required store functions;
5. payment approach;
6. fulfilment and delivery approach;
7. support and returns ownership;
8. budget and resources;
9. major compliance/security needs;
10. measurable launch objectives.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A small stationery business wants national online sales. Its first idea includes 500 products, same-day national delivery and four marketing channels. Readiness analysis reveals limited staff, incomplete product data and a courier service that cannot support same-day national delivery. The business reduces launch scope to 60 high-demand products, realistic courier timeframes and two priority marketing channels. Better planning creates a more achievable launch.

## Practical Activity
Create a two-page **E-commerce Readiness Brief** containing target customer, value proposition, offer type, catalogue scope, eight essential requirements, five operational owners, major costs, three constraints, five assumptions to verify, three risks and eight launch-readiness criteria.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why is “we need a website” not a sufficient business requirement?
2. Give three operational responsibilities that must be assigned.
3. What is the difference between an essential and a future requirement?
4. Why should assumptions be tested?
5. Name five areas that should be clear before detailed store setup begins.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. It does not explain the customer need, required functionality or business outcome.
2. Examples include catalogue maintenance, order monitoring, fulfilment, delivery and support.
3. Essential requirements are necessary for viable launch; future requirements can be postponed.
4. Untested assumptions can cause expensive platform or operational decisions.
5. Examples include customer, offer, catalogue, payments, delivery, support, budget and risk.

## Key Takeaways
- E-commerce planning begins with business and customer requirements.
- Functional and operational requirements must both be defined.
- Total cost and staff capacity affect readiness.
- Essential capabilities should be prioritised ahead of optional features.
- Important assumptions should be verified before major commitments.
- A readiness brief prepares the business for evidence-based platform selection.
