# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 1: E-commerce Fundamentals & Business Readiness
### Lesson 6: E-commerce Operations, Inventory, Fulfilment & Customer Service Overview

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the basic operational flow from order to completion.
2. Identify the roles of inventory, payment, fulfilment, delivery and customer service.
3. Recognise common operational exceptions that require intervention.
4. Explain why order records and status accuracy matter.
5. Map responsibilities across a simple e-commerce operation.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A customer sees one store, but behind that store are several connected processes. Once an order is placed, the business must know whether payment succeeded, whether the correct stock exists, who will prepare the order, how it will be delivered and who will respond if something goes wrong. This lesson provides the operational foundation for the deeper order, fulfilment and customer-service modules later in the course.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Inventory** — Goods or stock available for sale or operational use.
- **Order** — A recorded customer request to purchase goods or services.
- **Fulfilment** — Preparing and supplying the order to the customer.
- **Dispatch** — Handing a prepared order into the delivery or collection process.
- **Exception** — A problem that prevents the normal workflow from continuing.
- **Customer service** — Support provided before, during or after a transaction.
- **Reconciliation** — Comparing related records to confirm that they agree.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. The order-to-completion flow
A typical physical-product order may move through:
1. order creation;
2. payment verification;
3. stock allocation;
4. picking;
5. packing;
6. dispatch;
7. delivery or collection;
8. completion and after-sales support.

The exact labels depend on the platform, but the underlying operational questions remain similar.

### 2. Inventory accuracy
A store should avoid selling stock it does not actually have unless a controlled backorder or pre-order process exists. Inventory errors create cancellations, delays and customer complaints. Product variants make this more important because the correct product family is not enough—the exact size, colour or model must be available.

### 3. Payment and order status are different
An order record can exist while payment is pending or failed. The business should use an approved payment record or system state rather than assume that an order is paid because the customer says so or sends a screenshot.

### 4. Fulfilment accuracy
Picking and packing convert the digital order into a physical shipment. Staff should verify the exact item, variant and quantity. Suitable packaging should protect the goods without unnecessary cost or waste.

### 5. Delivery is part of the customer promise
Delivery methods, geographic coverage, charges and time estimates should reflect the business's real capability. Tracking and proof-of-delivery records can support customer communication and exception investigation.

### 6. Customer service connects the system
Customer-service staff need reliable information from orders, payments, stock and delivery systems. A helpful response should be based on verified facts. Support cannot compensate for permanently weak operations, so repeated complaints should be analysed for root causes.

### 7. Manage exceptions rather than hide them
Common exceptions include failed payment, unavailable stock, wrong item, damaged stock, incomplete address, failed delivery, cancellation request or duplicate transaction. Changing a status to make a dashboard appear clean does not resolve the underlying issue.

### 8. Reconcile records
Orders, payment records, stock changes and fulfilment events should tell a consistent story. Reconciliation helps identify missing payments, duplicate transactions, paid-but-unfulfilled orders and other discrepancies.

### 9. Define responsibility
Small businesses may have one person performing several roles, while larger operations separate functions. Either way, responsibilities should be clear: who monitors new orders, who verifies payments, who packs, who updates tracking and who handles escalations?

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A customer buys two shirts. The order exists, but the payment dashboard shows pending. Warehouse staff should not dispatch merely because the order appears in the system. Once payment is verified, the correct sizes are allocated, picked and packed. Tracking is added after dispatch, and any delivery exception remains visible until resolved.

## Practical Activity
Draw a swim-lane workflow for an imaginary store showing Customer, Store System, Payment, Warehouse and Courier. Add at least one exception and responsible owner at every major stage.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why can an order exist without being ready for fulfilment?
2. What is stock allocation?
3. Why should variant information be checked during picking?
4. Give three common order exceptions.
5. What does reconciliation help identify?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Payment, stock or risk checks may still be unresolved.
2. Reserving or assigning available stock to an order.
3. Similar products may have different sizes, colours or models.
4. Examples include failed payment, unavailable stock and delivery failure.
5. Missing, duplicate or inconsistent records across connected processes.

## Key Takeaways
- E-commerce operations connect payment, stock, fulfilment, delivery and service.
- Accurate statuses must reflect real events.
- Inventory and variant accuracy protect fulfilment quality.
- Exceptions should stay visible until properly resolved.
- Clear responsibility and reconciliation reduce operational loss.
