# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 1: E-commerce Fundamentals & Business Readiness
### Lesson 1: E-commerce Models, Ecosystem & Value Creation

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Define e-commerce and distinguish it from general digital business activity.
2. Explain common B2C, B2B, C2C and direct-to-consumer models.
3. Identify the main participants in an e-commerce ecosystem.
4. Explain how an online store creates value for customers and the business.
5. Evaluate whether a proposed e-commerce model is operationally realistic.

## Why This Lesson Matters
E-commerce is more than putting products on a website. A successful online transaction depends on a connected system of customers, products, technology, payments, stock, delivery, customer service and data. Understanding the business model first helps learners make better decisions later about platforms, pricing, fulfilment, marketing and security.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **E-commerce** — Buying and selling goods or services through electronic networks, normally involving a digital transaction process.
- **B2C** — Business-to-consumer commerce.
- **B2B** — Business-to-business commerce.
- **C2C** — Consumer-to-consumer commerce, often enabled by a marketplace.
- **D2C** — Direct-to-consumer selling by a producer or brand without relying entirely on traditional retail intermediaries.
- **Marketplace** — A platform that enables multiple sellers or providers to reach buyers under platform rules.
- **Value proposition** — The reason a defined customer would choose an offer.
- **E-commerce ecosystem** — The connected participants, systems and processes required to support online trade.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. What e-commerce includes
An e-commerce transaction normally involves more than a digital advertisement. The customer must be able to understand an offer, decide what to buy, provide required information, complete or arrange payment, and receive the product or service. Behind the scenes, the business must record the order, manage stock or service capacity, fulfil the transaction and provide support.

Digital marketing may attract a customer, but marketing alone is not e-commerce. Likewise, having a social-media page does not automatically mean a business has a reliable commerce operation. The complete transaction and operational process matters.

### 2. Common e-commerce models
In **B2C**, a retailer sells directly to individual consumers. In **B2B**, one business sells to another and may use negotiated pricing, account approval or bulk ordering. **C2C** often takes place through marketplaces that connect individuals. **D2C** describes producers or brands selling directly to customers rather than relying only on wholesalers and retailers.

A business may use more than one model. A clothing producer could sell directly through its own website, supply independent retailers and also list selected products on a marketplace. Each channel creates different costs, customer relationships and operational requirements.

### 3. The e-commerce ecosystem
A practical ecosystem can include:
- the merchant or supplier;
- customers;
- an e-commerce platform;
- payment providers;
- banks or other financial participants;
- couriers and fulfilment partners;
- suppliers and manufacturers;
- marketing channels;
- customer-service staff;
- analytics and security services.

A weakness in one part can affect the whole customer experience. A technically excellent store still fails if stock records are inaccurate or orders are never dispatched.

### 4. Value creation for customers
Customers may choose online shopping for convenience, wider selection, easier comparison, delivery, access outside normal retail hours or specialised products. But value depends on the target customer. A national store may compete on range and delivery reach, while a local store may compete on same-day service or specialist knowledge.

The value proposition should be specific and supportable. “Best online store” is not a useful operational strategy. “Affordable study bundles for university students with clear pricing and national courier delivery” gives the business something concrete to design around.

### 5. Value creation for the business
E-commerce can help a business reach customers beyond one physical location, automate parts of ordering, collect useful demand information and create repeat-purchase relationships. It also introduces costs: platform fees, payment charges, packaging, delivery, advertising, returns, fraud losses, technical maintenance and customer support.

Digital reach therefore does not equal automatic profitability. A viable model must connect revenue with the real cost of serving customers.

### 6. Own store versus third-party channels
An independent store generally offers stronger control over branding and the customer journey, but the merchant must generate traffic and operate the technology. Marketplaces may provide existing demand and infrastructure, but sellers accept platform fees, policies and limits.

A channel should be chosen because it supports the business model, not because it is fashionable.

### 7. Test business-model realism
Before building, ask:
1. Who is the target customer?
2. What exactly is being sold?
3. Why would the customer buy online?
4. How will payment be handled?
5. How will the order be fulfilled?
6. Who handles enquiries and returns?
7. What are the major costs?
8. What risks could prevent reliable delivery of the promise?

These questions turn an idea into an operational concept.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A small Eastern Cape craft producer wants to sell handmade homeware nationally. The value proposition is not simply “buy crafts online.” The business defines a limited catalogue, reliable product dimensions, online payment, protective packaging, courier delivery, realistic dispatch times and a clear support channel. The e-commerce model becomes a connected service rather than a collection of social-media posts.

## Practical Activity
Choose an imaginary South African online business. Create an ecosystem map showing the customer, merchant, platform, payment provider, supplier, delivery process and customer-service process. Then write one sentence explaining the store's value proposition and three operational risks that could prevent it from delivering that value.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. E-commerce includes the transaction and supporting operational process, not only promotion.
2. B2C sells to consumers; B2B sells to other businesses.
3. Examples include merchant, customer, platform, payment provider, supplier and courier.
4. Online selling still carries product, payment, fulfilment, support, marketing and risk costs.
5. It identifies meaningful, supportable value for a defined customer.

## Key Takeaways
- E-commerce is a connected commercial and operational system.
- Business models affect technology, costs and customer relationships.
- Every store depends on an ecosystem of participants and processes.
- A clear value proposition guides later store decisions.
- Digital reach must be supported by realistic economics and fulfilment.
