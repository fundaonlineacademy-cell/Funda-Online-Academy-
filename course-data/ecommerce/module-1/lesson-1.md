# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 1: E-commerce Foundations & Digital Business Models
### Lesson 1: Understanding E-commerce & the Digital Marketplace

**Estimated study time:** 35–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- Define e-commerce and distinguish it from broader digital business activity.
- Identify the main participants and processes in an online commercial transaction.
- Explain how digital marketplaces differ from physical retail environments.
- Recognise common opportunities and limitations of e-commerce for small businesses.
- Map a simple online customer transaction from discovery to after-sales support.

## Why This Lesson Matters
E-commerce is not simply “selling on the internet.” A successful online business depends on how customers discover an offer, evaluate it, trust the seller, pay, receive the product or service, and obtain support afterwards. Understanding the full system helps a learner make better decisions about store design, pricing, marketing, fulfilment, customer service and risk. It is also valuable for employment because many retail, administration, marketing and customer-service roles now involve digital commerce tools.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
**E-commerce** – The buying and selling of goods or services through electronic networks, usually the internet, where important parts of the commercial transaction happen digitally.

**Digital marketplace** – An online environment where buyers and sellers interact, compare offers and complete or begin transactions.

**Online store** – A website or app operated by a seller where products or services are displayed and customers can place orders.

**Marketplace platform** – A third-party platform that hosts many sellers and gives customers a shared place to browse and buy.

**Transaction** – A commercial exchange in which a customer agrees to provide payment or another agreed form of value in return for goods or services.

**Fulfilment** – The operational process of preparing, packing, dispatching, delivering or otherwise completing a customer order.

**Conversion** – A desired customer action, such as completing a purchase, registering an account or submitting an enquiry.

**Customer journey** – The sequence of interactions a person has with a business before, during and after a purchase.

## Main Lesson Content
E-commerce refers to commercial activity conducted through electronic systems. In a typical online purchase, a customer may first see a social-media post, search for a product, visit a store, compare options, read product information, add an item to a basket, pay online, receive confirmation and later receive delivery. Each of those steps is part of the e-commerce environment. The website is important, but the complete system also includes marketing, stock information, payment processing, order records, fulfilment, delivery, communication and after-sales support.

It is useful to distinguish e-commerce from the wider idea of digital business. Digital business can include many activities that are not direct sales, such as online appointment booking, digital collaboration, electronic recordkeeping, remote customer support or internal workflow systems. E-commerce specifically focuses on commercial exchanges. A business may therefore be highly digital without operating a full online store, while another business may rely on e-commerce as its main route to market.

The digital marketplace contains several participants. The customer creates demand and makes a buying decision. The seller creates and presents the offer, controls product information and accepts responsibility for delivering what was promised. A payment service helps move money securely. A courier or fulfilment partner may handle physical delivery. A platform provider may host the store or marketplace. Marketing channels such as search engines, social networks or email help customers discover the offer. In some businesses, these functions are handled internally; in others, several external service providers are involved.

One major difference between online and physical retail is the absence of immediate face-to-face inspection. A customer in a physical shop can usually touch a product, ask a staff member a question and leave with the item immediately. An online customer depends more heavily on photographs, descriptions, reviews, sizing information, delivery estimates, return policies and trust signals. This means information quality is a core operational issue, not merely a marketing detail. If a product listing is unclear, the business may face abandoned baskets, complaints, returns or negative reviews.

E-commerce can create strong opportunities for small businesses. A seller can reach customers beyond the immediate neighbourhood, operate without a large retail floor, display a broad catalogue and automate parts of ordering and communication. Online data can also help a business understand which products attract attention, where customers leave the buying process and which marketing activities produce sales. However, online selling also creates challenges. Competition can be intense, customers can compare prices quickly, fraud risk exists, delivery failures can damage trust and poor cybersecurity can expose business or customer information.

A useful way to understand an e-commerce business is to map the complete customer journey. The first stage is awareness, when the customer discovers the business. The next stage is consideration, when the customer compares products, prices and credibility. The transaction stage includes selecting products, checkout and payment. Fulfilment follows when the seller prepares and delivers the order. After-sales activity includes support, returns, reviews, repeat purchases and relationship building. A weak point at any stage can affect the whole result. Excellent advertising cannot compensate for an unreliable checkout, and a beautiful website cannot compensate for repeated delivery problems.

Learners should also recognise that e-commerce may involve physical goods, digital products, subscriptions, bookings or services. The operational requirements differ. A physical product needs stock and delivery arrangements. A digital product may require controlled access and reliable electronic delivery. A service business may use e-commerce tools to collect payment and schedule work. The commercial principles remain similar: the offer must be clear, the customer must understand the price and conditions, the transaction must be recorded, and the business must deliver what was promised.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A home-based candle business promotes products on social media. A customer taps a link, enters the online store, reads fragrance descriptions, chooses two candles, pays through a payment gateway and receives an order confirmation. The seller receives the order, checks stock, packs the items, books a courier and sends tracking information. After delivery, the customer receives a follow-up message asking for feedback.

This is one complete e-commerce journey. Social media supported discovery, the store supported evaluation and ordering, the payment gateway handled payment, fulfilment handled preparation and dispatch, and follow-up supported the customer relationship.

## Practical Activity
Choose any product or service that could realistically be sold online. Draw or write a six-stage customer journey covering: discovery, product evaluation, checkout, payment, fulfilment/delivery and after-sales support. For each stage, identify one thing that could go wrong and one action the business could take to reduce that risk.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is the difference between e-commerce and broader digital business activity?
2. Why is accurate product information especially important in online retail?
3. Name three participants that may be involved in an online transaction besides the buyer and seller.
4. What does fulfilment mean in an e-commerce context?
5. Give one opportunity and one limitation of selling online.

## Feedback / Explanation for Knowledge Check
1. E-commerce focuses on commercial buying and selling, while digital business includes wider digital operations that may not involve a sale.
2. Customers cannot always inspect products physically, so they depend on online information to judge suitability and trust the offer.
3. Examples include a payment provider, courier, marketplace platform, hosting provider or marketing platform.
4. Fulfilment is the process of completing the order operationally, including preparing, packing, dispatching or delivering it.
5. An opportunity could be wider market reach; a limitation could be competition, delivery complexity, fraud or cybersecurity risk.

## Key Takeaways
- E-commerce is a complete commercial system, not only a website.
- The customer journey extends from discovery through after-sales support.
- Product information, payment, fulfilment and customer communication all affect trust and conversion.
- Online selling can expand market reach but also creates operational, security and service risks.
- Strong e-commerce practice requires the business to manage the entire transaction reliably.
