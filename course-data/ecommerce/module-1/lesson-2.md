# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 1: E-commerce Foundations & Digital Business Models
### Lesson 2: E-commerce Business Models & Revenue Models

**Estimated study time:** 35–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- Distinguish major e-commerce business models by the parties involved.
- Explain common revenue models used by online businesses.
- Identify advantages and operational demands associated with different models.
- Match a business idea to a suitable business and revenue model.
- Explain why a model must be commercially viable, not merely popular.

## Why This Lesson Matters
An online business can fail even when the website looks professional if the underlying business model is weak. Learners need to understand who the customer is, who supplies the value, how money is earned and what costs must be covered. These decisions influence platform choice, marketing, customer service, inventory, payment methods and growth. Employers also expect e-commerce staff to understand the commercial logic behind the activities they perform.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
**Business model** – The overall logic of how a business creates value for customers and earns enough income to sustain its operations.

**B2C (Business-to-Consumer)** – A model in which a business sells directly to individual consumers.

**B2B (Business-to-Business)** – A model in which one business sells products or services to another business.

**C2C (Consumer-to-Consumer)** – A model in which individuals sell to other individuals, often through a marketplace platform.

**D2C (Direct-to-Consumer)** – A model in which a producer or brand sells directly to end customers instead of relying mainly on traditional intermediaries.

**Revenue model** – The method a business uses to generate income, such as product sales, subscriptions, commissions or service fees.

**Subscription** – A recurring payment arrangement in which customers pay at regular intervals for continued access to a product or service.

**Commission** – A percentage or fixed amount earned for facilitating a transaction or sale.

## Main Lesson Content
A business model explains how an organisation creates value, serves a particular customer and earns enough money to continue operating. In e-commerce, the model affects nearly every practical decision. A business selling handmade products directly to consumers needs a different operation from a wholesale supplier selling cartons of stock to retailers. A marketplace that connects buyers and sellers has different responsibilities from a business that owns its own inventory.

One of the most common models is B2C, or business-to-consumer. In B2C e-commerce, a business sells directly to individual customers. Examples include clothing stores, electronics stores, online grocery services and many service businesses. B2C businesses usually need clear product information, convenient checkout, reliable customer communication and strong consumer trust. They may process many relatively small orders, so efficiency is important.

B2B, or business-to-business, involves one business selling to another. An office-supply distributor, packaging supplier or software provider may operate this way. B2B orders can be larger and purchasing decisions may take longer. Customers may require quotations, tax invoices, account terms, bulk pricing, approval processes or repeat ordering. The online store or portal therefore needs to support the way business customers actually buy.

C2C, or consumer-to-consumer, usually occurs through a platform that allows individuals to list and sell products to one another. The platform may provide search, messaging, ratings and payment tools. The platform itself may earn fees or commissions rather than owning the products. Trust and dispute handling are especially important because the platform is connecting parties who may not know one another.

D2C, or direct-to-consumer, describes a producer or brand selling directly to the final customer. For example, a skincare manufacturer may sell through its own website rather than relying only on pharmacies or supermarkets. This can give the brand more control over customer data, pricing, presentation and relationships. However, the brand also takes responsibility for functions that intermediaries may previously have handled, including fulfilment, returns, customer support and customer acquisition.

The business model explains the structure of the relationship, while the revenue model explains how money is earned. The simplest revenue model is direct product sales: the business buys or makes a product, adds a margin and sells it. Service businesses may charge once-off fees. Subscription businesses charge recurring amounts for continuing access. Marketplaces may charge sellers a listing fee, transaction fee or commission. Some businesses combine several revenue streams, such as selling products while also charging for premium membership or add-on services.

A revenue model must be evaluated against costs. Revenue is not the same as profit. A store may generate strong sales but still lose money if product costs, delivery subsidies, advertising, payment fees, returns and overheads consume too much of the income. This is why choosing a model based only on popularity can be dangerous. The commercial question is whether the business can deliver value consistently while covering its costs and generating an acceptable return.

The model also affects risk. A retailer that holds inventory has money tied up in stock and may face unsold products. A dropshipping business may hold less stock but has less control over supplier reliability and delivery. A subscription business can benefit from predictable recurring revenue, but customers expect ongoing value and may cancel if service quality declines. A marketplace can scale without owning all products, but it must manage platform trust and seller quality.

When selecting a model, a learner should ask practical questions: Who is the customer? What problem is being solved? Who owns or produces the product? Who controls pricing? Who handles delivery? How is income earned? What recurring costs exist? What happens if demand is lower than expected? A clear model helps the business make informed decisions before spending heavily on technology or marketing.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
Imagine three businesses selling coffee. Business A buys packaged coffee and sells it through its own website to individual customers. This is mainly B2C with a direct-sales revenue model. Business B supplies cases of coffee to restaurants and offices. This is B2B and may use bulk pricing and account terms. Business C connects independent coffee roasters with customers and earns 10% commission on each sale. This is a marketplace model with commission revenue.

The product category is similar, but the operational design, customer relationship and method of earning income are different.

## Practical Activity
Choose one online business idea. Write down: the target customer, the business model, the revenue model, the main product or service cost, two operating costs, and one major commercial risk. Then explain in three to five sentences why the chosen model is appropriate.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is the difference between a business model and a revenue model?
2. Which model describes a business selling directly to individual consumers?
3. Why can D2C give a brand more control?
4. Give two examples of revenue models other than direct product sales.
5. Why is high revenue not automatically evidence of a profitable business?

## Feedback / Explanation for Knowledge Check
1. The business model explains how value is created and delivered; the revenue model explains how income is generated.
2. B2C.
3. The brand can control presentation, customer relationships, pricing and customer data more directly, although it also takes on more operational responsibility.
4. Examples include subscription fees, commissions, listing fees, service fees or memberships.
5. Revenue must still cover product costs, marketing, payment costs, delivery, returns and other operating expenses before profit is produced.

## Key Takeaways
- Different e-commerce models involve different customer relationships and operational responsibilities.
- B2C, B2B, C2C and D2C are common but not identical approaches.
- A revenue model explains how the business gets paid.
- Revenue should always be considered together with costs and risk.
- A viable model should match the customer, value proposition, operations and economics of the business.
