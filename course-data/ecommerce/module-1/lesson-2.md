# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 1: E-commerce Fundamentals & Business Readiness
### Lesson 2: Online Customers, Buying Behaviour & the Customer Journey

**Estimated study time:** 35–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain how online buying behaviour differs from some face-to-face purchasing situations.
2. Map the main stages of an e-commerce customer journey.
3. Identify friction, uncertainty and trust factors that influence conversion.
4. Distinguish customer needs from assumptions made by the seller.
5. Recommend improvements to a basic online buying journey.

## Why This Lesson Matters
An online store is designed around customer decisions. Customers cannot normally touch the product, speak immediately to a salesperson or inspect the premises before paying. They depend on information, images, policies, reviews, prices, delivery promises and digital interfaces. Understanding the customer journey helps a business remove unnecessary obstacles without manipulating the customer.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Customer journey** — The sequence of interactions a customer has with a business before, during and after a purchase.
- **Touchpoint** — A point where the customer encounters or interacts with the business.
- **Conversion** — Completion of a defined desired action, such as a purchase.
- **Friction** — Unnecessary difficulty or effort that makes a customer task harder.
- **Trust** — Confidence that the store, offer and transaction are credible and likely to meet reasonable expectations.
- **Intent** — The customer's underlying purpose or reason for visiting.
- **Abandonment** — Leaving a process such as a cart or checkout before completing it.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Start with customer intent
Not every visitor arrives for the same reason. One customer may know exactly which product they want. Another may compare prices. Another may need education before choosing. Store structure and content should help these different forms of intent rather than force everyone through the same discovery path.

Useful customer understanding can come from enquiries, search terms, returns, analytics, interviews, support tickets and observation of common problems. Assumptions should be tested where possible.

### 2. A typical e-commerce journey
A simplified journey may include:
1. Awareness — the customer discovers the store or product.
2. Consideration — the customer compares options and evaluates suitability.
3. Selection — the customer chooses a product, variant or service.
4. Cart — the customer reviews intended purchases.
5. Checkout — customer, payment and delivery details are confirmed.
6. Confirmation — the store acknowledges the transaction correctly.
7. Fulfilment — the order is prepared and delivered or the service is provided.
8. Post-purchase — support, returns, reviews or repeat purchases may follow.

The journey is not always linear. Customers may leave and return, research on a phone and purchase on a laptop, or compare several stores before deciding.

### 3. Reduce uncertainty
Online customers often need reassurance about product suitability, price, stock, delivery, returns and business legitimacy. Clear product specifications, useful images, transparent charges and realistic delivery information reduce uncertainty.

Removing information to create pressure may increase short-term clicks but can damage trust. The objective is informed purchasing, not confusion.

### 4. Recognise friction
Friction can include:
- confusing navigation;
- slow or broken pages;
- poor mobile usability;
- hidden delivery costs;
- unnecessary form fields;
- unclear error messages;
- unavailable payment methods;
- forced account creation when it is not genuinely required;
- contradictory policy information.

Some steps are necessary for security, legal or operational reasons. The goal is not zero effort; it is appropriate effort.

### 5. Trust across the journey
Trust can be strengthened by consistent business identity, accurate product information, secure payment processes, visible contact routes, credible reviews, understandable policies and reliable communication. Trust can be damaged by fake countdown timers, unsupported claims, inconsistent contact information or promises the business cannot keep.

### 6. Mobile customer behaviour
Many customers discover and purchase through phones. Small screens make hierarchy, button size, form design and readable content especially important. A desktop-only journey can exclude or frustrate a large portion of potential customers.

### 7. Post-purchase experience matters
The transaction does not end at payment. Customers judge the store by confirmation, delivery updates, packaging, order accuracy and support. A difficult return or unanswered complaint can outweigh an attractive checkout experience.

### 8. Measure journey quality
Useful indicators can include product-page engagement, search success, add-to-cart rate, checkout completion, abandonment, repeat purchase and support contacts. Numbers should be interpreted with context. A high abandonment rate may reflect unexpected delivery charges, technical errors or customers using the cart for comparison.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A customer finds a pair of shoes through social media, opens the product page on a phone and sees good images but no size guide. The customer adds a pair to the cart, then discovers an unexpected delivery charge at the final checkout step and leaves. The business should not simply conclude that the customer “was not serious.” The journey contains two avoidable uncertainties: product fit and late delivery-cost disclosure.

## Practical Activity
Map an online purchase from awareness to post-purchase for a fictional store. At each stage, write one customer question, one possible friction point and one improvement the store could make.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is a customer touchpoint?
2. Why is a customer journey not always linear?
3. Give three examples of checkout friction.
4. Why are clear returns and delivery information trust factors?
5. Why should abandonment data be interpreted rather than judged in isolation?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. A point where the customer encounters or interacts with the business.
2. Customers may research, leave, return and switch devices or channels.
3. Examples include hidden charges, unnecessary fields and confusing errors.
4. They reduce uncertainty about important transaction outcomes.
5. Different causes can produce the same metric, so context is needed.

## Key Takeaways
- E-commerce design should support real customer tasks and questions.
- The customer journey continues after payment.
- Friction and uncertainty can prevent otherwise valid purchases.
- Trust depends on truthful information and reliable operations.
- Mobile behaviour and post-purchase service must be included in journey design.
