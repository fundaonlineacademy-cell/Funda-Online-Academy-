# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 1: E-commerce Fundamentals & Business Readiness
### Lesson 7: Security, Fraud & Privacy Foundations for Online Stores

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Identify common security and fraud risks in e-commerce.
2. Explain why account, payment and customer-data security are connected.
3. Describe basic protective controls such as unique passwords, MFA and least privilege.
4. Recognise common phishing and social-engineering indicators.
5. Explain why suspicious events should be documented and escalated.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Online stores handle accounts, payments, customer information and operational access. Criminals may target the website, staff, payment process or customers. Security is therefore part of everyday e-commerce management rather than a specialist topic that can be ignored until an incident occurs. This lesson introduces the principles developed more deeply in Module 7.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Cybersecurity** — Practices used to protect systems, accounts, information and services from digital threats.
- **Phishing** — Deceptive communication intended to trick a person into revealing information or taking an unsafe action.
- **Social engineering** — Manipulating people rather than only attacking technology.
- **MFA** — Multi-factor authentication, requiring more than one factor to verify access.
- **Least privilege** — Limiting access to what a user genuinely requires.
- **Fraud indicator** — A signal that may justify additional review but does not necessarily prove fraud.
- **Incident** — An event that threatens or compromises security, privacy or normal operations.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Security protects the business model
An attacker who gains administrator access may change prices, payout details, customer information or store settings. A compromised email account can be used to reset other passwords. A fraudulent order can create inventory and financial loss. Security controls should therefore protect the full operating environment, not only the public website.

### 2. Protect accounts
Important business accounts should use unique credentials and strong authentication. MFA can reduce the risk that a stolen password alone is enough to gain access. Shared administrator passwords should be avoided because they weaken accountability and make offboarding harder.

### 3. Limit permissions
Not every worker needs access to every system. A warehouse worker may require fulfilment data but not payment configuration. A marketer may require campaign tools but not refund permissions. Least privilege reduces the impact of mistakes and compromised accounts.

### 4. Recognise phishing
Phishing can arrive through email, SMS, messaging apps or fake login pages. Warning signs include unexpected urgency, requests for passwords or codes, suspicious domains, unusual attachments and messages asking staff to bypass normal procedures.

A professional response is to verify through an independent trusted channel rather than clicking first and investigating later.

### 5. Understand fraud indicators
Unusual order value, repeated failed payments, mismatched details, rapid account changes or unexpected redirect requests can justify review. One signal does not prove fraud. Businesses should use consistent risk procedures to avoid both financial loss and unfair rejection of legitimate customers.

### 6. Protect customer information
Names, contact details, addresses and transaction history should be available only where operationally justified. Staff should avoid discussing private order information publicly or copying customer data into uncontrolled systems.

### 7. Use approved payment processes
Customers should be directed through approved payment methods. Staff should not ask customers to send full card credentials through ordinary chat or email. Payment confirmation should come from the business's authoritative system or provider process.

### 8. Update and back up systems
Outdated software, unused integrations and weak third-party applications can create risk. Businesses should apply supported updates, remove unnecessary access and maintain appropriate backups for information required to restore operations.

### 9. Report incidents quickly
A suspicious MFA prompt, unknown administrator, unexpected payout change or customer-data disclosure should not be ignored. Early reporting can reduce harm. Staff should preserve useful evidence and follow the approved incident process rather than deleting records to hide the problem.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A staff member receives an urgent email claiming to be from the payment provider and asking them to log in through a link to “prevent account suspension.” Instead of entering credentials, the employee opens the provider's known website independently and checks the account there. The suspicious message is reported. The control is not advanced technology; it is disciplined verification.

## Practical Activity
Create a basic security checklist for an imaginary online store covering administrator accounts, MFA, staff permissions, payment handling, customer data, software updates, backups and incident reporting. Add one owner for every control.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why is a compromised email account a risk to other business systems?
2. What does least privilege mean?
3. Give three phishing warning signs.
4. Does one fraud indicator automatically prove fraud?
5. What should staff do after discovering a suspicious access event?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Email is often used for password reset and account recovery.
2. Give users only the access required for authorised work.
3. Examples include urgency, suspicious links, credential requests and unexpected attachments.
4. No. Indicators require context and review.
5. Follow the approved incident process, preserve evidence and escalate promptly.

## Key Takeaways
- E-commerce security covers people, accounts, payments, systems and data.
- Unique credentials, MFA and least privilege reduce account risk.
- Phishing often targets human judgement.
- Fraud signals require evidence-based review.
- Customer and payment information should be handled through controlled processes.
- Security incidents should be reported and documented quickly.
