# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 1: E-commerce Fundamentals & Business Readiness
### Lesson 4: Revenue, Costs, Profitability & Basic E-commerce Metrics

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Distinguish revenue, cost, gross profit and operating expenses at a basic level.
2. Identify important e-commerce costs beyond product purchase cost.
3. Calculate simple average order value and conversion rate examples.
4. Explain why sales growth does not automatically mean profitability.
5. Use basic metrics to ask better business questions.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Online stores can generate many orders and still lose money. Product cost is only one part of the economics. Payment fees, packaging, delivery subsidies, advertising, returns, platform charges and staff time can materially change the result. Learners need a basic commercial foundation before studying detailed pricing and marketing later in the course.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Revenue** — Income generated from sales before deducting costs.
- **Cost of goods sold (COGS)** — The direct cost associated with goods sold, depending on the business's accounting approach.
- **Gross profit** — Sales revenue minus the direct cost of goods sold in a simplified gross-profit calculation.
- **Operating expense** — A cost of running the business that is not necessarily part of the direct product cost.
- **Average order value (AOV)** — Total order revenue divided by the number of orders for a defined period.
- **Conversion rate** — The percentage of a defined visitor or session population that completes a target action, according to the measurement method used.
- **Customer acquisition cost (CAC)** — A measure of the cost associated with acquiring customers, calculated under a defined method.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Revenue is not profit
If a store sells 100 items at R500 each, revenue is R50,000. That does not mean the business earned R50,000. Product costs and operating expenses must still be considered.

A simple gross-profit example is:
`Gross Profit = Sales Revenue - Cost of Goods Sold`

If R50,000 of sales relates to goods that cost R30,000, simplified gross profit is R20,000 before other operating expenses.

### 2. Understand the broader cost structure
Common e-commerce costs may include:
- product or production cost;
- payment-processing charges;
- platform subscriptions and transaction fees;
- packaging;
- warehousing or storage;
- courier and delivery subsidies;
- returns and exchanges;
- advertising and content production;
- customer-service labour;
- software and integrations;
- fraud losses and chargebacks;
- professional or compliance costs.

Not every cost belongs in the same accounting category, but managers must understand that each can affect commercial viability.

### 3. Average order value
AOV is useful for understanding the average revenue represented by an order.

`AOV = Order Revenue ÷ Number of Orders`

If a store records R24,000 in order revenue from 40 orders, AOV is R600.

A higher AOV is not automatically better if discounts, free-delivery costs or returns make those orders unprofitable.

### 4. Conversion rate
A simplified e-commerce conversion rate can be expressed as:

`Conversion Rate = Conversions ÷ Relevant Visits or Sessions × 100`

If 1,000 measured sessions produce 25 purchases, the simplified purchase conversion rate is 2.5%.

The measurement basis matters. Businesses should use consistent analytics definitions rather than compare unrelated numbers.

### 5. Acquisition cost and marketing economics
Advertising can increase traffic and sales, but a campaign should be evaluated against commercial results. If R5,000 in campaign cost produces ten new customers under the business's chosen measurement method, a simplified acquisition cost is R500 per acquired customer.

Whether R500 is acceptable depends on order value, margin, repeat purchase and other costs. A metric is useful only when interpreted within the business model.

### 6. Returns affect economics
Returns can create reverse-delivery, inspection, support and stock-handling costs. A product category with high sales but very high return rates may need better sizing information, quality control or merchandising rather than more advertising.

### 7. Avoid vanity metrics
Website visits, social-media followers and impressions can be useful, but they do not directly prove profitable growth. A business should connect attention to meaningful outcomes such as qualified traffic, sales, customer retention and contribution.

### 8. Use metrics as questions, not automatic answers
If conversion falls, investigate product relevance, pricing, technical issues, traffic quality, mobile usability or delivery conditions. If AOV rises, check whether margin and order profitability also improved. Metrics direct attention; they do not replace analysis.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A store receives R12,000 from 20 orders, so AOV is R600. Product cost for those orders is R6,800. Payment, packaging and delivery subsidies total R1,500, while advertising for the period costs R2,000. Looking only at R12,000 revenue would exaggerate performance. The business needs the full cost picture before deciding whether the campaign was successful.

## Practical Activity
Create a one-week fictional store report with revenue, number of orders, product cost, payment fees, packaging, delivery subsidy, advertising and returns cost. Calculate AOV and a simplified gross-profit figure, then write three questions management should investigate before making a decision.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why is revenue different from profit?
2. Give four e-commerce costs other than product cost.
3. What is AOV if R18,000 revenue comes from 30 orders?
4. What is the simplified conversion rate if 20 purchases result from 800 measured sessions?
5. Why can a high-traffic campaign still be commercially poor?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Revenue is sales income before costs are deducted.
2. Examples include payment fees, packaging, delivery, advertising, platform costs and returns.
3. R600.
4. 2.5%.
5. Traffic may not convert, or the cost of acquiring and serving customers may exceed the value created.

## Key Takeaways
- Sales volume alone does not prove profitability.
- E-commerce has transaction, fulfilment, marketing and service costs.
- AOV and conversion rate are useful when consistently defined.
- Returns and acquisition costs can materially change performance.
- Metrics should guide investigation rather than replace judgement.
