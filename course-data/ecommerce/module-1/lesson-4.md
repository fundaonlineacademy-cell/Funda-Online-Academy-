# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 1: E-commerce Foundations & Digital Business Models
### Lesson 4: Choosing Products, Services & Niches

**Estimated study time:** 35–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- Explain what a market niche is and why focus can help an online business.
- Evaluate products or services using demand, competition, margin, operational complexity and risk.
- Distinguish between a promising product idea and an untested assumption.
- Identify common product-selection risks such as fragility, returns, seasonality and supplier dependence.
- Apply a simple product-selection scorecard to an e-commerce idea.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Many new online sellers start by choosing products they personally like. Personal interest can be useful, but commercial success requires more than enthusiasm. The seller must consider customer demand, competition, pricing, sourcing, delivery, returns, legal or safety implications and whether enough margin remains after costs. A disciplined selection process reduces avoidable losses and helps the business choose offers it can actually support.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
**Niche** – A focused part of a broader market serving customers with particular needs, characteristics or interests.

**Demand** – The level of customer willingness and ability to buy a product or service at a given time and price.

**Margin** – The amount or percentage remaining from sales revenue after specified costs are deducted; the exact calculation used should be stated clearly.

**Seasonality** – Predictable changes in demand associated with seasons, holidays, events or periods of the year.

**Supplier dependence** – The operational risk created when a business relies heavily on one supplier or source of stock.

**Return risk** – The likelihood that customers will return a product because of fit, expectations, defects, damage or other reasons.

**Scalability** – The ability of a business operation to handle increased sales without costs, complexity or failure increasing at the same rate.

**Validation** – The process of testing assumptions with evidence before committing substantial time or money.

## Main Lesson Content
Choosing what to sell is one of the most important decisions in e-commerce. A product can look attractive in photographs and still be difficult to operate profitably. The right question is not simply “Will people like this?” but “Can we serve a defined customer with an offer that has sufficient demand, acceptable competition, workable economics and manageable operational risk?”

A niche is a focused section of a larger market. For example, “clothing” is very broad, while “durable workwear for women in technical trades” is more specific. A narrower niche can help a new business communicate clearly, understand customer needs and use marketing resources more efficiently. However, a niche can be too small, so the business should still investigate whether enough potential customers exist.

Demand is the first major consideration. Interest can be observed through customer enquiries, marketplace activity, competitor sales indicators, search behaviour, social-media discussions, local availability gaps and direct interviews. None of these signals alone proves that a product will sell profitably, but several consistent signals can reduce uncertainty. Businesses should be cautious about mistaking social-media attention for genuine purchase intent. People may like, share or comment on a product without being willing to pay the required price.

Competition should also be evaluated carefully. Competition is not automatically negative. Existing competitors can confirm that customers already spend money in the category. The more useful questions are whether the market is overcrowded, whether competitors are strongly established, what customers complain about, and whether the new business can offer a meaningful difference. Competing only on the lowest price can be dangerous because larger sellers may have better purchasing power and lower operating costs.

The economics of the product matter. A seller should understand purchase or manufacturing cost, packaging, payment fees, delivery contribution, advertising, storage, returns and expected losses before deciding whether the selling price leaves enough room for a sustainable margin. Margin should never be discussed without clarifying the calculation. Gross margin, for example, is usually based on sales revenue less cost of goods sold; it does not mean all operating expenses have already been paid.

Operational complexity is another important factor. Fragile products require protective packaging and may be damaged in transit. Heavy or bulky goods may be expensive to deliver. Products with many sizes or colours create more inventory combinations. Fashion and footwear may face high return rates because of fit. Perishable products require careful storage and delivery timing. Products that are regulated or safety-sensitive may require specialised knowledge and compliance. A beginner should not ignore these issues simply because demand appears high.

Supplier reliability can determine whether the business keeps its promises. A seller should consider lead times, minimum order quantities, product consistency, communication, replacement procedures and the risk of depending on one source. If the supplier repeatedly runs out of stock, changes specifications or delivers late, the seller faces the customer consequences. Diversifying supply may reduce risk, but alternative suppliers should also be evaluated for quality and consistency.

Seasonality affects cash flow and inventory planning. Some products sell mainly around holidays, school terms, weather conditions or events. Seasonal products can be profitable, but the business needs a plan for stock that remains after the peak period. A seller should also distinguish temporary trends from durable demand. Trend products can grow quickly and fall quickly, leaving unsold stock.

Services and digital products require similar discipline even though they may not involve physical inventory. A service may depend on the seller’s available time and therefore have limited capacity. A digital product can be scalable but may face piracy, support demands or intense competition. Subscription services require ongoing value so customers continue paying.

A simple scorecard can support better decisions. The seller can rate each idea on customer demand, competition, margin potential, operational complexity, return risk, supplier reliability, differentiation and scalability. The scores do not replace judgement, but they force the seller to compare ideas against the same criteria rather than choosing emotionally.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A seller is considering glass drink bottles and fabric tote bags. The glass bottles may have good demand and attractive margins, but they are fragile and require protective packaging. Tote bags may be easier and cheaper to ship, but the market could be crowded and the seller may struggle to differentiate. The best choice depends on evidence, not on which product looks more attractive.

A simple scorecard might rate each option from 1 to 5 for demand, margin, shipping ease, return risk, supplier reliability and differentiation. The seller can then investigate weak areas before deciding.

## Practical Activity
Choose three possible products or services for an online business. Score each from 1 to 5 on: demand evidence, competition position, margin potential, delivery or service complexity, return/cancellation risk, supplier or capacity reliability, differentiation and scalability. Write a short explanation for the highest-scoring option and identify one assumption that still needs validation.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is a niche?
2. Why does social-media attention not automatically prove demand?
3. Name three operational factors that can make a physical product difficult to sell online.
4. Why is supplier reliability important to the customer experience?
5. What is the purpose of a product-selection scorecard?

## Feedback / Explanation for Knowledge Check
1. A niche is a focused part of a wider market serving customers with more specific needs or characteristics.
2. Engagement does not prove that customers are willing and able to buy at the required price.
3. Examples include fragility, weight, size, perishability, many variants, return risk or complex compliance requirements.
4. Supplier delays, stock-outs or quality problems can prevent the seller from fulfilling customer promises.
5. A scorecard helps compare ideas consistently against commercial and operational criteria and exposes assumptions needing further research.

## Key Takeaways
- Product selection should be evidence-based rather than purely personal.
- A useful niche is focused but still large enough to support viable demand.
- Demand, competition and margin must be considered together with operational risk.
- Suppliers, delivery, returns and seasonality can materially affect profitability.
- Validation should happen before major investment whenever possible.
