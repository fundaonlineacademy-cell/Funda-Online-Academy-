# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 1: E-commerce Fundamentals & Business Readiness
### Lesson 3: Products, Services & Digital Commerce Offers

**Estimated study time:** 35–40 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Distinguish physical products, services and digital products in e-commerce.
2. Explain how fulfilment differs across offer types.
3. Identify product-selection factors that affect online suitability.
4. Recognise the importance of accurate product and service information.
5. Evaluate whether an offer is practical to sell online.

## Why This Lesson Matters
The type of offer determines much of the store's operation. A physical product requires stock, packaging and delivery. A digital product may require controlled access or download delivery. A service may depend on scheduling, availability and clear scope. Businesses should design the online journey around what is actually being sold rather than forcing every offer into the same process.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Physical product** — A tangible item that must normally be stored, handled or delivered.
- **Digital product** — A non-physical product supplied electronically, such as approved downloadable content or software access.
- **Service** — Work or value provided to a customer rather than a physical item alone.
- **Fulfilment** — The process of delivering the purchased product or service.
- **Catalogue** — The organised set of products or services offered for sale.
- **Variant** — A sellable version of a product, such as a specific size or colour.
- **Availability** — Whether an offer can currently be supplied under the stated conditions.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Physical products
Physical goods require reliable product data and operational handling. Weight, dimensions, fragility, storage conditions and variant structure can affect delivery and fulfilment. A seller of glassware has different packaging requirements from a seller of clothing.

### 2. Services
Services often require the customer to understand scope, timing, deliverables and any booking conditions. Capacity replaces physical stock as an important constraint. If a consultant can serve only five clients per week, the online system should not create unlimited confirmed bookings.

### 3. Digital products
Digital products can be delivered quickly but still require clear licensing, access, customer support and security. The business should define whether access is permanent, time-limited, account-based or downloadable and communicate those conditions before purchase.

### 4. Product suitability for online selling
A product may be technically sellable online but commercially difficult. Important questions include:
- Can it be described accurately?
- Can it be packaged and delivered economically?
- Is the likely selling price sufficient to support transaction and fulfilment costs?
- Are there restrictions or specialist handling requirements?
- Is customer demand sufficient?
- Can returns or service failures be managed?

### 5. Use accurate information
Customers make decisions without physically inspecting the offer. Material characteristics should therefore be described truthfully. Size, colour, compatibility, included accessories, service scope and delivery conditions should not be guessed.

### 6. Understand variants
One product can contain many separately managed sellable variants. A shirt in three colours and four sizes may create twelve combinations if every colour-size combination is stocked. Variant complexity affects stock accuracy, product selection and fulfilment.

### 7. Availability must reflect reality
A product displayed as available should normally be capable of being supplied. Overselling can occur when stock is not updated across channels. Services can also be oversold if capacity is not controlled. Availability is therefore both a customer-facing promise and an operational record.

### 8. Build the right offer, not just a large catalogue
A smaller, well-managed catalogue can be stronger than hundreds of poorly maintained listings. The business should prioritise products it can describe, price, stock, fulfil and support reliably.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A business considers selling custom cakes online. The offer is physical but also service-like because production capacity, customisation and collection/delivery dates matter. The store should not use the same workflow as stocked stationery. It needs order lead times, customisation information, capacity control and clear delivery or collection arrangements.

## Practical Activity
Choose one physical product, one digital product and one service. For each, identify the information customers need, the fulfilment method, the main availability constraint and one likely customer-service risk.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why does a service need capacity control even when it has no physical stock?
2. What is a product variant?
3. Give two factors that affect whether a physical product is commercially suitable for online selling.
4. Why is availability a customer promise as well as an internal record?
5. Why can a smaller catalogue sometimes be stronger than a large one?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Service providers have limited time or resources even without physical inventory.
2. A separately selectable sellable version such as a size or colour.
3. Examples include delivery cost, fragility, demand, price and return complexity.
4. Customers rely on the store's availability information when deciding to buy.
5. It can be maintained and fulfilled more accurately.

## Key Takeaways
- Different offer types require different fulfilment designs.
- Accurate information is essential when customers cannot inspect the offer physically.
- Variants increase catalogue and inventory complexity.
- Availability must match operational reality.
- Online product selection should consider both demand and the cost of serving the customer.
