# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 1: E-commerce Foundations & Digital Business Models
### Lesson 6: Digital Customer Journeys & Buying Behaviour

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. map major stages of a digital customer journey;
2. identify common customer questions and barriers at each stage;
3. explain how trust, convenience and risk influence online buying;
4. distinguish useful journey metrics from assumptions; and
5. recommend practical improvements to an online buying journey.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Online customers cannot normally touch a product, speak face-to-face with a salesperson or immediately inspect a business premises. They depend on information, design, reviews, policies, payment processes and communication to judge whether buying is worthwhile and safe. Understanding the journey helps an e-commerce operator remove unnecessary friction without manipulating customers.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Customer journey** — the sequence of interactions a customer experiences before, during and after a purchase.
- **Touchpoint** — a specific interaction between a customer and the business, such as an advert, product page, email or delivery message.
- **Conversion** — completion of a defined desired action, such as a purchase, registration or enquiry.
- **Friction** — unnecessary difficulty, uncertainty or effort that makes progress through a journey harder.
- **Cart abandonment** — when a shopper adds item(s) to a cart but leaves before completing the purchase.
- **Trust signal** — credible information or evidence that reduces uncertainty about a seller or transaction.
- **Post-purchase experience** — interactions after ordering, including confirmation, fulfilment, delivery, support and follow-up.

## Main Lesson Content
A customer journey is broader than the checkout screen. It can begin when a person first recognises a need, sees a recommendation, searches online or encounters a social-media post. It continues through evaluation, purchase, fulfilment, use, support and possible repeat purchase. Different customers may enter at different stages, so a journey map is a model rather than a rigid path.

A useful high-level journey includes awareness, consideration, decision, purchase, fulfilment, post-purchase use and retention or advocacy. At awareness, the customer asks whether the offer is relevant. During consideration, they compare alternatives and seek evidence. At decision, they evaluate price, risk, delivery, returns and trust. During checkout they need a clear, reliable transaction. After purchase they expect confirmation and progress information. Later, service quality influences whether they return or recommend the business.

Each stage contains touchpoints. Awareness touchpoints may include search results, social posts, advertisements or referrals. Consideration touchpoints include category pages, product pages, reviews and FAQs. Purchase touchpoints include the cart, checkout and payment provider. Fulfilment touchpoints include confirmation emails, tracking messages, packaging and delivery. Mapping these interactions helps the operator see the business from the customer's perspective.

Online buying behaviour is strongly affected by perceived risk. Customers may worry that a product will differ from its description, payment may be unsafe, delivery may be late, returns may be difficult or the seller may disappear after receiving money. A professional store reduces uncertainty with accurate descriptions, complete pricing, realistic delivery information, accessible contact details, appropriate security, clear policies and consistent communication.

Trust signals must be genuine. Examples include verifiable business/contact information, transparent terms, secure transaction processes, authentic reviews, clear returns information and professional customer support. Decorative badges that imply certifications the business does not hold are not legitimate trust signals. Trust is created by evidence and reliable behaviour.

Convenience also shapes behaviour. Customers may abandon a journey when pages are difficult to use on mobile devices, required information is unclear, checkout asks for unnecessary data, payment fails, delivery cost appears unexpectedly, or the customer cannot understand what happens next. Removing friction does not mean hiding important information. Good design makes important information easier to understand before commitment.

Price is only one component of perceived value. A customer may accept a higher price when the seller offers better availability, clearer information, trusted quality, faster fulfilment or stronger service. Conversely, an attractive headline price may lose its appeal if unavoidable charges appear late. South African electronic-commerce rules require significant supplier and transaction information to be made available to consumers, including full pricing information and relevant terms; legal requirements should be implemented using current authoritative guidance rather than treated as optional design choices.

Journey analysis should use evidence. Useful metrics include product-page views, add-to-cart rate, checkout starts, completed purchases, payment failures, support enquiries, returns and repeat purchases. A high exit rate at one stage is a clue, not automatically proof of the cause. Combine quantitative data with customer feedback, usability observation and operational records before deciding what to change.

Segmentation can reveal different journeys. A first-time buyer may need more reassurance than a returning customer. A mobile shopper may encounter different usability problems from a desktop user. A customer purchasing a high-value product may conduct more research. Journey improvements should therefore address meaningful customer needs rather than assume everyone behaves identically.

The post-purchase journey is especially important. Once money has been paid, uncertainty can increase. Immediate confirmation, realistic fulfilment updates, accurate tracking and accessible support reassure the customer. If something goes wrong, clear service recovery can protect the relationship. Repeat business is often influenced by whether the complete promise was delivered, not merely whether the checkout was attractive.

Ethical journey design respects customer choice. Interfaces should not deliberately confuse customers, conceal charges, create false urgency, preselect unwanted extras or make cancellation unreasonably difficult. A sustainable online store seeks conversion by making value and choices clear, not by tricking users into actions they did not intend.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A small clothing store receives many product views but relatively few completed purchases. The owner initially assumes prices are too high. A journey review shows customers frequently ask about sizing and only discover delivery charges at checkout. The owner improves the size guide, displays delivery information earlier and tests the mobile checkout. Conversion improves without reducing product prices. The lesson is that the observed problem must be investigated before choosing a solution.

## Practical Activity
Map a seven-stage journey for a customer buying one product online. For each stage record: customer goal, likely question, touchpoint, possible friction, trust requirement and one metric. Identify the three improvements you would test first and explain why.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why is the customer journey broader than checkout?
2. Give three examples of genuine trust signals.
3. What is friction in an e-commerce journey?
4. Why does a high exit rate not automatically reveal the cause of abandonment?
5. Give one example of unethical journey design.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. The relationship begins before purchase and continues through fulfilment, service and retention. 2. Examples include verifiable contact details, transparent policies, authentic reviews and secure payment processes. 3. Friction is unnecessary difficulty or uncertainty. 4. Analytics show behaviour but often require qualitative evidence to explain why it occurred. 5. Examples include hidden unavoidable charges, false urgency or preselected unwanted extras.

## Key Takeaways
- Map the entire customer experience, not only the transaction screen.
- Trust, convenience, information and perceived risk influence buying behaviour.
- Use evidence to diagnose journey problems before changing the store.
- Post-purchase communication is part of the customer experience.
- Ethical conversion improvement makes choices clearer rather than manipulating customers.
