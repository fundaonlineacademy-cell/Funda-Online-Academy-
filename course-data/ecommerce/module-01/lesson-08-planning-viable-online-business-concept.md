# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 1: E-commerce Foundations & Digital Business Models
### Lesson 8: Planning a Viable Online Business Concept

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. integrate customer, market, offer and operating assumptions into one business concept;
2. describe a practical e-commerce value proposition and revenue logic;
3. identify major cost, fulfilment, trust and compliance considerations;
4. assess important assumptions and risks before launch; and
5. present a concise evidence-based online business concept.

## Why This Lesson Matters
The previous lessons examined individual foundations: e-commerce models, customer needs, niches, competitors, validation, journeys and professional conduct. A viable concept connects these elements. This lesson gives the learner a structured way to decide whether an idea is coherent enough to move into store planning and setup.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Business concept** — a structured explanation of whom a business serves, what it offers, how it delivers value and how it expects to operate sustainably.
- **Value proposition** — the specific reason a target customer should consider the offer rather than relevant alternatives.
- **Revenue logic** — the mechanism through which the business expects to earn income.
- **Cost structure** — the important fixed and variable costs required to operate the model.
- **Fulfilment model** — the method used to prepare and deliver products or services after an order.
- **Key assumption** — an important belief that must be true for the business model to work.
- **Feasibility** — the practical ability to execute an idea within available resources, constraints and requirements.
- **Unit economics** — the revenue and directly associated costs considered at the level of a sale, customer or other useful unit.

## Main Lesson Content
A business concept is not simply a store name plus a list of products. It should explain a connected system. The customer has a need; the business makes an offer; the customer discovers and evaluates it; a transaction may occur; the business fulfils its promise; costs are incurred; revenue is earned; and the experience influences future demand. If one part of this chain is unrealistic, the overall concept may fail.

Begin with the target customer and problem or desire. Avoid descriptions so broad that they provide no direction. "Everyone who shops online" is not a useful starting segment. A stronger description identifies relevant characteristics or circumstances without making unsupported assumptions. For example, a store might initially serve small home-based bakers in a defined delivery area who need professional packaging in quantities smaller than wholesale suppliers commonly offer.

Next define the value proposition. State the practical value rather than relying on vague claims such as "best quality" or "number one." A value proposition might combine a carefully selected range, smaller pack sizes, clear specifications and predictable local delivery. Each element should respond to evidence from customer and competitor research.

Define the offer and revenue logic. Will the business sell physical goods, digital goods, services, subscriptions, marketplace access or a combination? Will revenue come mainly from individual sales, recurring fees, commissions or another legitimate mechanism? Revenue is not profit. The concept must also consider product costs, payment fees, packaging, delivery contributions, returns, marketing, software, labour and other operating costs.

Basic unit economics help test whether the model deserves further work. Suppose an item sells for R400. The product cost is R210, packaging is R15, payment cost is R12, and the seller contributes R45 toward delivery. The remaining R118 is not automatically profit because advertising, salaries, software, rent, losses, tax obligations and other overhead may still need to be covered. The purpose of this simple calculation is to prevent the common mistake of treating selling price minus supplier price as profit.

Fulfilment must be realistic. Where will stock come from? How reliable is the supplier? How much inventory is needed? Who picks and packs orders? What delivery areas can be served? What happens when stock is unavailable, an address is incorrect or an item is returned? Service businesses need equivalent planning around capacity, scheduling and delivery of the service.

Customer acquisition is another essential assumption. A store does not generate customers merely because it exists. The concept should identify plausible discovery channels such as search, social content, marketplaces, referrals, email or paid campaigns. Each channel has costs, skills and limitations. Later modules teach marketing in depth; at concept stage the goal is to show that acquisition has been considered rather than assumed.

Trust and compliance should be built into the concept. South African online sellers operate within legal frameworks including the Consumer Protection Act and the Electronic Communications and Transactions Act, with privacy and information-processing obligations also relevant under POPIA. ECTA includes requirements for information that suppliers offering goods or services through electronic transactions must make available, including supplier details, descriptions, full price information, payment manner, relevant terms, delivery timing, return/refund information, and security/privacy information. A concept should list key assumptions explicitly. Examples: customers will accept the proposed price; a supplier can maintain required quality; delivery can be achieved within the promised area; the acquisition channel can reach the target segment; return levels will remain manageable; and the business can meet applicable requirements. Rank assumptions by importance and uncertainty. High-impact, uncertain assumptions should be tested early.

Risk planning is not pessimism. It improves decision quality. Operational risks include supplier delays, inventory errors and courier problems. Commercial risks include weak demand and excessive acquisition cost. Financial risks include insufficient cash to replenish stock. Trust/compliance risks include misleading listings or poor information handling. For each major risk, identify prevention, detection and response measures appropriate to the size of the business.

Finally, decide what evidence is needed before proceeding. A concept can be classified as proceed to next-stage testing, revise and retest, or stop for now. This is more useful than declaring every idea "good." Entrepreneurship requires disciplined allocation of limited time and money.

A concise concept document should therefore include target customer, customer need, offer, value proposition, competitors/substitutes, validation evidence, revenue logic, major costs, fulfilment approach, acquisition channels, trust/compliance considerations, key risks, assumptions and next test. This becomes an input to Module 2, where the learner translates the business concept into store requirements and technical setup decisions.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
Lebo proposes an online store selling starter packaging kits to small food businesses in Cape Town. Interviews suggest that small operators struggle with wholesale minimum quantities. Competitor research identifies large suppliers and general retailers but few curated small packs. Lebo plans three starter kits, calculates preliminary unit economics, limits the initial delivery area, identifies two suppliers and tests demand with a simple catalogue and enquiry form. The concept remains conditional on supplier consistency and acceptable acquisition cost. This is more decision-ready than simply saying, "I want to sell packaging online."

## Practical Activity
Prepare a one-page concept for an online business. Include: target customer; need; offer; value proposition; business/revenue model; three competitors/substitutes; validation evidence; major costs; fulfilment model; acquisition channels; trust/compliance considerations; five key assumptions; five risks; and the next validation test. Finish with one decision: proceed, revise or stop for now, supported by evidence.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why is a product list alone not a complete business concept?
2. What is the difference between revenue and profit?
3. Why should high-impact uncertain assumptions be tested early?
4. Name four areas that should be considered in a fulfilment model.
5. What three possible decisions can follow a concept review?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. A concept must connect customer, value, operations, acquisition, economics and delivery. 2. Revenue is income earned; profit depends on revenue after relevant costs and obligations. 3. If they prove false, they can invalidate major parts of the model, so early testing reduces wasted resources. 4. Examples include sourcing, inventory, picking/packing, delivery, exceptions and returns. 5. Proceed to next-stage testing, revise and retest, or stop for now.

## Key Takeaways
- A viable concept connects customer need, offer, economics, operations and trust.
- Revenue must never be confused with profit.
- Fulfilment and customer acquisition are core parts of the model.
- Compliance and ethical practice should be designed in, not added after launch.
- Explicit assumptions and evidence support better go/no-go decisions.
