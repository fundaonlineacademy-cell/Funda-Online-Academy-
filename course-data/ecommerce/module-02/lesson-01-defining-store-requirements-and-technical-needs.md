# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 2: Online Store Planning, Platforms & Setup
### Lesson 1: Defining Store Requirements & Technical Needs

**Estimated study time:** 35–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- identify the business, customer and operational requirements of an online store;
- distinguish essential store functions from optional enhancements;
- translate business needs into clear technical requirements;
- recognise practical constraints such as budget, skills, integrations, security and scalability; and
- prepare a basic requirements checklist before selecting a platform.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Many online-store problems begin before a website is built. A business may choose software because it is popular, cheap or visually attractive without first deciding what the store must actually do. This can create expensive problems later: payment methods may not fit the target market, stock may not synchronise, delivery options may be difficult to manage, or the store may become too complex for the owner to maintain. Clear requirements help a business compare platforms logically, control costs, reduce rework and build a store that supports real customer and operational needs.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
**Requirement** — A specific capability, condition or outcome that the online store must satisfy.

**Functional requirement** — A task the system must perform, such as adding products to a basket, accepting payments or sending order confirmations.

**Non-functional requirement** — A quality or operating condition, such as speed, security, reliability, usability or mobile responsiveness.

**Integration** — A connection that allows two systems to exchange information, for example an online store connecting to a payment gateway, courier service or accounting system.

**Scalability** — The ability of a system to handle growth in products, customers, traffic or orders without major failure or costly rebuilding.

**User role** — A defined level of access or responsibility in a system, such as owner, administrator, fulfilment staff or customer-service user.

**Technical constraint** — A limitation that affects the solution, such as internet quality, available skills, device compatibility, budget or platform restrictions.

## Main Lesson Content
An online store is not only a collection of product pages. It is a working business system that must support customers, staff, payments, stock, delivery, communication, record keeping and security. Before choosing technology, the business should first define what the store must achieve.

A useful starting point is the business model. A store selling five handmade products directly to local customers has very different needs from a store managing thousands of stock-keeping units, multiple warehouses and national courier services. The first business may need a simple product catalogue, card and instant-electronic-payment options, basic shipping rules and an easy administration panel. The larger business may require bulk product imports, advanced inventory controls, warehouse integrations, multiple user roles, automated tax settings and more detailed reporting.

Requirements should be grouped into categories. The first category is **customer-facing requirements**. These include browsing, search, product information, mobile use, basket functions, checkout, payment choices, delivery selection, order confirmation and account management. The business must think from the customer's point of view. A feature is useful only if it supports a real need or removes a barrier from the buying process.

The second category is **operational requirements**. These concern what happens after the customer places an order. The business may need stock deduction, order-status changes, picking lists, packing information, courier labels, tracking numbers, returns management and internal notes. If the store does not support the way the business actually works, staff may create manual spreadsheets and duplicate processes, increasing the risk of errors.

The third category is **management and reporting requirements**. Decision-makers may need sales reports, product performance, stock alerts, refund records, customer data, promotional-code performance and marketing-source information. A small business does not necessarily need advanced analytics from the first day, but it should identify what information is essential for daily and monthly control.

The fourth category is **technical and quality requirements**. The store should work well on common smartphones and browsers, load reasonably quickly, use secure connections, protect accounts, support backups where applicable and allow authorised staff to manage the system safely. The business should also consider accessibility. Navigation, readable text, clear labels, meaningful headings and usable forms improve the experience for a wider range of customers.

Another important distinction is between **must-have**, **should-have** and **nice-to-have** requirements. A payment method needed by most target customers may be a must-have. A loyalty-points programme may be useful but not necessary for launch. An advanced product recommendation engine may be a future enhancement. Prioritising requirements prevents the project from becoming overloaded with expensive features that do not contribute to launch readiness.

Integrations require special attention because they often influence platform choice. The business should list any systems that need to connect to the store, such as payment gateways, couriers, accounting software, email marketing services, inventory tools or marketplace channels. The business should confirm whether the integration is native, provided by a trusted application, requires custom development, or is not supported at all. An integration that technically exists may still carry subscription fees, transaction fees or setup complexity.

A good requirements document also records constraints. Budget is one constraint, but it is not the only one. The business may have limited technical skills, weak internet connectivity, old staff devices, a small team or strict launch deadlines. These realities should influence the solution. A technically powerful platform can still be the wrong choice if staff cannot operate it reliably.

Finally, requirements should consider growth without assuming unrealistic scale. A new store should not pay for enterprise complexity that it does not need, but it should avoid choices that make reasonable growth impossible. The key question is whether the platform can support expected increases in products, orders, staff roles and integrations over the next stage of the business.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A small South African skincare business plans to sell 30 products online. Customers mainly use mobile phones. The owner needs card and instant EFT-type payments, national courier delivery, stock tracking, discount codes and automatic order emails. The owner has no developer and needs to update products without coding.

The requirements can therefore be written as follows: mobile-friendly storefront; 30–100 product capacity; simple product administration; secure checkout; suitable local payment integration; national delivery configuration; basic inventory tracking; discount-code support; automated confirmations; and owner-level reporting. Advanced multi-warehouse management and custom application development are marked as future needs rather than launch requirements.

## Practical Activity
Choose a hypothetical online business. Create a requirements sheet with five headings: customer functions, operations, payments and delivery, management/reporting, and technical quality. Under each heading, write at least three requirements. Mark each requirement as Must Have, Should Have or Nice to Have. Then identify two constraints that could affect the choice of platform.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is the difference between a functional and a non-functional requirement?
2. Why should integrations be identified before choosing a platform?
3. Give one example of a business constraint other than budget.
4. Why is it useful to classify features as must-have, should-have and nice-to-have?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. A functional requirement describes what the store must do; a non-functional requirement describes the quality or conditions under which it should operate.
2. Some platforms may not support the required payment, courier or accounting connections, or may support them only at extra cost.
3. Examples include staff skills, internet quality, device limitations, launch deadlines or available support.
4. Prioritisation helps control cost and complexity and ensures that essential launch functions are completed first.

## Key Takeaways
- Define the store's needs before selecting technology.
- Separate customer, operational, management and technical requirements.
- Prioritise essential functions instead of adding features simply because they are available.
- Confirm important integrations and limitations early.
- Select a solution that fits current operations while allowing realistic growth.
