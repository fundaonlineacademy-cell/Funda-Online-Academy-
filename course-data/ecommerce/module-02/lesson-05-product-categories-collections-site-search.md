# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 2: Online Store Planning, Platforms & Setup
### Lesson 5: Product Categories, Collections & Site Search

**Estimated Study Time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. Explain how categories, subcategories and collections organise an online product catalogue.
2. Design a customer-centred category structure for a small or medium online store.
3. Explain the role of filters, sorting, breadcrumbs and internal site search.
4. Identify common catalogue-navigation problems and propose practical improvements.
5. Test whether shoppers can find products efficiently using browsing and search.

## Why This Lesson Matters
An online store can have excellent products and still lose sales when shoppers cannot find them. Physical shops use aisles, departments, signs and staff assistance. Online stores use navigation menus, categories, collections, filters, search tools, breadcrumbs and product links. These elements form a digital wayfinding system. Good catalogue organisation reduces confusion, supports product discovery, improves the customer experience and can also make important catalogue pages easier for search engines to understand.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Category** — A logical group of related products, such as Shoes, Homeware or Skin Care, used to help customers browse a catalogue.
- **Subcategory** — A narrower group within a larger category, such as Running Shoes within Shoes.
- **Collection** — A curated grouping of products based on a shared purpose or rule, such as New Arrivals, Winter Essentials or Gifts Under R500.
- **Taxonomy** — The organised system used to classify and name products, categories and related catalogue information.
- **Filter** — A control that narrows displayed products according to attributes such as size, colour, price, brand or availability.
- **Sorting** — Reordering a product list according to criteria such as price, newest products or popularity.
- **Breadcrumb** — A navigation trail showing where the current page sits within the store hierarchy.
- **Site search** — The search function inside an online store that helps shoppers locate products or information using words or phrases.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Organise the catalogue around the customer
A store's internal records may organise stock according to suppliers, warehouse shelves or accounting codes. Customers usually think differently. A shopper may think “women's running shoes,” “black office chair” or “birthday gift under R500.” The public catalogue should therefore reflect understandable customer needs rather than merely copying the business's internal stock system.

A useful starting point is to list all products and ask what broad groups a new shopper would naturally expect. These become candidate top-level categories. Each category should have a clear purpose and a name customers can understand without explanation. Avoid vague labels such as “Solutions,” “Other” or internal abbreviations when a direct product term would be clearer.

### 2. Build a sensible hierarchy
A hierarchy moves from broad to specific. For example:

Home & Living → Kitchen → Cookware → Frying Pans

The hierarchy should be deep enough to organise products but not so complicated that shoppers must repeatedly open menus before seeing useful items. A small catalogue may need only a few broad categories. A large catalogue may require subcategories, filters and stronger search functionality. The correct structure depends on catalogue size, product diversity and customer behaviour.

Products may sometimes belong in more than one meaningful collection. A reusable bottle might appear under Drinkware and also under Back-to-School Essentials. This is useful when the groupings represent genuine customer shopping paths. However, uncontrolled duplication and overlapping category names can make maintenance difficult and confuse both customers and search systems.

### 3. Use collections for merchandising
Categories normally describe what a product is. Collections can describe why or when a customer may want it. Examples include New Arrivals, Best Sellers, Mother's Day Gifts, Clearance or Winter Essentials. Collections allow a store to merchandise products around campaigns and customer needs without rebuilding the permanent catalogue structure.

Collections should still be maintained carefully. An expired seasonal collection should not continue promising products that are no longer available. Automated collections can save time when rules are accurate, while manually curated collections give the merchant more control over presentation.

### 4. Filters and sorting support comparison
When a category contains many products, filters help shoppers reduce the list. Useful filters are based on attributes that genuinely influence a purchase decision. A clothing store may use size, colour and price. An electronics store may use capacity, brand and technical features. Filters should match the product type rather than being added simply because the platform supports them.

Sorting serves a different purpose. Instead of removing products, it changes their order. Options may include price low-to-high, price high-to-low, newest or relevance. The store should make the meaning of each sorting option clear.

Poor filtering creates frustration. A filter that says a product is available in “Large” when that variant is actually out of stock wastes the customer's time. Catalogue attributes and inventory information therefore need consistent maintenance.

### 5. Site search is a major navigation route
Some customers browse menus; others immediately type into the search box. Site search should therefore be treated as a core shopping function, particularly in stores with larger catalogues.

Good search depends on good product data. Product titles, categories, brands, attributes and useful keywords need to be accurate. If a customer searches for “navy backpack” but the catalogue records the colour only as “blue” and contains no useful descriptive data, the search engine may fail to return a relevant item.

Search results should help the shopper recover from imperfect queries. Depending on the platform, useful features may include suggestions, typo tolerance, synonyms and category suggestions. When no result is found, the store should avoid a dead end. It can offer alternative search terms, popular categories or a route to customer assistance.

### 6. Breadcrumbs and internal links create context
Breadcrumbs show the customer's location within the catalogue, for example Home → Shoes → Running Shoes. They help shoppers understand the hierarchy and move back to broader categories. Related-product links and links to complementary categories can also support discovery when used genuinely rather than excessively.

### 7. Test findability with real tasks
A store owner already knows where everything is, so personal familiarity can hide navigation problems. Testing should use realistic tasks such as: “Find a black backpack under R800,” “Find the returns policy,” or “Find a size 7 running shoe.” Observe how many steps the task takes, where confusion occurs and whether both menu browsing and search produce useful results.

The goal is not to force every customer through one route. The goal is to provide several clear routes that lead to the same accurate product information.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A small online store sells 120 fitness products. Its original menu contains “Products A–F,” “Products G–M” and “Products N–Z.” This mirrors an internal spreadsheet but does not help customers shop.

A customer-centred redesign could use:
- Training Clothing
- Footwear
- Fitness Equipment
- Recovery & Wellness
- Accessories

Within Fitness Equipment, subcategories could include Resistance Bands, Weights and Mats. Filters could include price and equipment type. A “Home Workout Essentials” collection could bring together products from several permanent categories for a campaign. The store should then test whether a shopper can find a yoga mat by browsing and by searching “yoga mat.”

## Practical Activity
Choose a hypothetical store with at least 20 products. Create:
1. Four to six top-level categories.
2. At least three useful subcategories.
3. Two merchandising collections.
4. Four useful filters for one category.
5. Three realistic customer search phrases.
6. A simple test task for checking whether another person can find a chosen product.

Explain why each category or filter would make sense to the customer.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is the main purpose of an e-commerce category?
2. How does a collection differ from a permanent product category?
3. What is the difference between filtering and sorting?
4. Why does accurate product data matter for site search?
5. Why should store navigation be tested by someone other than the person who built it?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. A category groups related products so shoppers can browse and understand the catalogue.
2. A collection is often a curated or rule-based grouping for a theme, campaign or shopping purpose, while a category normally represents a stable catalogue classification.
3. Filtering narrows the products shown; sorting changes the order in which products are shown.
4. Search relies on catalogue information to match a shopper's words to relevant products.
5. The builder already knows the structure and may overlook confusing labels or routes that are difficult for a new customer.

## Key Takeaways
- Design catalogue organisation around customer language and shopping needs.
- Use categories and subcategories for logical structure and collections for useful merchandising themes.
- Filters narrow choices; sorting changes order; search provides a direct route to products.
- Accurate catalogue data is essential for reliable navigation and search.
- Test product findability with realistic customer tasks before launch.
