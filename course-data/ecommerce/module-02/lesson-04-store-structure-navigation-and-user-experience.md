# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 2: Online Store Planning, Platforms & Setup
### Lesson 4: Store Structure, Navigation & User Experience

**Estimated study time:** 35–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- organise an online store into clear customer-facing sections;
- design simple navigation that helps customers find products and information;
- explain how information architecture affects usability and conversion;
- identify common navigation and usability problems; and
- evaluate a store structure from the customer's point of view.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Customers cannot buy products they cannot find or understand. A store may have excellent products and competitive prices but still lose sales if categories are confusing, menus are overloaded, important policies are hidden or the customer becomes uncertain about what to do next. Clear structure and navigation reduce effort, support trust and help customers move from discovery to purchase.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
**Information architecture** — The way content, pages and products are organised so that users can understand where information belongs and how to find it.

**Navigation** — Menus, links and controls that help users move through a website or application.

**Hierarchy** — The ordered relationship between broad sections and more specific pages or categories.

**Category** — A grouping of related products or content that helps customers browse logically.

**Breadcrumb** — A navigation trail showing the user's location within the site structure, for example Home > Clothing > Shoes.

**Call to action (CTA)** — A clear instruction or control encouraging the user to take a next step, such as Add to Cart or Proceed to Checkout.

**User experience (UX)** — The overall experience a person has while interacting with a product, service or digital system.

**Friction** — Unnecessary difficulty, confusion or effort that makes it harder for the customer to complete a task.

## Main Lesson Content
Store structure describes how the main pages, categories and functions of an online store fit together. Good structure is usually simple enough for customers to understand without training. The customer should be able to answer three questions quickly: Where am I? What can I do here? How do I get to what I need?

A typical online store includes a home page, product categories or collections, product-detail pages, basket, checkout, contact information, customer-service information and relevant store policies. Depending on the business, it may also include an About page, frequently asked questions, size guides, account area, blog or educational content. These pages should not simply exist; they should be placed where customers can reasonably find them.

The most important design principle is to organise the store according to the customer's mental model rather than the internal structure of the business. A retailer may internally divide products according to suppliers, warehouse zones or accounting codes, but customers may think in terms of product type, use, style, recipient or problem being solved. Customer-facing categories should therefore use language the target audience understands.

A useful hierarchy begins broad and becomes more specific. For example, an apparel store could use Women, Men and Kids as primary sections, with subcategories such as Tops, Bottoms and Footwear. Another store might organise around product purpose rather than audience. There is no universal structure, but related items should be grouped consistently and category names should not overlap so much that customers are unsure where to look.

Navigation should support both browsing and direct movement. The primary menu should contain the most important sections without becoming overloaded. Secondary links can appear in the footer or account area. Frequently used controls such as search, basket and account access should be easy to recognise. On mobile devices, limited screen space makes prioritisation particularly important.

Search can be essential for stores with larger catalogues. However, search does not replace good categories. Customers may not know the exact product name, may misspell terms, or may simply prefer browsing. A strong store therefore supports more than one way to find products.

Product pages also form part of the structure. Once a customer reaches a product, the page should make the next steps clear. Product title, images, price, important options, stock status where relevant, delivery information and the main call to action should be understandable. Additional information such as specifications, care instructions or returns guidance should be organised so that the page is informative without becoming chaotic.

**Friction** occurs when the customer must make unnecessary effort. Examples include hidden delivery costs, unclear button labels, too many pop-ups, broken links, difficult forms or menus that change unexpectedly between pages. Each small problem increases the chance that the customer abandons the journey. Removing friction does not mean eliminating useful information; it means presenting information at the right time in a clear way.

Consistency is another core UX principle. Similar actions should look and behave similarly. If one product page uses “Add to Cart” and another uses an unclear icon for the same function, customers may hesitate. Headings, buttons, price displays, navigation labels and page layouts should follow consistent patterns across the store.

The structure should also make trust and service information easy to find. Customers may want to know who the supplier is, how delivery works, how to contact the business, and what happens if something goes wrong. Applicable South African consumer and electronic-transaction requirements can require certain supplier and transaction information to be made available, so businesses should treat these pages as part of the purchasing experience rather than hiding them in obscure locations.

Testing should be task-based. Instead of asking only whether the site “looks good,” give a tester a goal: find a medium black shirt under a certain price, determine the delivery time, locate the returns policy, and place an item in the basket. Observe where the tester hesitates. A few realistic tasks can reveal structural problems that the store owner no longer notices because they already know the site.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
An online stationery store initially uses the menu Items > Supplier A > Supplier B > Supplier C because that matches the owner's purchasing records. Customers struggle because they do not know which supplier sells notebooks or pens. The store restructures the menu to Writing, Notebooks, Art Supplies, Office Essentials and School Supplies. Supplier information remains available on product pages and in administration, but the customer navigation now reflects how buyers search.

## Practical Activity
Sketch a store map for a hypothetical online shop. Include the home page, at least four product categories, product pages, basket, checkout, contact page and policy/service pages. Then write three test tasks that a new customer should be able to complete without assistance. Review the map and remove any unnecessary or confusing steps.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why should customer-facing categories not automatically copy the business's internal departments or supplier structure?
2. What is friction in an online shopping experience?
3. Why should a store offer clear navigation even if it has a search function?
4. Give one example of a task-based usability test.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Internal structures may make sense to staff but not to customers; categories should reflect how customers understand and search for products.
2. Friction is unnecessary difficulty or confusion that makes completing a task harder.
3. Customers may not know exact search terms or may prefer browsing, so categories and menus remain important.
4. Examples include asking a tester to find a specific product, determine delivery information, locate a returns policy or complete a basket action.

## Key Takeaways
- Organise the store around customer understanding, not internal business systems.
- Use simple, predictable hierarchy and navigation.
- Make important product, service and trust information easy to find.
- Reduce unnecessary friction and keep interaction patterns consistent.
- Test the store using realistic customer tasks rather than appearance alone.
