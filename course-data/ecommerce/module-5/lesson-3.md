# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 5: Customer Service & Returns
### Lesson 3: Handling Enquiries, Complaints & Escalations

**Estimated study time:** 35–40 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Distinguish between an enquiry, complaint and escalation.
2. Investigate a customer complaint using evidence.
3. Structure a professional complaint response.
4. Identify issues that require escalation.
5. Record complaint outcomes for future improvement.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Complaints reveal where customer expectations and actual service have separated. Some complaints result from genuine business errors; others arise from misunderstanding, delays or incomplete information. The goal is not to prove the customer wrong or to approve every demand. The goal is to establish the facts, apply the correct policy or legal requirement and reach a fair, documented outcome.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Enquiry** — A request for information or assistance.
- **Complaint** — An expression of dissatisfaction requiring attention or resolution.
- **Escalation** — Referral to a person or team with additional authority or expertise.
- **Evidence** — Reliable information used to establish what happened.
- **Root cause** — The underlying reason a problem occurred.
- **Resolution note** — A record explaining the final outcome and action taken.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Understand what the customer is reporting
Read or listen carefully before responding. Identify the order, product, date, requested outcome and specific problem. Do not assume that the first message contains every relevant fact.

### 2. Investigate using reliable records
Useful evidence may include the order record, payment status, product information, warehouse notes, tracking events, previous messages, refund records and approved policies. When records disagree, the discrepancy should be resolved rather than choosing whichever version is most convenient.

### 3. Respond in a structured way
A strong complaint response usually acknowledges the issue, explains the verified facts, states the action available and gives a clear next step. If the business made an error, the response should focus on correcting it rather than defending it.

### 4. Know when to escalate
Escalation may be necessary for high-value refunds, legal or compliance concerns, repeated failed resolutions, suspected fraud, threats to safety, serious privacy concerns or cases outside the staff member's authority. Escalation should include the evidence already gathered so the next person does not restart the case unnecessarily.

### 5. Separate emotion from evidence
Customers may communicate angrily, especially when they feel ignored. Staff should remain calm and focus on the issue. Professional service does not require accepting abusive behaviour, but it does require avoiding retaliation, sarcasm or unsupported accusations.

### 6. Learn from complaints
Repeated complaints can reveal catalogue errors, poor packaging, unreliable delivery promises, confusing policies or training gaps. Complaint data should therefore be analysed, not merely closed.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
Three customers complain in one week that a kettle is described as including a detachable filter, but their units do not contain one. Staff review the approved supplier specification and discover that the listing is outdated. The immediate cases are resolved according to the applicable process, the listing is corrected and the catalogue team is notified so the same problem does not continue.

## Practical Activity
Create a complaint-investigation record for an imaginary customer who received the wrong product. Include the evidence to check, likely root causes, possible resolutions and the point at which escalation would be required.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is the difference between an enquiry and a complaint?
2. Name three records that may help investigate a complaint.
3. Why should escalation include evidence already gathered?
4. What is root cause?
5. Why should complaint trends be reviewed?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. An enquiry requests information; a complaint expresses dissatisfaction requiring attention.
2. Examples include order, payment, tracking, warehouse and communication records.
3. It prevents unnecessary repetition and supports faster informed decisions.
4. The underlying reason the problem occurred.
5. Repeated complaints may reveal a systemic business problem.

## Key Takeaways
- Complaints should be investigated with evidence.
- Responses should explain facts, action and next steps clearly.
- Escalation is appropriate when authority, risk or complexity exceeds the current role.
- Professional communication remains important during conflict.
- Complaint trends can guide operational improvement.
