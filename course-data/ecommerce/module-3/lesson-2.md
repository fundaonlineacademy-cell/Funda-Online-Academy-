# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 3: Products, Listings, Pricing & Merchandising
### Lesson 2: Writing Effective Product Titles & Descriptions

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Write clear, accurate product titles.
2. Distinguish product features from customer-relevant benefits.
3. Structure product descriptions for readability and decision-making.
4. Avoid vague, exaggerated or misleading claims.
5. Apply a quality check before publishing listing copy.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Strong copy does not simply sound attractive; it communicates the information a customer needs to compare options and decide whether the product is suitable.

Poor copy creates friction. A vague title can make search results confusing, while copied supplier text may contain errors or claims the retailer cannot verify. In South Africa, section 43 of the Electronic Communications and Transactions Act requires sufficient information about the main characteristics of goods or services to enable an informed decision. Consumer-protection rules also restrict misleading descriptions and representations. Accuracy must therefore come before persuasion.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Product title** — The main name used to identify a product.
- **Feature** — A factual characteristic such as material, capacity or size.
- **Benefit** — The practical value a feature may provide to a customer.
- **Product description** — Customer-facing copy explaining the product and its important characteristics.
- **Claim** — A statement about what a product is, contains, does or can achieve.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Write titles for identification first
A good product title should help the customer recognise the item without opening the full page. It should normally include the product type and the most useful distinguishing information, such as brand, model, size, capacity, colour or pack quantity where relevant.

“Bottle” is too vague. “750 ml Stainless Steel Water Bottle – Black” gives useful identification information immediately.

Do not overload titles with every possible keyword, promotion and specification. Use the title for essential identification and place supporting detail in the description or specification fields.

### 2. Keep titles consistent
A store should use a repeatable naming pattern. A useful pattern may be:

**Brand + Product Type + Model/Key Attribute + Size/Capacity**

The exact structure can differ by category, but consistency helps customers compare products and helps staff maintain the catalogue. Internal SKUs should not replace customer-friendly titles unless the code itself is meaningful to the buyer.

### 3. Separate features from benefits
Features describe facts. Benefits explain why those facts may matter.

Example:
- Feature: “5000 mAh battery.”
- Benefit: “Provides extended portable power between charges.”

Benefits must remain reasonable and supportable. “Water-resistant outer fabric” may justify “helps protect contents from light rain,” but it does not justify “completely waterproof” unless that claim is verified.

### 4. Structure descriptions for easy reading
Customers often scan product pages. A useful description should present the most important information first, followed by supporting detail.

A practical structure is:
- short explanation of what the product is;
- main features and benefits;
- important specifications;
- what is included;
- compatibility, sizing or care information where relevant.

Short paragraphs and bullets are usually easier to scan than one long block of text. The goal is clarity, not unnecessary length.

### 5. Use precise, supportable language
Words such as “best”, “guaranteed”, “100% safe”, “unbreakable” or “works with every device” can create risk when they are not supportable.

Professional copy distinguishes facts from opinion. “Made from 304 stainless steel” is a factual claim that should be checked against a reliable source. “Modern appearance” is more subjective. If a factual claim cannot be verified, remove it or escalate it before publication.

### 6. Write for the actual customer
Different product categories require different information. Clothing customers may need sizing, material and care details. Electronics buyers may need compatibility, power requirements and included accessories. Furniture buyers may need dimensions, materials, assembly and delivery information.

The description should answer the questions a reasonable customer is likely to ask before buying.

### 7. Check copy against approved product data
Before publishing, compare the title and description with the approved product record. Confirm that size, colour, model, material, capacity, quantities, included items and compatibility details agree with the source information.

This prevents a common problem where the catalogue database contains one value while the customer-facing description contains another.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
**Weak listing:** “Premium Backpack – Best Quality! Perfect for Everyone!”

The title is vague and relies on unsupported promotional claims.

**Improved listing:** “20 L Water-resistant Laptop Backpack – Fits up to 15.6-inch Laptop”

The description can then explain the capacity, laptop compartment, outer material, dimensions, pockets and care instructions. It should not imply full waterproof protection unless that claim is supported.

## Practical Activity
Choose three imaginary products from different categories. For each, write a clear title, three verified features, one customer-relevant benefit for each feature and a 100–150 word product description. Identify the source you would use to verify every factual claim.

## Knowledge Check
1. What should a useful product title prioritise?
2. What is the difference between a feature and a benefit?
3. Why can exaggerated claims create risk?
4. Why should descriptions be checked against approved product data?
5. Give two examples of information that may be important for electronics.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Essential identifying details such as product type, brand, model, size or capacity.
2. A feature is a factual characteristic; a benefit explains its practical value.
3. Unsupported claims can mislead customers and create complaints or compliance problems.
4. The check ensures customer-facing copy agrees with the approved record.
5. Examples include compatibility, power requirements, dimensions and included accessories.

## Key Takeaways
- Titles should identify products clearly.
- Descriptions should combine verified facts with useful customer context.
- Features and benefits are related but different.
- Copy should be structured for scanning and comparison.
- Unsupported or misleading claims should not be published.
- Accuracy and consistency are essential parts of professional merchandising.
