# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 3: Products, Listings, Pricing & Merchandising
### Lesson 4: Product Categories, Collections, Tags & Navigation

**Estimated study time:** 30–35 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain why catalogue structure affects product discovery.
2. Distinguish between categories, subcategories, collections and tags.
3. Organise products using customer-friendly logic.
4. Identify navigation structures that create confusion.
5. Review a catalogue structure for consistency and usability.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A product can be perfectly written and photographed but still perform poorly if customers cannot find it. Catalogue structure connects individual listings to the browsing journey. Categories, collections, filters and tags should help customers move from a broad need to a relevant group of products without unnecessary effort.

The goal is not to create as many categories as possible. The goal is to create a structure that reflects how customers understand the range.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Category** — A primary grouping of related products.
- **Subcategory** — A more specific grouping within a larger category.
- **Collection** — A flexible product grouping based on a theme, season, promotion or customer need.
- **Tag** — A descriptive label used internally or by the platform to organise or filter products.
- **Filter** — A control that narrows visible products according to attributes such as price, brand or size.
- **Breadcrumb** — A navigation trail showing the customer's location within a site structure.
- **Taxonomy** — The organised system used to classify products.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Build around customer logic
Categories should reflect meaningful customer choices. A clothing store may use Men, Women and Children, followed by subcategories such as Shirts, Trousers and Footwear. A hardware store may organise around product type, use or department.

Avoid structures based only on internal supplier names, warehouse codes or staff terminology that customers are unlikely to understand.

### 2. Keep category levels manageable
Too few categories can create huge, difficult product lists. Too many can create a maze. The correct structure depends on catalogue size and diversity.

A useful test is whether a new customer can predict where a product should be found. If the same item could reasonably belong in several unrelated places, the naming or structure may need improvement.

### 3. Use collections for flexible merchandising
Collections are useful when products need to be grouped without changing the core category structure. Examples include New Arrivals, Winter Essentials, Back to School, Gifts Under R500 and Staff Picks.

Collections can support campaigns and merchandising, but they should not replace a stable product taxonomy. A product may belong to one core category while appearing in several temporary collections.

### 4. Use tags and attributes consistently
Tags and attributes should use controlled naming. If staff use `summer`, `Summer`, `SUMMER-2026` and `hot weather` for the same purpose, filtering and automation become unreliable.

Define approved terms and use them consistently. Tags are most valuable when they support a clear operational or customer-facing purpose.

### 5. Design useful filters
Filters are especially important in larger catalogues. Customers may need to narrow products by price, size, colour, brand, material, compatibility or availability.

Only offer filters that are supported by accurate product data. A colour filter is useless if half the catalogue has missing or inconsistent colour attributes.

### 6. Avoid empty and confusing paths
Categories with no products, duplicate names, broken links or products placed in clearly incorrect groups reduce trust. Catalogue maintenance should therefore include checks for empty categories, uncategorised products and outdated seasonal collections.

### 7. Support orientation
Breadcrumbs, clear headings and visible category labels help customers understand where they are. A customer who enters through a search engine or shared product link should still be able to navigate to related products.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
**Scenario:** A store sells kitchen appliances, cookware and dining products. Every item is currently placed under one category called “Home”.

**Improvement:** The store creates Kitchen Appliances, Cookware and Dining as primary groups, with useful subcategories such as Kettles, Blenders, Pots and Cutlery. It also creates a temporary “Kitchen Starter Bundle” collection without changing the core structure.

**Result:** Customers can browse logically while the business retains flexibility for campaigns.

## Practical Activity
Create a catalogue structure for an imaginary store with at least 30 products. Define three to five main categories, appropriate subcategories, two collections and five useful filters. Explain why each element helps the customer find products.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is the difference between a category and a collection?
2. Why can too many category levels be harmful?
3. When are tags useful?
4. Why do filters depend on accurate product data?
5. What should happen to outdated seasonal collections?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Categories form the stable catalogue structure; collections are flexible groupings based on themes or campaigns.
2. Excessive levels can make navigation confusing and slow product discovery.
3. They are useful when consistently applied for organisation, automation or filtering.
4. Inconsistent attributes produce incomplete or misleading filter results.
5. They should be reviewed, updated, hidden or removed when no longer relevant.

## Key Takeaways
- Catalogue structure should reflect customer thinking.
- Categories, collections, tags and filters have different roles.
- Consistent naming supports reliable search and merchandising.
- Navigation should minimise confusion and dead ends.
- Good product discovery depends on both structure and accurate data.
