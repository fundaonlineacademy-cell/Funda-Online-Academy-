# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 3: Products, Listings, Pricing & Merchandising
### Lesson 8: Catalogue Quality, Listing Maintenance & Product Performance

**Estimated study time:** 35–40 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain why product listings require ongoing maintenance after publication.
2. Identify common catalogue-quality problems.
3. Apply a practical product-listing audit process.
4. Use basic performance information to prioritise improvements.
5. Record and control important catalogue changes.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Publishing a product is not the end of catalogue management. Prices change, stock runs out, suppliers revise specifications, products are discontinued and customer behaviour reveals weaknesses that were not obvious at launch. A catalogue that is not maintained gradually becomes inaccurate and difficult to trust.

Ongoing quality control protects customer confidence and improves operations. It also reduces avoidable problems such as selling discontinued products, displaying outdated prices, showing incorrect variants or sending fulfilment staff incomplete product information.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Catalogue audit** — A structured review of product records for accuracy, completeness and consistency.
- **Listing status** — The current state of a product, such as active, draft, out of stock or discontinued.
- **Data quality** — The accuracy, completeness, consistency and usefulness of product information.
- **Change log** — A record of important updates made to a product or catalogue.
- **Conversion rate** — The proportion of relevant visitors who complete a desired action such as purchasing.
- **Return reason** — The recorded explanation for why a customer returned a product.
- **Performance indicator** — A measure used to identify how a listing or product is performing.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Maintain listings continuously
Listings should be reviewed whenever important source information changes. This may include supplier price updates, new packaging, corrected dimensions, product discontinuation, new variants or stock-policy changes.

Do not leave an old product active simply because the page still receives traffic. If the product cannot be supplied, the listing should be updated according to the store's policy and platform capabilities.

### 2. Audit for completeness and accuracy
A catalogue audit can check titles, descriptions, images, SKUs, prices, variants, stock states, categories, specifications, delivery data and listing status.

The audit should also look for duplicate products, missing images, inconsistent units and items placed in the wrong categories. High-value or high-volume products may deserve more frequent review because errors affect more customers or create greater financial risk.

### 3. Check customer-facing and back-office consistency
A product may look correct on the storefront while the back-office record is wrong. The SKU shown to staff, stock quantity, supplier code or fulfilment note may not match the customer-facing variant.

Good quality control therefore checks the full record rather than only the public page.

### 4. Use performance data intelligently
Performance information can help identify listings that need attention. A product receiving many visits but very few purchases may have unclear pricing, weak descriptions, poor images or a mismatch between customer expectations and the offer.

A high return rate can also reveal problems. If many customers return a shirt because it is smaller than expected, the issue may be sizing information rather than product quality alone.

Performance data does not automatically explain the cause. It identifies where investigation is needed.

### 5. Control important changes
Changes to price, SKU, variant structure, product status or specifications can affect several systems. These updates should be made carefully and, where appropriate, recorded.

A simple change log can include the date, product, field changed, old value, new value, reason and responsible person. This helps teams investigate errors and understand when information was updated.

### 6. Remove outdated merchandising
Catalogue quality also includes collections, recommendations and promotional labels. Remove expired sale messages, old campaign tags and “new” labels that are no longer true.

Outdated merchandising can mislead customers and makes the store appear poorly maintained.

### 7. Prioritise corrective action
Not every defect has the same impact. An incorrect selling price, wrong variant, misleading specification or product that cannot be supplied should normally receive higher priority than a minor formatting inconsistency.

Prioritisation should consider customer harm, financial impact, fulfilment risk, legal or compliance exposure and the number of affected products.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
**Scenario:** A store audits 200 listings and finds three problems: 25 products have inconsistent image sizes, two products show outdated prices, and one popular product has the wrong storage-capacity specification.

**Priority:** The incorrect specification and prices are corrected first because they can materially affect customer decisions and transactions. Image consistency is then repaired as a presentation improvement.

## Practical Activity
Create a 12-point catalogue audit checklist. Apply it to five imaginary product listings and record each defect as low, medium or high priority. For every defect, state the correction required and the reason for the priority rating.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why should product listings be reviewed after publication?
2. Give three items that a catalogue audit should check.
3. Why does a high return rate require investigation rather than an automatic conclusion?
4. What information can be recorded in a change log?
5. Which should usually be prioritised first: an incorrect product price or inconsistent thumbnail sizing? Why?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Product, supplier, price, stock and customer information changes over time.
2. Examples include titles, SKUs, prices, stock, images, variants, specifications and categories.
3. Returns indicate a problem pattern but do not by themselves prove the exact cause.
4. Date, product, field, old value, new value, reason and responsible person.
5. The incorrect price, because it can directly affect transactions, customer expectations and financial outcomes.

## Key Takeaways
- Catalogue management continues after a product is published.
- Audits should check both customer-facing and operational data.
- Performance information helps identify where investigation is needed.
- Important catalogue changes should be controlled and traceable.
- Corrections should be prioritised according to customer, financial and operational risk.
