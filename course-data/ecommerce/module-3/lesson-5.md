# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 3: Products, Listings, Pricing & Merchandising
### Lesson 5: Pricing Products for E-commerce

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Distinguish between cost price, selling price, markup and margin.
2. Calculate basic markup, gross profit and gross margin correctly.
3. Identify important cost factors when setting an online selling price.
4. Explain why pricing should be commercially sustainable and clearly presented.
5. Review a proposed price using simple commercial reasoning.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Pricing affects revenue, profit, customer perception and competitiveness. A price that is too high may reduce sales, while a price that is too low may create losses even when order volume looks strong. E-commerce businesses must also consider costs that are easy to overlook, such as payment fees, packaging, marketplace commissions, delivery subsidies, returns and promotional discounts.

Pricing decisions should therefore be based on evidence rather than guesswork. The learner does not need advanced accounting to make better pricing decisions, but must understand the difference between common pricing measures and calculate them accurately.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Cost price** — The direct amount paid to obtain or produce a product.
- **Selling price** — The amount charged to the customer before considering any later refund or adjustment.
- **Markup** — The amount added to cost to arrive at a selling price.
- **Markup percentage** — Markup expressed as a percentage of cost.
- **Gross profit** — Selling price less cost of goods sold for the item.
- **Gross margin percentage** — Gross profit expressed as a percentage of selling price.
- **VAT** — Value-added tax charged according to applicable South African tax rules by registered vendors.
- **Contribution** — The amount remaining after relevant variable costs to contribute toward fixed costs and profit.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Start with reliable cost information
A selling price should not be calculated from an incomplete cost figure. The product's purchase or production cost is the starting point, but an e-commerce business may also incur import charges, packaging, platform commissions, payment-processing fees, fulfilment costs and delivery subsidies.

Some costs apply directly to a single unit, while others are shared across the business. The pricing method should be appropriate to the level of analysis required. At minimum, the business should know which significant costs may reduce the profit expected from each sale.

### 2. Understand markup
Markup compares profit added to the cost price.

**Formula:**
`Markup = Selling Price - Cost Price`

**Markup % = Markup ÷ Cost Price × 100**

If an item costs R200 and is sold for R300:
- Markup = R100
- Markup percentage = R100 ÷ R200 × 100 = 50%

A 50% markup does not mean a 50% margin.

### 3. Understand gross margin
Gross margin compares gross profit with the selling price.

**Formula:**
`Gross Profit = Selling Price - Cost Price`

**Gross Margin % = Gross Profit ÷ Selling Price × 100**

Using the same example:
- Gross profit = R100
- Gross margin = R100 ÷ R300 × 100 = 33.33%

Confusing markup and margin can cause serious pricing errors. Staff should use the correct formula for the business decision being made.

### 4. Consider the full online transaction
Suppose a product costs R200 and sells for R300, but the business also absorbs R20 packaging, R10 payment fees and R25 of delivery cost. The apparent R100 gross difference between selling price and product cost does not represent the amount ultimately available to the business.

This is why online pricing should be reviewed alongside realistic transaction costs. A promotion that reduces the price by another R50 may make the order unattractive or loss-making.

### 5. Use competitor prices carefully
Competitor prices provide market information, but they are not automatically the correct price for your business. Competitors may have different supplier terms, product quality, service levels, delivery charges or strategic goals.

Price comparisons should use genuinely comparable products and should not replace internal cost analysis.

### 6. Present prices clearly
Customer-facing pricing should be accurate, understandable and consistent across product pages, cart and checkout. Where the business is a VAT vendor, tax treatment must be handled correctly in accordance with applicable requirements and the store's accounting setup.

Avoid a situation where a product appears to cost one amount on the listing but materially different mandatory charges appear only at the end without appropriate disclosure. Clear pricing supports informed purchasing decisions and reduces disputes.

### 7. Review price changes before publishing
Price changes can affect promotions, margins, marketplace listings, advertising feeds and customer expectations. Important changes should therefore be checked before publication.

A useful review asks: Is the source cost current? Is the intended margin reasonable? Are transaction costs considered? Is VAT treatment correct for the business? Are promotional rules affected? Will the same price appear consistently across connected channels?

## Worked Example / Demonstration
**Scenario:** An online retailer buys a small appliance for R480. The proposed selling price is R720.

**Calculation:**
- Gross profit before other transaction costs = R720 - R480 = R240
- Markup percentage = R240 ÷ R480 × 100 = 50%
- Gross margin percentage = R240 ÷ R720 × 100 = 33.33%

The retailer then estimates R25 payment and packaging costs per order. The team recognises that the R240 gross product difference is not the same as final profit and considers the additional cost before approving a discount.

## Practical Activity
For three imaginary products, record cost price and proposed selling price. Calculate markup amount, markup percentage, gross profit and gross margin percentage. Then add at least two realistic e-commerce transaction costs and explain whether the proposed price still appears commercially reasonable.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is the difference between markup percentage and gross margin percentage?
2. Calculate the markup amount if cost is R150 and selling price is R225.
3. Why should payment and packaging costs be considered when reviewing an online price?
4. Why is copying a competitor's price risky?
5. What should be checked before publishing a major price change?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Markup percentage uses cost as the denominator; margin percentage uses selling price.
2. R225 - R150 = R75 markup.
3. These costs reduce the amount actually retained from the transaction.
4. The competitor may have different costs, quality, service or strategy.
5. Check source cost, intended margin, transaction costs, tax treatment, promotions and connected sales channels.

## Key Takeaways
- Good pricing begins with reliable cost information.
- Markup and margin are different measures and must not be confused.
- E-commerce transaction costs can materially reduce expected profit.
- Competitor pricing is useful context, not a substitute for commercial calculation.
- Prices should be accurate and consistently presented throughout the customer journey.
