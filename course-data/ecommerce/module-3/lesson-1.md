# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 3: Products, Listings, Pricing & Merchandising
### Lesson 1: Product Information, SKUs, Variants & Catalogue Data

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the role of structured product information in an e-commerce catalogue.
2. Distinguish between a product, SKU, variant, attribute and specification.
3. Organise accurate catalogue data.
4. Identify product-information errors that affect customers, stock or fulfilment.
5. Apply a practical pre-publication quality check.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Online customers usually cannot inspect a product physically before buying. The product record must therefore help them identify the item, understand its important characteristics, select the correct option and know what will be supplied. Poor catalogue data can cause returns, stock errors, complaints and loss of trust.

In South Africa, accuracy also has a compliance dimension. Section 43(1)(h) of the Electronic Communications and Transactions Act requires an electronic supplier to make available a sufficient description of the main characteristics of goods or services so that a consumer can make an informed decision. The Consumer Protection Act also restricts misleading trade descriptions and false or deceptive representations.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Product** — The item, service or digital offering presented for sale.
- **SKU** — An internal identifier used to distinguish a specific sellable item or variant.
- **Variant** — A version of a product that differs by an option such as size, colour or capacity.
- **Attribute** — A descriptive characteristic such as brand, colour or material.
- **Specification** — A precise technical or measurable detail such as dimensions or weight.
- **Source of truth** — The approved record regarded as authoritative product information.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Build a complete product record
A professional product record contains more than a title and price. Depending on the item, it may include the name, SKU, category, brand, description, images, attributes, specifications, variants, price, tax treatment, stock quantity, weight, dimensions and shipping information.

### 2. Separate products from variants
A product is the main item being sold, while variants are selectable versions of that item. A T-shirt may be one product with black and white colours and several sizes. Each separately stocked colour-size combination may need its own SKU.

### 3. Use SKUs consistently
A SKU is mainly an internal management tool. It should be unique, stable and easy for staff or systems to use. A business might use `TSH-BLK-M` for a black medium T-shirt, while another business may use system-generated numbers.

Avoid assigning one SKU to different items or changing SKUs without a clear reason. Supplier, barcode or marketplace identifiers should be stored separately where the system allows.

### 4. Distinguish attributes from specifications
Attributes help customers browse and compare. Examples include colour, brand, material and size. Specifications provide exact facts such as 500 mm width, 1.8 kg weight or 256 GB storage.

Use consistent naming and units. Recording the same colour as “navy”, “Navy Blue” and “dark-blue” can weaken filters and reporting. Mixing units without clear labels can also create mistakes.

### 5. Verify information before publication
Product information should describe the actual item supplied. Material claims, included accessories, dimensions, compatibility, condition and capacity should be checked against reliable source information.

If two sources disagree, staff should not guess. For example, if a supplier spreadsheet states that a container holds 20 litres but the current manufacturer specification states 18 litres, the discrepancy should be resolved before publication. The business should use a recognised source of truth and escalate information that cannot be verified.

### 6. Perform a pre-publication quality check
Before publishing a product, confirm that:
- the title identifies the item clearly;
- the SKU is unique and linked to the correct item;
- variants match real stock options;
- attributes and specifications use approved terms and units;
- images represent the correct product;
- important claims are supported by reliable information;
- price, stock and delivery fields are complete; and
- the listing displays correctly on desktop and mobile.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
**Scenario:** A South African retailer is adding running shoes available in black and white, sizes 6–10. Stock is held separately for every colour and size combination.

**Approach:** The retailer creates one main product and ten variants. Each variant receives a unique SKU, such as `RUN-BLK-06`. Colour and size attributes use consistent labels, technical information is checked against the approved supplier specification, and every SKU connects to its own stock quantity.

**Result:** Customers can choose the correct option, staff can monitor stock accurately and fulfilment teams can identify the exact item to pick.

## Practical Activity
Create a **Product Data Sheet** for three imaginary products: one simple physical product, one product with at least four variants and one digital product. Record the product name, SKU or SKU pattern, category, attributes, specifications, variants, stock method, source of truth and two quality risks.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is the difference between a product and a variant?
2. Why should separately stocked variants normally have unique SKUs?
3. Give two examples of attributes and two examples of specifications.
4. Why is a source of truth important?
5. What should staff do when a specification cannot be verified?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. A product is the main offering; a variant is a selectable version such as a size or colour.
2. A unique SKU identifies the exact sellable item in inventory, order and fulfilment records.
3. Attributes may include colour and material; specifications may include weight and dimensions.
4. A source of truth prevents staff from choosing conflicting information arbitrarily.
5. Information should be verified before publication rather than guessed.

## Key Takeaways
- Accurate product data supports customer decisions, inventory and fulfilment.
- Products, variants, SKUs, attributes and specifications serve different purposes.
- Consistent identifiers, terminology and units improve catalogue quality.
- Product claims should be based on verified information.
- South African law reinforces the need for clear, non-misleading product information.
