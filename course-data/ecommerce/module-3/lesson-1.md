# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 3: Products, Listings, Pricing & Merchandising
### Lesson 1: Product Information, SKUs, Variants & Catalogue Data

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the role of structured product information in an e-commerce catalogue.
2. Distinguish between a product, SKU, variant, attribute and specification.
3. Organise accurate catalogue data for physical, digital and service-based offerings.
4. Identify product-information errors that can create customer, stock, fulfilment or compliance problems.
5. Apply a practical quality-control process before publishing or updating a product record.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A customer cannot physically inspect most products before buying online. The product record therefore has to perform much of the work that packaging, shelf labels and a salesperson would perform in a physical store. It must help the customer identify the item, understand its characteristics, select the correct option and know what will actually be supplied.

Poor catalogue data causes more than untidy pages. An incorrect size can lead to returns. A duplicated SKU can cause stock errors. A missing colour variant can prevent a sale. An inaccurate description can create complaints and damage trust. In South Africa, product information also has consumer-protection implications: electronic suppliers must provide a sufficient description of the main characteristics of goods or services to enable an informed transaction, while false or misleading representations are restricted under consumer-protection law. Product-data accuracy is therefore both an operational discipline and part of responsible selling.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Product** — The item, service or digital offering presented for sale.
- **SKU (Stock Keeping Unit)** — An internal identifier used by a business to distinguish a specific sellable item or variant.
- **Variant** — A version of a product that differs by an option such as size, colour, capacity or configuration.
- **Attribute** — A descriptive characteristic used to classify, filter or compare products, such as brand, material or colour.
- **Specification** — A precise technical or measurable detail, such as dimensions, weight, storage capacity or power rating.
- **Product identifier** — A code used to identify a product, which may be internal or external depending on the business and sales channel.
- **Catalogue** — The organised set of product records presented and managed by an online store.
- **Source of truth** — The approved record or system regarded as the authoritative version of product information.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Understand what a product record contains
A professional product record contains more than a title and price. Depending on the product, it may include the product name, SKU, category, brand, description, images, attributes, specifications, variants, price, tax treatment, stock quantity, weight, dimensions, shipping information, supplier reference and status.

Not every product requires every field. A downloadable e-book does not need parcel dimensions, while a refrigerator may require capacity, measurements, energy information and delivery considerations. The store should therefore define which data fields are mandatory for each product type.

### 2. Separate products from variants
A product is the main item being sold, while variants are the selectable versions of that item. For example, a T-shirt may be one product with black, white and navy colours and sizes from Small to Extra Large. Each sellable colour-size combination may need its own SKU because it can have its own stock quantity.

This distinction is essential for inventory accuracy. If all variants share one stock record, the store may continue selling a size that is already sold out. Good variant design connects the customer-facing options to the correct back-office stock record.

### 3. Create a sensible SKU system
A SKU is primarily an internal management tool. It should be unique, stable and easy for staff or systems to use. A business might use a structured format such as `TSH-BLK-M`, meaning T-shirt, black, medium. Another business may use a numeric system generated by its inventory platform.

The exact format is less important than consistency. Avoid assigning the same SKU to different items, changing SKUs without a business reason or embedding information that will become inaccurate. If an external marketplace, supplier or barcode system uses another identifier, keep that identifier in the appropriate field rather than confusing it with the store's own SKU.

### 4. Use attributes and specifications correctly
Attributes help customers browse and compare. Examples include colour, brand, material, size, style and compatibility. Specifications provide more exact facts, such as 500 mm width, 1.8 kg weight, 256 GB storage or 220–240 V operating range.

These fields should use consistent naming and units. Writing “navy”, “Navy Blue”, “dark-blue” and “blue/navy” for the same colour makes filtering and reporting unreliable. Likewise, mixing centimetres and millimetres without clear units can create fulfilment mistakes.

A catalogue standard should define approved values and measurement units. This improves search, filters, reporting, integrations and customer understanding.

### 5. Match information to the actual item supplied
Product information should reflect the real product, not an idealised version. Images, material claims, included accessories, dimensions, capacity, condition and compatibility should be checked against reliable source information.

South Africa's Electronic Communications and Transactions Act requires an online supplier to make available a sufficient description of the main characteristics of the goods or services so a consumer can make an informed decision. The Consumer Protection Act also restricts false, misleading or deceptive representations and misleading trade descriptions. This means catalogue teams should never guess specifications or copy unverified claims simply to make a listing look complete.

### 6. Establish a source of truth
Product information often arrives from several places: supplier spreadsheets, manufacturer sheets, packaging, photographs, purchasing records and previous listings. When these disagree, staff need a defined source of truth and an escalation process.

For example, if a supplier spreadsheet says a storage container holds 20 litres but the manufacturer's current specification says 18 litres, the discrepancy should be resolved before publication. Staff should record the verified value rather than selecting whichever figure looks more attractive.

### 7. Control catalogue changes
Product data changes over time. Packaging may change, an item may be discontinued, a new variant may be introduced or a specification may be corrected. Updates should be controlled so that staff know what changed, why it changed and who approved it where appropriate.

Important fields such as price, SKU, variant structure, product status and technical specifications deserve extra care because mistakes can affect inventory, payments, fulfilment and customer expectations.

### 8. Perform a pre-publication quality check
Before publishing a product, verify at least the following:
- The product title identifies the item clearly.
- The SKU is unique and linked to the correct sellable item.
- Variants match real stock options.
- Attributes and specifications use approved values and units.
- Images represent the actual product or are clearly appropriate illustrations.
- Material facts are supported by reliable information.
- Required price, stock, delivery and category fields are complete.
- The product appears correctly on desktop and mobile views.

A second-person check is useful for high-value, technical or regulated products because the person who entered the data may overlook their own error.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
**Scenario:** A South African online retailer is adding running shoes available in black and white, sizes 6–10. The supplier sends one general product code, but the retailer holds separate stock for every colour and size combination.

**Approach:** The retailer creates one main product record and ten sellable variants. Each variant receives a unique internal SKU, for example `RUN-BLK-06` and `RUN-WHT-06`. The catalogue uses consistent colour and size attributes, verifies the upper material and sole information against the supplier's approved specification, and links each SKU to its own stock quantity.

**Result:** Customers can select the correct option, stock can be managed accurately and fulfilment staff can identify exactly which item must be picked.

## Practical Activity
Create a **Product Data Sheet** for three imaginary products: one simple physical product, one product with at least four variants and one digital product. For each item record the product name, SKU or SKU pattern, category, attributes, specifications, variants, stock method, source of truth and two quality risks to check before publication.

Then review your sheet and identify any field where inconsistent terminology, missing units or duplicated identifiers could cause a customer or operational problem.

2. Why should each separately stocked variant normally have a unique SKU?

4. Why is a defined source of truth important when product information conflicts?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. A product is the main offering; a variant is a selectable version of that product such as a particular size or colour.
2. A unique SKU allows inventory, orders and fulfilment records to identify the exact sellable item.
3. Attributes may include colour and material. Specifications may include weight and dimensions.
4. A source of truth prevents staff from choosing conflicting information arbitrarily and supports consistent catalogue records.
5. The information should be escalated and verified before publication rather than guessed or presented as fact.

## Key Takeaways
- Accurate product information is the foundation of a reliable e-commerce catalogue.
- Products, variants, SKUs, attributes and specifications serve different purposes and should not be confused.
- Consistent naming, identifiers and units improve search, filtering, inventory and fulfilment.
- Product claims should be based on verified source information.
- South African consumer and electronic-transaction rules reinforce the need for sufficient and non-misleading product information.
- Every product record should pass a structured quality check before publication.
