# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 4: Orders, Payments, Fulfilment & Delivery
### Lesson 6: Cancellations, Refunds & Payment Reversals

**Estimated study time:** 35–40 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Distinguish between cancellation and refund processes.
2. Explain full and partial refunds.
3. Identify records needed before processing a refund.
4. Prevent duplicate or unauthorised refunds.
5. Communicate refund status accurately to customers.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Refunds affect customers, payments, stock and financial records at the same time. A poorly controlled refund can result in money being returned twice, stock being adjusted incorrectly or a customer being told that a refund is complete when it has only been requested.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Cancellation** — Stopping an order before completion according to the applicable process.
- **Refund** — Returning money from a completed or collected payment.
- **Partial refund** — Returning only part of the amount paid.
- **Refund reference** — A transaction or system reference identifying the refund.
- **Reversal** — A payment-system action that cancels or reverses a transaction according to the provider workflow.
- **Authorisation level** — The level of staff authority required to approve a financial action.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Determine the order stage
Before cancelling or refunding, staff should identify whether the order is unpaid, paid but not fulfilled, already dispatched or delivered. The correct process can differ at each stage.

### 2. Separate cancellation from refund
Cancelling an unpaid order may require no money movement. Cancelling a paid order may require a refund. If an order has already been dispatched, the business may also need to manage return or recovery of the goods.

### 3. Verify the amount
A full refund returns the approved amount for the entire transaction. A partial refund may apply to one returned item, a delivery adjustment or another approved reason. Staff should calculate the amount from the order record rather than guessing.

### 4. Use authorised processes
Refunds should be processed through approved systems and according to staff permissions. Before submitting a refund, confirm the order, payment record, reason, amount and whether any previous refund already exists.

### 5. Record stock implications
If goods are returned, stock should only be restored when the item has been received and meets the business's condition and restocking rules. A refund does not automatically mean the item is physically back in saleable inventory.

### 6. Communicate accurately
Customers should be told whether the refund has been approved, submitted or completed. Processing times may depend on the payment provider or financial institution, so staff should avoid promising an exact bank-credit time unless supported.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A customer paid R1,200 for three items and cancels one R300 item before dispatch. Staff verify the order, approve a R300 partial refund, record the refund reference and update fulfilment so only the remaining items are packed.

## Practical Activity
Create a refund-control checklist covering: order verification, payment status, refund amount, approval, previous refund check, stock impact, transaction reference and customer communication.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why can an order be cancelled without a refund?
2. What is a partial refund?
3. Why must previous refunds be checked?
4. Does a refund automatically make returned stock saleable again?
5. Why should staff distinguish between refund submitted and refund completed?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. An unpaid order may be cancelled without money having been collected.
2. It returns only part of the amount paid.
3. To prevent duplicate refunds.
4. No. Physical receipt and condition may still need to be checked.
5. They are different stages and customers should receive accurate status information.

## Key Takeaways
- Cancellation and refund are related but different processes.
- Refund amounts must be verified from reliable records.
- Financial actions should follow approved authority and systems.
- Stock should not be restored merely because money was refunded.
- Customer communication should reflect the actual refund stage.
