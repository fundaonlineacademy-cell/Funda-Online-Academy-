# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 4: Orders, Payments, Fulfilment & Delivery
### Lesson 7: Fraud Indicators, Suspicious Orders & Order Review

**Estimated study time:** 35–40 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Identify common indicators of suspicious e-commerce orders.
2. Distinguish indicators from proof of fraud.
3. Apply a structured order-review process.
4. Escalate suspicious transactions without unfair assumptions.
5. Protect customer and payment information during review.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Online sellers face fraud risks such as stolen payment credentials, account takeover, false delivery claims and chargeback abuse. At the same time, unusual customer behaviour does not automatically prove wrongdoing. Staff therefore need a fair, evidence-based review process that protects the business without treating legitimate customers as criminals.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Fraud indicator** — A signal that may justify additional review but does not by itself prove fraud.
- **Account takeover** — Unauthorised control of a customer's account.
- **Chargeback** — A payment dispute processed through the relevant payment system.
- **Velocity** — Unusually high transaction activity over a short period.
- **Manual review** — Human examination of a transaction flagged for additional checking.
- **False positive** — A legitimate transaction incorrectly identified as suspicious.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Recognise indicators carefully
Potential indicators can include repeated failed payment attempts, unusually large orders, rapid orders from a new account, mismatched information, requests to redirect high-value parcels or behaviour that differs sharply from normal patterns.

No single indicator proves fraud. A customer may legitimately order a large quantity or deliver to a different address. Review should combine available evidence.

### 2. Use platform and provider tools
Payment gateways, fraud-screening services and e-commerce platforms may provide risk scores, verification results or alerts. Staff should understand what those signals mean and avoid overriding them casually.

### 3. Apply a consistent review
A review can check order history, payment status, delivery information, account activity and relevant provider warnings. Staff should follow approved procedures and escalate high-risk cases to authorised personnel.

### 4. Avoid unsafe verification
Staff should not ask customers to send full card numbers, passwords or unnecessary identity information. Verification methods should use approved and proportionate processes.

### 5. Document the decision
If an order is approved, held or cancelled, the reason should be recorded. Documentation supports later investigation and helps the business improve fraud rules.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A new account places four high-value electronics orders within ten minutes, with repeated failed payment attempts followed by one successful payment and a request to redirect the parcel. These are multiple indicators, not automatic proof. The order is held for review and escalated according to the store's fraud procedure before fulfilment.

## Practical Activity
Create a risk-review table with five indicators. For each, explain why it may be suspicious, one legitimate explanation and the appropriate review action.

## Knowledge Check
1. Does one unusual order prove fraud?
2. What is a false positive?
3. Why should high-risk transactions be documented?
4. What sensitive information should staff avoid requesting unnecessarily?
5. When should an order be escalated for review?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. No. Indicators require context and evidence.
2. A legitimate transaction incorrectly identified as suspicious.
3. Records support consistent decisions and future investigation.
4. Passwords, full card details and other unnecessary sensitive credentials.
5. When indicators exceed the business's approved risk threshold or staff authority.

## Key Takeaways
- Fraud indicators are signals, not proof.
- Reviews should be evidence-based and consistent.
- Approved fraud tools and provider information should be used properly.
- Verification should not expose unnecessary sensitive information.
- Suspicious orders should be documented and escalated before fulfilment when required.
