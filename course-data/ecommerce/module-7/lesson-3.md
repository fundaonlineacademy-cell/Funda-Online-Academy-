# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 7: Online Security & Fraud Awareness
### Lesson 3: Phishing, Social Engineering & Suspicious Communications

**Estimated study time:** 35–40 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain phishing and social engineering.
2. Identify common warning signs in suspicious messages.
3. Apply a safe verification process before acting on requests.
4. Recognise risks in urgent payment, login or supplier-change requests.
5. Report suspicious communications appropriately.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Many security incidents begin with a message rather than a technical exploit. Attackers may impersonate a bank, courier, supplier, manager or platform provider and pressure staff to click a link, disclose a password or change payment details. The strongest defence is a habit of verification before action.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Phishing** — A deceptive message intended to trick a person into revealing information or taking an unsafe action.
- **Social engineering** — Manipulating people into bypassing normal security controls.
- **Impersonation** — Pretending to be a trusted person or organisation.
- **Credential theft** — Obtaining usernames, passwords or other authentication information without authority.
- **Verification channel** — A trusted method used to confirm whether a request is genuine.
- **Urgency cue** — Language designed to pressure immediate action.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Recognise common warning signs
Suspicious messages may use urgent language, unexpected links, unusual attachments, unfamiliar sender domains, requests for passwords, sudden banking changes or threats that an account will be closed immediately.

No single sign proves a message is malicious, but several unusual details should increase caution.

### 2. Verify using a separate trusted route
Do not verify a suspicious message by replying to it or using the phone number inside it. Use a known contact, official dashboard or independently confirmed number. Verification should not depend on the same channel that created the doubt.

### 3. Treat payment-detail changes carefully
A message claiming that a supplier has changed bank details deserves independent confirmation using the organisation's approved process. Staff should not redirect payments because of a single email request.

### 4. Protect credentials
Legitimate support teams should not require staff to disclose passwords. Login links in unexpected messages should be treated cautiously; where possible, navigate directly to the known service rather than following the message link.

### 5. Report instead of silently deleting
Reporting suspicious communications allows the business to warn other staff, block malicious senders or investigate whether anyone else interacted with the message.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A staff member receives an email appearing to come from the store owner: “Urgent — buy gift cards immediately and send the codes.” The sender address is slightly different from the normal company domain. The employee verifies the request through an approved internal contact method and confirms that the owner never sent it.

## Practical Activity
Create a suspicious-message checklist with at least eight warning signs and a three-step verification process that staff can follow before acting.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is phishing?
2. Why should suspicious requests be verified through a separate route?
3. Why are urgent bank-detail changes high risk?
4. Should staff disclose passwords to someone claiming to be support?
5. Why should suspicious messages be reported?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. A deceptive attempt to obtain information or trigger unsafe action.
2. The original communication may itself be controlled by an attacker.
3. They can redirect business payments if fraudulent.
4. No; passwords should remain confidential.
5. Reporting helps protect other staff and supports investigation.

## Key Takeaways
- Phishing often relies on urgency and trust.
- Verification should use a known independent channel.
- Payment and login requests deserve strong scrutiny.
- Passwords should never be disclosed through ordinary support requests.
- Suspicious messages should be reported and documented.
