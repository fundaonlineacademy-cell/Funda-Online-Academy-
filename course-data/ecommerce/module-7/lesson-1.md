# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 7: Online Security & Fraud Awareness
### Lesson 1: E-commerce Security Fundamentals & Common Threats

**Estimated study time:** 35–40 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain why security is a shared e-commerce responsibility.
2. Identify common threats affecting online stores.
3. Distinguish confidentiality, integrity and availability.
4. Recognise the business impact of security failures.
5. Apply basic risk-reduction principles.

## Why This Lesson Matters
An online store holds valuable assets: customer information, staff accounts, order records, payment connections, product data and access to business systems. Security failures can interrupt trading, expose personal information, enable fraud or damage customer trust. Security is therefore not only an IT issue. Store owners, customer-service staff, marketers and fulfilment teams all influence risk through the way they use systems and handle information.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Confidentiality** — Protecting information from unauthorised access.
- **Integrity** — Keeping information accurate and protected from unauthorised change.
- **Availability** — Keeping systems and information accessible when legitimately needed.
- **Threat** — Something capable of causing harm to systems, information or operations.
- **Vulnerability** — A weakness that may be exploited or lead to harm.
- **Risk** — The possibility and impact of harm occurring.
- **Security control** — A measure used to reduce security risk.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Protect the complete e-commerce environment
Security includes the storefront, admin accounts, devices, email, payment integrations, customer-service tools, databases and third-party services. A secure checkout cannot protect a business if an administrator account is compromised through a weak password.

### 2. Understand common threats
Common risks include phishing, credential theft, malicious software, compromised devices, unauthorised account access, fraudulent orders, data exposure, insecure integrations and accidental staff errors. Not every incident is caused by a sophisticated attacker; simple mistakes can also create serious harm.

### 3. Use the confidentiality-integrity-availability model
Confidentiality asks who may see information. Integrity asks whether information can be trusted. Availability asks whether legitimate users can access systems when required. An incident may affect one or all three.

### 4. Reduce risk using layers
No single control solves every security problem. Useful layers include strong authentication, limited access permissions, software updates, secure payment providers, backups, staff awareness and incident procedures.

### 5. Focus on business impact
Security decisions should consider financial loss, service disruption, privacy harm, legal exposure and reputation. High-value administrator access normally deserves stronger protection than a public product page.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A staff member receives an email asking them to “verify the store account urgently” using a link. The message looks convincing but leads to a fake login page. A security-aware employee does not enter credentials, verifies the request through an approved route and reports the message. Staff awareness has prevented a credential compromise.

## Practical Activity
Create a risk register with five e-commerce assets. For each, identify one threat, one possible impact and one practical security control.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Protecting information from unauthorised access.
2. A weakness that may allow harm.
3. Examples include phishing, credential theft and malicious software.
4. Different controls protect against different failure paths.
5. Staff behaviour and business processes can create or reduce risk.

## Key Takeaways
- E-commerce security protects systems, information and business continuity.
- Confidentiality, integrity and availability provide a useful security model.
- Threats include both malicious activity and human error.
- Strong security uses multiple layers of control.
- Risk decisions should consider business impact.
