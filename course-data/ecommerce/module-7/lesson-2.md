# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 7: Online Security & Fraud Awareness
### Lesson 2: Passwords, Multi-factor Authentication & Access Control

**Estimated study time:** 30–35 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain why unique strong passwords matter.
2. Describe the value of multi-factor authentication (MFA).
3. Apply least-privilege access principles.
4. Identify risks caused by shared accounts.
5. Use a basic joiner-mover-leaver access process.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Administrator and staff accounts can change prices, issue refunds, access customer information and alter store settings. If these accounts are poorly protected, a single stolen password can create major business risk. Access security should therefore combine strong authentication with careful permission management.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Authentication** — Verifying that a user is who they claim to be.
- **MFA** — Requiring more than one factor to verify access.
- **Least privilege** — Giving users only the access needed for their role.
- **Shared account** — One login used by more than one person.
- **Access review** — A periodic check that permissions remain appropriate.
- **Credential** — Information used to authenticate a user.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Use unique passwords
Reusing the same password across services creates a chain risk. If one service is compromised, attackers may try the same credentials elsewhere. Staff should use unique passwords and an approved password manager where the organisation permits it.

### 2. Enable MFA
MFA reduces the risk that a stolen password alone can unlock an account. Higher-risk accounts such as store administration, payment dashboards and primary email accounts should receive strong protection wherever supported.

### 3. Apply least privilege
A marketing assistant may need access to campaigns but not refunds. A warehouse employee may need fulfilment information but not payment administration. Permissions should match job responsibilities.

### 4. Avoid shared logins
Shared accounts weaken accountability because it becomes difficult to identify who performed an action. Individual accounts support audit trails and allow access to be removed without affecting other staff.

### 5. Manage access through staff changes
When someone joins, changes role or leaves, permissions should be reviewed promptly. Old accounts and excessive permissions should not remain active indefinitely.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A temporary contractor needs to update product images for two weeks. Instead of sharing the owner's administrator password, the store creates an individual account with limited catalogue permissions and removes access when the work ends.

## Practical Activity
Create an access table for owner, customer-service agent, warehouse picker and marketer. Give each role only the permissions required for its work.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why is password reuse risky?
2. What does MFA add?
3. What is least privilege?
4. Why are shared accounts weak for accountability?
5. What should happen when a staff member leaves?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. A compromised password may be reused against other accounts.
2. An additional verification factor beyond the password.
3. Providing only the access necessary for a role.
4. Actions cannot be reliably linked to one person.
5. Access should be removed promptly according to the approved process.

## Key Takeaways
- Passwords should be unique and securely managed.
- MFA adds an important layer of account protection.
- Permissions should follow least privilege.
- Individual accounts improve accountability.
- Access must change when roles or employment change.
