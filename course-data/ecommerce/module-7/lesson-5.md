# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 7: Online Security & Fraud Awareness
### Lesson 5: Customer Data, Privacy & POPIA Awareness

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain why customer data requires controlled handling.
2. Identify common categories of personal information in e-commerce.
3. Apply basic data-minimisation and access principles.
4. Recognise important POPIA responsibilities at an operational level.
5. Escalate suspected privacy incidents appropriately.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Online stores process names, contact details, delivery addresses, order history and sometimes other personal information. This information is useful for fulfilling transactions and supporting customers, but it can also cause harm if exposed, misused or retained without a legitimate purpose. Privacy therefore overlaps with security, customer service and legal compliance.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Personal information** — Information relating to an identifiable person or, in certain contexts, juristic person as defined by POPIA.
- **Data subject** — The person to whom personal information relates.
- **Responsible party** — The party determining why and how personal information is processed.
- **Operator** — A party processing personal information for a responsible party under applicable arrangements.
- **Data minimisation** — Limiting collection and use to information relevant for the purpose.
- **Security compromise** — Unauthorised access to, loss of or interference with personal information requiring assessment and response.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Know what customer data the store handles
Typical e-commerce data includes names, email addresses, phone numbers, delivery addresses, order history, support messages and marketing preferences. Some information may be more sensitive than other information, and access should reflect the task being performed.

### 2. Collect for a defined purpose
A business should understand why it collects each field. If a piece of information is not needed for fulfilment, service, compliance or another legitimate purpose, unnecessary collection can create additional risk.

### 3. Limit access
A warehouse picker may need the delivery details required to dispatch an order but may not need full marketing or refund history. Least-privilege access reduces unnecessary exposure.

### 4. Use data only through approved processes
Customer information should not be copied into personal messaging apps, unmanaged spreadsheets or private devices simply because it is convenient. Staff should use approved systems and follow the organisation's retention and deletion procedures.

### 5. Respect data-subject rights and marketing choices
POPIA provides rights relating to personal information, and direct marketing has additional rules. Staff should know how to route access, correction, objection or other privacy requests to the appropriate process rather than improvising answers.

### 6. Respond to suspected compromises
If customer information is sent to the wrong person, exposed publicly or accessed through a compromised account, staff should report the incident promptly. The business may have legal notification and investigation responsibilities depending on the circumstances, so incidents should not be hidden or silently deleted.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A support employee accidentally sends one customer's invoice to another customer. The employee should not simply delete the sent message and move on. They should report the incident immediately, preserve the facts and follow the organisation's privacy-incident process so the impact and required next steps can be assessed.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. The person to whom personal information relates.
2. Limiting information collection and use to what is relevant for the purpose.
3. Their job responsibilities require different information.
4. Unmanaged tools can weaken access, retention and security controls.
5. Report it promptly through the approved incident process.

## Key Takeaways
- Customer data is a business asset that requires careful protection.
- Collection and access should be tied to legitimate purposes.
- Least privilege and data minimisation reduce privacy risk.
- Staff should use approved systems for personal information.
- Suspected privacy incidents require prompt reporting and assessment.
