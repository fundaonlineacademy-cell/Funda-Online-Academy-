# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 2: Online Store Planning, Platforms & Setup
### Lesson 4: Store Structure, Navigation & User Experience

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain information architecture in an e-commerce context.
2. Design logical navigation around customer tasks.
3. Identify common causes of browsing and checkout friction.
4. Apply basic usability principles to menus, search and product discovery.
5. Review a store structure from the perspective of a first-time customer.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Customers should not need to understand the merchant's internal stock system to find a product. Store structure translates a catalogue and set of policies into a customer-facing experience. Clear navigation helps shoppers understand where they are, what choices exist and how to move toward a useful next step.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Information architecture** — Organisation and labelling of content so users can understand and navigate it.
- **Navigation** — Menus, links and other controls used to move through the store.
- **Hierarchy** — The relationship between broader and narrower levels of content.
- **Breadcrumb** — A navigation aid showing position within a hierarchy.
- **Usability** — How effectively and efficiently people can complete tasks using an interface.
- **Friction** — Unnecessary effort or confusion that makes a task harder.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Organise around customer understanding
Categories and menu labels should use language customers recognise. Internal codes such as “Range 4B” may be useful to staff but are poor customer navigation if shoppers do not know what they mean.

### 2. Build a clear hierarchy
A fashion store might begin with Men, Women and Children, followed by useful subcategories such as Shoes or Jackets. The structure should be deep enough to organise products but not so deep that customers must click through many vague layers before seeing relevant items.

### 3. Keep primary navigation focused
A menu containing dozens of unrelated links can increase cognitive load. Prioritise important shopping paths and place secondary information in appropriate supporting locations.

### 4. Support different discovery methods
Some customers browse categories while others search directly. Larger stores may also use filters. These tools should complement one another. Search quality depends on useful product data, while filters depend on consistent attributes.

### 5. Use meaningful labels
A link should help users predict what happens next. “Delivery & Returns” is more informative than “Information 2.” Buttons such as “Add to Cart,” “Continue to Checkout” and “View Size Guide” should be understandable without guesswork.

### 6. Reduce checkout friction
Checkout should request information that is genuinely required and explain errors clearly. Repeated fields, unclear costs, hidden requirements and unexpected steps can cause abandonment. Security and compliance controls may require effort, but unnecessary effort should be removed.

### 7. Maintain orientation
Breadcrumbs, page headings and clear selected states help customers understand where they are. This is especially useful in larger catalogues with several hierarchy levels.

### 8. Test realistic customer tasks
Do not evaluate navigation only by looking at the menu. Give a tester tasks such as “find a black running shoe under R1,200” or “find the return policy and delivery cost.” Observe whether the structure helps or obstructs completion.

### 9. Design across devices
Navigation that works with a mouse may fail on a touch screen. Mobile menus, filters and checkout controls need appropriate tap targets, readable text and sensible prioritisation.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A homeware store places all 400 products in a single “Shop” list and relies on scrolling. Customers looking for lamps must browse many unrelated products. The store introduces meaningful categories, search and filters for room, product type and price. Product discovery becomes a structured task rather than a test of patience.

## Practical Activity
Draw a site map for a fictional online store containing the homepage, three top-level product categories, useful subcategories, search, cart, checkout, contact and policy pages. Then write five realistic customer tasks and identify the shortest sensible path for each.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is information architecture?
2. Why can too many menu links reduce usability?
3. What is the purpose of a breadcrumb?
4. How are search and categories different discovery methods?
5. Give two examples of checkout friction.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Organising and labelling content so users can understand and navigate it.
2. It increases cognitive load and makes important choices harder to identify.
3. To show a user's position in a hierarchy and support navigation back through levels.
4. Search accepts direct queries; categories support structured browsing.
5. Examples include repeated fields, hidden charges and unclear error messages.

## Key Takeaways
- Store structure should reflect customer mental models rather than internal codes.
- Clear hierarchies reduce discovery effort.
- Search, navigation and filters work together.
- Checkout should avoid unnecessary friction.
- Realistic task testing reveals structural problems that visual inspection may miss.
