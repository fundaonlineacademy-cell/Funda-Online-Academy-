# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 2: Online Store Planning, Platforms & Setup
### Lesson 6: Mobile-first Design & Accessibility Basics

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain why mobile usability is essential for e-commerce.
2. Apply basic responsive-design principles to shopping tasks.
3. Identify common accessibility barriers in navigation, forms and product content.
4. Explain the role of readable text, keyboard access, labels and alternatives.
5. Test key store journeys on smaller screens and with different input methods.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Customers may discover, compare and purchase products entirely on a phone. A store that works only on a large desktop screen can lose legitimate customers at product selection, cart or checkout. Accessibility is equally important: people use websites with different abilities, devices and assistive technologies. Designing for clarity and operability often improves the experience for everyone.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Responsive design** — An approach where layout and interface adapt to different screen sizes and device conditions.
- **Mobile-first** — Designing essential content and interactions for constrained mobile conditions before progressively enhancing larger layouts.
- **Accessibility** — Designing digital experiences so people with different abilities can perceive, understand and operate them.
- **Alternative text** — Text describing the useful purpose or information of an image when a non-visual equivalent is needed.
- **Keyboard navigation** — Operating interface elements without relying on a mouse or touch pointer.
- **Focus indicator** — A visible sign showing which interactive element currently has keyboard focus.
- **Touch target** — An interactive control area designed to be usable by touch.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Mobile is not a smaller desktop
Small screens change how much information can be shown comfortably. Touch input differs from a mouse, and mobile networks may be slower or less stable. Important actions should remain visible, readable and easy to use.

### 2. Prioritise essential content
Product name, price, key images, variant selection, stock state, important delivery information and the main purchase action should not become difficult to locate on a phone. Secondary information can be organised progressively without hiding material facts.

### 3. Design usable controls
Buttons and links should be large enough and spaced well enough for touch. Tiny close buttons, overlapping menus and controls that require hover can create mobile barriers.

### 4. Keep text readable
Customers should not need constant zooming to read product information or checkout instructions. Use readable text sizes, adequate spacing and strong visual hierarchy.

### 5. Forms need clear labels
Checkout and contact forms should use clear labels and instructions. Placeholder text alone may disappear when a customer starts typing and should not always replace a proper label. Error messages should explain what needs correction rather than using colour alone.

### 6. Support keyboard operation
Interactive controls should be reachable and usable with a keyboard where applicable. Keyboard traps—situations where a user can enter a component but cannot move focus away—are serious usability and accessibility problems.

Visible focus indicators help users understand where they are in the interface.

### 7. Use meaningful image alternatives
Product images can contain important information. Where appropriate, meaningful alternative text can communicate relevant image content to users who rely on assistive technology. Decorative images may be handled differently so they do not add unnecessary noise.

### 8. Use structure and headings
Logical headings help all users scan content and can support assistive technology navigation. A page should not use headings only because they look large; headings should represent the content hierarchy.

### 9. Do not rely only on colour
If an error field is shown only with a red border, some users may not perceive the distinction. Add clear text or another understandable indicator. The same principle applies to stock states, selected options and warnings.

### 10. Test realistic mobile journeys
Test search, filters, galleries, variant selectors, cart, checkout, policy links, forms and payment transitions on representative screen sizes. Where possible, include real devices and browsers rather than only one desktop emulator.

### 11. Accessibility is ongoing
A store can become less accessible when new apps, pop-ups or content are added. Accessibility checks should therefore continue after launch and be included in major changes.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A customer opens checkout on a phone. The “Pay Now” button extends beyond the screen, form errors are shown only as red outlines and a promotional pop-up traps keyboard focus. The page technically loads, but the transaction is not reliably usable. The business should fix layout, add clear error messages and ensure the pop-up can be dismissed and navigated correctly.

## Practical Activity
Audit a fictional product and checkout flow under Mobile Layout, Touch Controls, Text Readability, Form Labels, Error Messages, Keyboard Access, Focus Visibility, Images and Policy Links. Record at least ten checks and classify findings as Pass, Needs Improvement or Blocking.

## Knowledge Check
1. What does mobile-first design prioritise?
2. Why can placeholder text be insufficient as the only form label?
3. What is a keyboard trap?
4. Why should errors not be communicated by colour alone?
5. Name four store areas that should be tested on mobile.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Essential content and interactions under mobile constraints.
2. It can disappear during input and may not provide adequate accessible labelling.
3. A component prevents keyboard focus from moving away normally.
4. Not every user can perceive colour differences reliably.
5. Examples include navigation, search, filters, product pages, cart and checkout.

## Key Takeaways
- Mobile usability is a core e-commerce requirement.
- Responsive design must preserve essential shopping tasks.
- Clear labels, readable content and usable controls help more customers.
- Keyboard access and visible focus are important accessibility basics.
- Accessibility should be retested as the store changes.
