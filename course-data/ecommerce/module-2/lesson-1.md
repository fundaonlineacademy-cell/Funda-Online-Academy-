# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 2: Online Store Planning, Platforms & Setup
### Lesson 1: Defining Store Requirements & Technical Needs

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Translate an e-commerce business concept into functional, operational and technical requirements.
2. Distinguish essential requirements from preferences and future needs.
3. Identify integration, scalability, support and ownership requirements.
4. Write requirements that can later be tested.
5. Create a requirements checklist before choosing a platform.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Platform selection should follow requirements, not the other way around. Businesses often choose software because it is popular, cheap or recommended by someone else, then discover that it cannot support required payments, delivery rules, inventory processes or customer-service workflows. A requirements-first approach reduces costly rework and gives the business a clear basis for comparing platforms.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Functional requirement** — A statement describing what the store must be able to do.
- **Non-functional requirement** — A quality or operating condition such as performance, security, usability or availability.
- **Integration** — A connection or exchange of information between systems.
- **Scalability** — The ability to support growth in products, users, orders or complexity.
- **Requirement priority** — Classification of a need as essential, desirable or future.
- **Acceptance criterion** — A condition used to verify that a requirement has been satisfied.
- **Technical ownership** — Responsibility for maintaining and controlling the store's technology.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Requirements begin with the business model
Start with the target customer, product type, expected catalogue size, order volume, payment needs, delivery model and support process established in Module 1. A platform is suitable only if it can support the actual operating model.

### 2. Define functional requirements
Functional requirements describe actions the store must support. Examples include:
- browse products by category;
- search the catalogue;
- choose product variants;
- add and remove cart items;
- calculate delivery according to defined rules;
- accept approved payment methods;
- create an order confirmation;
- update inventory;
- allow staff to view and manage orders;
- record tracking information.

Good requirements are specific enough to test. “The checkout must be good” is weak. “Customers must be able to select delivery or collection and review the final order total before confirmation” is clearer.

### 3. Define operational requirements
Operational requirements cover how staff will run the business. Consider catalogue maintenance, stock updates, fulfilment, refunds, customer support, reporting, staff roles and access permissions. If the platform requires technical work the business cannot support, that is an operational concern even if the feature exists.

### 4. Define non-functional requirements
Important non-functional areas can include:
- mobile usability;
- accessibility;
- page performance;
- account security;
- backup and recovery;
- reliability;
- administrative usability;
- audit trails;
- privacy and data handling.

These qualities influence whether the system can be operated safely and professionally.

### 5. Identify integrations
A store may need to connect with payment gateways, couriers, accounting systems, inventory services, email platforms or analytics. Integration should not be assumed merely because both services are popular. Confirm whether the connection exists, what information it exchanges, what it costs and who maintains it.

### 6. Estimate scale
A store with 20 products and ten orders a week has different requirements from one with 50,000 variants and thousands of daily transactions. Consider expected growth in products, traffic, orders, staff and channels. Scalability does not mean buying the most expensive system; it means avoiding a design that cannot reasonably support foreseeable growth.

### 7. Define ownership and support
Determine who controls administrator accounts, domain access, payment settings and subscriptions. Know how technical support is obtained, what happens if the platform has an outage and how the business can export important records if required.

### 8. Prioritise
Classify requirements as:
- Essential for launch;
- Desirable but not launch-critical;
- Future capability.

Prioritisation supports trade-offs when budget or time is limited.

### 9. Add acceptance criteria
A requirement should have a practical way to confirm that it works. If the requirement is “national delivery with exclusions for unsupported areas,” acceptance testing should include valid and invalid addresses and confirm the correct behaviour.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A retailer expects 80 products, card and instant electronic payment, courier delivery, collection at one location, inventory tracking and mobile checkout. Instead of choosing a platform first, the owner documents each capability, required integrations, expected order volume, staff responsibilities and test criteria. Platform demonstrations are then evaluated against this list.

## Practical Activity
Create a requirements register with at least 20 entries under Functional, Operational, Security/Privacy, Integration and Future Growth. Give each requirement a priority and one acceptance criterion.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. It describes what the store must be able to do.
2. Requirements provide evidence for whether a platform is suitable.
3. Examples include payment, courier, accounting, inventory and email systems.
4. The ability to support increasing products, traffic, orders or complexity.
5. They make requirements testable rather than subjective.

## Key Takeaways
- Requirements should guide technology selection.
- Functional, operational and non-functional needs all matter.
- Integrations must be verified rather than assumed.
- Scalability should reflect realistic growth.
- Technical ownership and support responsibilities must be clear.
- Acceptance criteria make platform comparison and testing more objective.
