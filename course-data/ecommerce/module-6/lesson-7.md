# E-commerce & Online Store
## Module 6: Digital Marketing & Social Media
### Lesson 7: Marketing Analytics, KPIs & Attribution Basics

**Estimated study time:** 35–40 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Distinguish marketing metrics from business outcomes.
2. Select KPIs that match campaign objectives.
3. Interpret basic e-commerce funnel data.
4. Explain why attribution is imperfect.
5. Use data to form and test marketing hypotheses.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Digital platforms produce many numbers, but not every number deserves equal attention. A campaign may have strong reach and weak sales, or fewer clicks but better conversion. Analytics is useful when it helps the business understand what happened, why it may have happened and what should be tested next.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **KPI** — Key performance indicator linked to an important objective.
- **Traffic source** — The channel or source through which a visitor reaches the store.
- **Funnel** — A sequence of customer actions from visit to purchase.
- **Bounce/engagement measure** — A platform-specific indication of how visitors interact with a page or session.
- **Attribution** — Assigning credit for a conversion to marketing touchpoints.
- **UTM parameter** — A tracking label commonly added to links to help identify campaign traffic.
- **Hypothesis** — A testable explanation or prediction.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Match KPIs to the objective
Awareness campaigns may focus on reach, impressions or qualified video views. Traffic campaigns may focus on relevant sessions and click quality. Sales campaigns should examine conversion, revenue, acquisition cost and commercial outcome.

### 2. Read the funnel
A basic e-commerce funnel might show product-page visits, add-to-cart actions, checkout starts and completed purchases. If many people add products to the cart but few begin checkout, the problem may be different from a store where customers reach checkout but do not complete payment.

### 3. Use segmentation
Overall averages can hide important differences. Performance may vary by device, channel, campaign, product, geography or new-versus-returning customer. Segmentation helps the team locate where a problem or opportunity is concentrated.

### 4. Understand attribution limits
A customer may see a social post, search the brand later, open an email and finally purchase directly. Different systems may assign credit differently. Attribution models are useful but should not be treated as perfect records of human decision-making.

### 5. Protect data quality
Tracking links, campaign naming and event configuration should be consistent. Broken tracking can lead to wrong conclusions. Marketing teams should also collect and use customer data in line with applicable privacy requirements.

### 6. Turn findings into tests
Analytics should lead to a hypothesis. For example: “Mobile checkout conversion is lower because the form is difficult to complete.” The team can then investigate mobile usability and test improvements rather than assuming the advertising audience is the problem.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A campaign brings 2,000 visitors. Four hundred add to cart, 300 begin checkout and 60 purchase. The conversion rate from visitor to purchase is 3%. The sharpest drop occurs between checkout start and purchase, so the team investigates payment, delivery-cost and checkout-friction issues before changing the advert.

## Practical Activity
Create a funnel with 5,000 sessions, 1,000 add-to-carts, 600 checkout starts and 180 purchases. Calculate overall purchase conversion and identify the largest drop-off by percentage.

## Key Takeaways
- Marketing data is useful only when connected to objectives.
- Funnel analysis helps locate where customers drop out.
- Segmentation reveals hidden performance differences.
- Attribution is helpful but imperfect.
- Good analytics leads to specific tests and decisions.
