# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 6: Adjustments & Basic Financial Reporting
### Lesson 8: Reading Basic Reports and Explaining Results

**Estimated study time:** 55–60 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Read basic profit-or-loss and financial-position reports.
2. Explain changes in balances using supporting bookkeeping records.
3. Distinguish profit, cash and working-capital concepts at a basic level.
4. Identify unusual trends requiring investigation.
5. Prepare a concise management-facing bookkeeping explanation.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Bookkeepers are often asked, “Why did profit fall?” or “Why is cash low when sales are strong?” Good answers come from tracing report movements back to the records rather than guessing. The bookkeeper's role is to explain what the books show, identify exceptions and refer higher-level analysis where necessary.

## Key Terms
- **Variance** — Difference between two amounts, periods or expectations.
- **Trend** — Direction or pattern in financial information over time.
- **Working capital** — Broadly, current assets less current liabilities.
- **Liquidity** — Ability to meet short-term obligations.
- **Gross margin** — Gross profit relative to sales, often expressed as a percentage.
- **Reasonableness review** — Assessment of whether balances make sense in business context.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Start with reconciled data
Do not explain a report before confirming major balances and adjustments are complete. An unreconciled bank or debtor account can make analysis misleading.

### 2. Explain movements, not just totals
If rent increased from R20,000 to R30,000, investigate whether there was a lease increase, duplicate invoice, catch-up charge or classification change.

### 3. Profit versus cash
Profit includes credit sales and non-cash expenses such as depreciation. Cash depends on collections, payments, financing and timing. A profitable company can still face cash pressure.

### 4. Debtors and cash flow
Sales may rise while cash falls if customers take longer to pay. Age analysis provides evidence for this explanation.

### 5. Creditors and cash
A rising creditor balance may mean more purchases on credit, delayed payments or missing payment allocations. Reconcile before drawing conclusions.

### 6. Gross profit movement
A falling gross profit may result from lower selling prices, higher direct costs, product mix or errors in cost-of-sales classification. Bookkeepers should provide evidence but avoid unsupported strategic conclusions.

### 7. Basic working-capital view
If current assets are R200,000 and current liabilities R150,000, basic working capital is R50,000. This alone does not prove strong liquidity because asset quality and payment timing matter.

### 8. Exception questions
Useful questions include:
- What changed from last period?
- Which accounts caused the change?
- Is the movement supported by source evidence?
- Is it timing, classification or genuine business activity?
- Does it require management or accountant review?

### 9. Communicating results
A concise explanation can state:
1. what changed;
2. the amount/percentage where useful;
3. the main verified causes;
4. unresolved items;
5. recommended bookkeeping follow-up.

## Worked Example
Sales increased from R300,000 to R360,000, but Bank fell by R25,000. Debtors increased from R60,000 to R120,000 and the age analysis shows R45,000 more in overdue balances.

A reasonable bookkeeping explanation is that stronger sales did not convert fully into cash because customer receivables increased, especially overdue balances. Management may need credit-control action. This is stronger than saying “the company spent too much” without evidence.

## Practical Activity
Compare two periods:
- Sales: R250,000 → R290,000
- Gross Profit: R100,000 → R105,000
- Debtors: R40,000 → R75,000
- Bank: R55,000 → R30,000
- Creditors: R35,000 → R50,000

Write a five-sentence factual explanation and list three follow-up checks.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Unresolved errors can make conclusions unreliable.
2. Profit includes credit sales and non-cash items, while cash follows actual receipts/payments.
3. Sales may remain unpaid and accumulate in receivables.
4. More credit purchases, delayed payments or allocation issues are possible explanations.
5. Current assets less current liabilities.
6. Changes may reflect price, cost, mix or bookkeeping classification issues.
7. Difference between two amounts or expectations.
8. Verified movement, cause, unresolved items and next action.

## Key Takeaways
- Financial reports should be explained from reconciled bookkeeping evidence.
- Profit and cash are different measures.
- Debtor and creditor movements often explain cash-flow pressure.
- Trend analysis should lead to evidence-based questions, not guesses.
- Bookkeepers explain records and escalate wider accounting or strategic judgement appropriately.
