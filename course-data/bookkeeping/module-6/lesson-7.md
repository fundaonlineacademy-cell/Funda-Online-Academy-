# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 6: Adjustments & Basic Financial Reporting
### Lesson 7: Balance Sheet / Statement of Financial Position Basics

**Estimated study time:** 50–55 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the purpose of a statement of financial position.
2. Classify common assets, liabilities and equity balances.
3. Apply the accounting equation to a basic report.
4. Explain how accumulated depreciation and receivables appear in financial position.
5. Perform simple reasonableness and reconciliation checks.

## Why This Lesson Matters
The statement of financial position, often called the balance sheet, shows what the business owns, owes and the owners' residual interest at a specific date. It depends directly on accurate ledger balances and period-end adjustments.

## Key Terms
- **Asset** — Resource controlled by the business expected to provide future economic benefit.
- **Liability** — Present obligation of the business.
- **Equity** — Owners' residual interest after liabilities are deducted from assets.
- **Current asset/liability** — Short-term classification determined by applicable reporting policy.
- **Non-current asset/liability** — Longer-term classification determined by reporting policy.
- **Carrying amount** — Amount at which an asset or liability is presented after relevant adjustments.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. A position at a date
A balance sheet is prepared at a specific date such as 31 December. It is not a summary of activity over the year; it is the financial position at that point.

### 2. Accounting equation
The core relationship is:

**Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

The statement should remain consistent with this relationship.

### 3. Common assets
Examples:
- Bank/cash;
- Debtors / accounts receivable;
- Inventory where relevant;
- Prepayments;
- equipment and vehicles;
- other assets supported by the chart of accounts.

### 4. Common liabilities
Examples:
- Creditors / accounts payable;
- accrued expenses;
- loans;
- tax or payroll control balances where applicable;
- other valid obligations.

### 5. Equity
Equity can include owner capital, retained profits or accumulated losses, drawings or other equity components depending on business type and accounting framework.

### 6. Fixed assets and accumulated depreciation
Equipment may appear at cost less accumulated depreciation. Example:
- Equipment cost R100,000
- Accumulated depreciation R30,000
- Carrying amount R70,000.

### 7. Debtors
Debtors should be supported by reconciled customer balances and adjusted for authorised write-offs or allowances where applicable.

### 8. Liabilities need completeness checks
Missing supplier invoices or accruals can understate liabilities and overstate profit/equity. Period-end creditor and accrual reviews support completeness.

### 9. Reasonableness checks
Compare balances to:
- bank reconciliations;
- debtor/creditor schedules;
- asset register;
- loan statements;
- prior periods.

### 10. Bookkeeper role
Bookkeepers support reliable underlying balances. Final classification, disclosure and presentation decisions may require an accountant or authorised finance professional.

## Worked Example
Assets:
- Bank R45,000
- Debtors R30,000
- Equipment carrying amount R75,000
Total assets = R150,000.

Liabilities:
- Creditors R35,000
- Loan R25,000
Total liabilities = R60,000.

Equity = R90,000.

Check:
R150,000 = R60,000 + R90,000.

Calculate total assets, liabilities and the balancing equity amount.

## Key Takeaways
- The balance sheet shows financial position at a date.
- Assets must equal liabilities plus equity.
- Reported balances should reconcile to supporting schedules.
- Accumulated depreciation reduces asset carrying amount.
- Completeness of liabilities is as important as accuracy of assets.
