# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 6: Adjustments & Basic Financial Reporting
### Lesson 2: Accruals and Prepayments

**Estimated study time:** 55–60 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the difference between accruals and prepayments.
2. Calculate simple period-end accruals and prepayments.
3. Record basic adjusting journal entries.
4. Explain how these adjustments affect profit, assets and liabilities.
5. Maintain supporting schedules for later reversal or settlement.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Bills and payments do not always arrive in the same period as the expense they relate to. Accruals and prepayments help reports reflect the cost of the correct period rather than simply the cash date.

## Key Terms
- **Accrual** — Expense incurred but not yet recorded or paid at period end.
- **Prepayment** — Payment made in advance for a future period benefit.
- **Accrued expense liability** — Amount recognised as owed for an expense already incurred.
- **Prepaid expense asset** — Unused portion of a payment that relates to future periods.
- **Apportionment** — Dividing an amount between accounting periods using a reasonable basis.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Accrual logic
If the business used a service before period end but the invoice has not arrived, the cost belongs to the period in which it was incurred.

Example: March security service estimated from contract at R3,000, invoice arrives in April.
- Debit Security Expense R3,000.
- Credit Accrued Expenses R3,000.

### 2. Prepayment logic
If the business pays for a future period, the unused portion is an asset at period end.

Example: R12,000 annual insurance paid on 1 January. At 31 March, three months have been consumed and nine remain.
- Expense consumed: R3,000.
- Prepaid portion: R9,000.

If the full R12,000 was initially expensed, an adjustment may:
- Debit Prepaid Insurance R9,000.
- Credit Insurance Expense R9,000.

### 3. Use a support schedule
For each accrual or prepayment record:
- supplier/expense;
- contract or source basis;
- total amount;
- period covered;
- calculation;
- adjustment amount;
- journal reference;
- reversal/settlement status.

### 4. Estimates need a basis
An accrual may involve an estimate, but it should be reasonable and supported by contracts, usage, prior invoices or another approved basis. Do not invent amounts merely to smooth profit.

### 5. Settlement next period
When the actual invoice arrives, compare it to the accrual. If the invoice is R3,150 and the accrual was R3,000, the additional R150 needs appropriate treatment.

### 6. Reversal
Some systems reverse accruals automatically at the start of the next period. Others clear the accrual when the invoice is captured. Understand the configured process to avoid double counting.

### 7. Common errors
- forgetting to reverse an accrual;
- expensing a full annual payment immediately when policy requires apportionment;
- accruing the same invoice twice;
- using the wrong number of months;
- failing to clear old accrual balances.

## Worked Example
Rent of R24,000 is paid every two months in advance on 1 February for February and March. At 28 February, only one month belongs to February.

If the full payment was expensed:
- February expense should be R12,000.
- R12,000 relates to March and should remain prepaid.

Adjustment:
- Debit Prepaid Rent R12,000.
- Credit Rent Expense R12,000.

## Practical Activity
Calculate and journal:
1. Electricity R2,400 used in June but invoice arrives in July.
2. R18,000 six-month insurance paid on 1 May; reporting date 30 June.
3. Security accrual of R4,000 where actual invoice next month is R4,250.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Expense incurred but not yet recorded/paid.
2. Payment for benefit that belongs partly or wholly to a future period.
3. The business still has future economic benefit from the payment.
4. By contracts, usage, prior invoices or another reasonable approved basis.
5. The difference is accounted for when the actual amount is processed.
6. They preserve calculation, source and clearing history.
7. Expenses/liabilities can be misstated.
8. To avoid double-counting or leaving stale accrual balances.

## Key Takeaways
- Accruals bring missing current-period costs into the books.
- Prepayments move future-period costs out of current expense.
- Calculations need a clear period basis.
- Support schedules help clear and review adjustments.
- Reversal and settlement must be controlled.
