# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 6: Adjustments & Basic Financial Reporting
### Lesson 6: Income Statement / Statement of Profit or Loss Basics

**Estimated study time:** 50–55 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the purpose of a statement of profit or loss.
2. Identify common income and expense categories.
3. Calculate gross profit and basic operating profit where relevant.
4. Explain how period-end adjustments affect reported profit.
5. Perform basic reasonableness checks on an income statement.

## Why This Lesson Matters
The income statement, also called the statement of profit or loss, summarises financial performance over a period. Bookkeepers help ensure that the income and expense balances feeding this report are complete, correctly classified and period-appropriate.

## Key Terms
- **Revenue / Income** — Amount earned from ordinary business activities or other recognised sources.
- **Cost of sales** — Direct cost associated with goods or services sold, where applicable.
- **Gross profit** — Revenue less cost of sales.
- **Operating expense** — Cost of running the business, such as rent or administration.
- **Profit** — Income exceeding expenses for the period.
- **Loss** — Expenses exceeding income for the period.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. The report covers a period
Unlike a balance sheet, which shows a position at a date, the income statement covers activity between dates, such as one month or one year.

### 2. Revenue
Revenue should represent valid income earned in the period. Credit sales can create revenue before cash is collected.

### 3. Cost of sales
For a trading business, cost of sales may include the cost of inventory sold. Gross profit is:

**Sales - Cost of Sales = Gross Profit**

Example: Sales R500,000; Cost of Sales R300,000 → Gross Profit R200,000.

### 4. Operating expenses
Examples include rent, salaries, utilities, bank charges, depreciation and office costs. Classification should follow the chart of accounts and reporting policy.

### 5. Basic profit calculation
If gross profit is R200,000 and operating expenses are R150,000, operating profit before other items is R50,000.

### 6. Adjustments matter
An omitted electricity accrual understates expenses and overstates profit. An unrecorded prepayment may overstate current expense. Depreciation reduces profit even though it does not represent a new cash payment in that period.

### 7. Compare with prior periods
Large movements deserve explanation. If telephone expense doubles, investigate whether usage increased, an annual charge was posted, or an invoice was duplicated.

### 8. Profit is not the same as cash
A profitable business may have low cash because customers have not paid, inventory was purchased or loans were repaid. Bookkeepers should avoid explaining profit as if it were bank balance.

### 9. Reporting boundaries
Final presentation and accounting-policy decisions may belong to an accountant or authorised finance role. Bookkeepers should ensure the underlying balances and schedules are reliable.

## Worked Example
Sales R250,000.
Cost of sales R140,000.
Operating expenses:
- Salaries R45,000
- Rent R18,000
- Utilities R7,000
- Depreciation R5,000
- Bank charges R2,000

Gross profit = R110,000.
Total operating expenses = R77,000.
Operating profit = **R33,000**.

## Key Takeaways
- The income statement measures performance over a period.
- Revenue, cost of sales and expenses must be correctly classified.
- Adjustments can materially change reported profit.
- Profit is not the same as cash.
- Reasonableness checks improve report quality.
