# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 6: Adjustments & Basic Financial Reporting
### Lesson 4: Bad Debts, Allowances and Other Routine Adjustments

**Estimated study time:** 50–55 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain when a debtor balance may require write-off or allowance treatment.
2. Distinguish a specific bad-debt write-off from an allowance estimate.
3. Record simple authorised bad-debt adjustments.
4. Identify other routine period-end adjustments.
5. Preserve evidence and approval for judgement-based adjustments.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Not every amount owed by a customer will be collected. Financial reports should not present receivables as fully recoverable when evidence suggests otherwise. Bookkeepers may support bad-debt and allowance calculations, but approval and accounting policy are essential because these adjustments affect profit and asset values.

## Key Terms
- **Bad debt** — Receivable determined to be irrecoverable under the organisation's approved process.
- **Write-off** — Removal of a specific receivable balance from the books after appropriate approval.
- **Allowance for doubtful debts / expected credit loss allowance** — Estimate reducing receivables for amounts that may not be collected, according to accounting policy.
- **Recoverability** — Likelihood that a receivable will be collected.
- **Adjustment schedule** — Supporting record showing calculations and approvals.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Old debt is not automatically bad debt
An overdue amount may be disputed, incorrectly allocated, missing a credit note or awaiting a promised payment. Reconcile and investigate before write-off.

### 2. Specific write-off
If a R4,000 customer balance is formally approved as irrecoverable, a simple entry may debit Bad Debt Expense and credit Debtors, subject to the accounting system and policy.

### 3. Allowances
An allowance does not necessarily remove individual customer invoices. Instead, it recognises that part of the total receivables may not be collected. The calculation can depend on ageing, historical loss patterns and accounting policy.

### 4. Authority
Bookkeepers should not decide alone that a customer balance is irrecoverable. Collection history, legal action, disputes and management judgement may be relevant.

### 5. Recoveries
If a previously written-off amount is later recovered, follow the approved accounting treatment and preserve the link to the original write-off.

### 6. Other routine adjustments
Depending on the business, period-end work may also include:
- inventory corrections;
- clearing suspense balances;
- reclassifying expenses/assets;
- correcting cut-off;
- allocating prepaid or accrued costs;
- adjusting owner drawings or inter-account transfers.

### 7. Avoid profit manipulation
Adjustments should reflect evidence and policy, not a target profit number. Unsupported provisions or delayed write-offs can misstate financial results.

### 8. Support schedules
Maintain customer, amount, reason, collection history, approval, journal reference and later recovery information where relevant.

## Worked Example
Debtors total R100,000. Management approves a specific R5,000 write-off after documented collection attempts, and separately approves an allowance based on the organisation's accounting policy for the remaining receivables.

The bookkeeper records only the authorised entries and keeps the calculations separate: the write-off removes a known specific balance, while the allowance estimates potential future losses on balances still recorded as receivables.

## Practical Activity
Classify each item as likely write-off, allowance consideration, allocation correction or dispute follow-up:
1. Customer insolvent; R7,000 approved irrecoverable.
2. R4,500 receipt posted to wrong customer.
3. Customer disputes R3,000 price difference.
4. Age analysis shows a growing group of 120-day balances.
5. Old balance cleared after a missing credit note is found.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. No. Reconcile and investigate first.
2. Authorised removal of a specific irrecoverable receivable.
3. An estimate reducing receivables for potential collection losses according to policy.
4. Recoverability involves judgement and affects reported profit/assets.
5. Follow the approved recovery accounting process and preserve the audit trail.
6. Examples: suspense clearing, reclassification, cut-off, inventory, accrual/prepayment adjustments.
7. Financial reports should reflect evidence, not desired outcomes.
8. Amount, basis, reason, approval and journal traceability.

## Key Takeaways
- Reconcile before treating old debt as irrecoverable.
- Specific write-offs and allowances are different concepts.
- Judgement-based adjustments need policy, evidence and authority.
- Recoveries should remain linked to prior treatment.
- Routine period-end corrections must remain traceable.
