# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 6: Adjustments & Basic Financial Reporting
### Lesson 1: Why Period-end Adjustments Are Needed

**Estimated study time:** 50–55 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain why bookkeeping records may need adjustment at period end.
2. Distinguish cash timing from income/expense recognition.
3. Identify common period-end adjustment categories.
4. Explain how adjustments affect the trial balance and reports.
5. Apply authority and evidence controls to adjustment journals.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Day-to-day bookkeeping records transactions as they occur, but financial reports aim to describe the correct period. Some costs are paid before or after the period they relate to; assets lose value over time; debts may become doubtful. Period-end adjustments align the books with the economic period being reported.

## Key Terms
- **Period end** — Cut-off date for an accounting reporting period.
- **Adjustment** — Entry made to correct timing, valuation or classification before reporting.
- **Accrual basis** — Accounting approach that recognises income and expenses when earned/incurred rather than only when cash moves.
- **Cut-off** — Ensuring transactions are recorded in the correct period.
- **Adjusting journal** — Journal used to record an approved period-end adjustment.
- **Adjusted trial balance** — Trial balance after approved adjustments are posted.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Cash timing and accounting timing can differ
A business may use electricity in March but receive the invoice in April. If March reports are prepared before the invoice arrives, an accrual may be needed so March includes the cost it incurred.

### 2. Common adjustment categories
These can include:
- accruals;
- prepayments;
- depreciation;
- bad debts and approved allowances;
- inventory or stock adjustments where relevant;
- correcting classification or cut-off errors.

### 3. Adjustments need evidence
A period-end journal should not be a guess. Use supporting calculations, contracts, schedules, invoices, asset registers or management/accountant instructions.

### 4. Authority matters
Some adjustments require accountant or manager approval. Bookkeepers may prepare calculations and journals but should not invent policies or estimates outside their authority.

### 5. Double entry still applies
Every adjustment must preserve total debits = total credits.

### 6. Adjustments affect reports
An accrued expense increases the period's expense and creates a liability. A prepayment adjustment reduces current expense and creates an asset for the future benefit. Depreciation creates an expense and accumulated depreciation.

### 7. Reversing entries
Some accrual systems use a reversal at the start of the next period to avoid double counting when the actual invoice arrives. Use the organisation's approved process rather than reversing every journal automatically.

### 8. Audit trail
Each adjustment should have a reference, clear narration, source schedule and approval evidence where required.

## Worked Example
At 31 March, telephone services of R1,800 were used but no invoice has arrived.

If an accrual is approved:
- Debit Telephone Expense R1,800.
- Credit Accrued Expenses R1,800.

March now contains the cost, while the liability shows the amount expected to be paid later.

## Practical Activity
For each item, decide whether a period-end adjustment may be needed:
1. March electricity invoice received in April.
2. Twelve-month insurance paid in advance.
3. Delivery vehicle used for another year.
4. Customer balance approved as irrecoverable.
5. Office stationery purchased and fully consumed in the same period.

State what evidence you would require before posting.

## Key Takeaways
- Cash movement does not always equal accounting timing.
- Period-end adjustments improve period accuracy.
- Adjustments require evidence, authority and balanced journals.
- Adjusted balances feed the financial reports.
- Clear schedules and narrations preserve the audit trail.
