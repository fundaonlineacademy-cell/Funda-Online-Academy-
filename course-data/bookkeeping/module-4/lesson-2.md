# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 4: Cashbooks, Petty Cash & Bank Reconciliation
### Lesson 2: EFTs, Debit Orders, Card Payments and Bank Entries

**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain how common electronic bank transactions appear in bookkeeping records.
2. Distinguish EFTs, debit orders and card transactions by source evidence and accounting effect.
3. Identify fees, reversals and timing issues in bank entries.
4. Avoid duplicate capture when transactions appear through more than one source.
5. Apply appropriate controls to unusual or changed payment details.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Most modern businesses move money electronically. Bookkeepers must understand how the same transaction can appear on invoices, payment instructions, bank feeds and statements. Without careful matching, the same payment can be captured twice or a bank charge can be overlooked.

## Key Terms
- **EFT** — Electronic funds transfer between bank accounts.
- **Debit order** — Authorised recurring or once-off collection initiated against a bank account.
- **Card transaction** — Payment processed through debit or credit card infrastructure.
- **Bank feed** — Electronic import of banking transactions into accounting software.
- **Reversal** — Transaction that reverses or returns a prior banking entry.
- **Merchant fee** — Charge deducted by a payment processor or bank from card receipts.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. EFT payments
An EFT to a supplier usually reduces Bank and settles Creditors if the supplier invoice was already captured. Avoid debiting the expense again.

### 2. EFT receipts
A customer EFT normally increases Bank and reduces Debtors when it settles an existing invoice. If the source is unclear, investigate before allocation.

### 3. Debit orders
Debit orders commonly cover items such as insurance, subscriptions, loan instalments and service contracts. The bookkeeper should confirm what the debit order relates to and whether VAT, interest or principal elements need separate treatment.

### 4. Card purchases
Company-card purchases require supporting receipts or invoices. The card statement proves the charge but may not explain the business purpose or correct account classification.

### 5. Card receipts
Card-sale settlements may be deposited net of merchant fees. For example, gross customer sales of R20,000 may result in a bank deposit of R19,500 and a R500 merchant fee. The bookkeeper should not reduce sales merely because the bank receives the net amount.

### 6. Reversals and returned payments
A reversed debit order, failed EFT or chargeback can undo an earlier banking movement. Investigate the original entry before capturing the reversal so the correct account is restored.

### 7. Bank charges and interest
Bank statements often contain service fees and interest that were not known until statement or feed review. These should be captured with the correct date, amount and account classification.

### 8. Avoiding duplicates
If an EFT was manually captured before the bank feed arrived, match the imported bank transaction to the existing entry rather than creating a second payment.

### 9. Changed bank details
A supplier request to change banking details should follow the organisation's independent verification and approval process. Urgency is not a reason to bypass controls.

## Worked Example
A company receives customer card sales of R12,000. The payment processor deposits R11,700 and deducts R300 in fees.

A basic bookkeeping approach records:
- Bank R11,700 Dr
- Merchant Fees R300 Dr
- Sales/settlement account R12,000 Cr

The exact posting depends on how the sales were originally recorded, but the gross-to-net difference must be explained rather than lost.

## Practical Activity
Classify these bank entries and identify the evidence required:
1. Supplier EFT R8,500.
2. Monthly insurance debit order R2,300.
3. Card settlement R17,640 with gross sales R18,000.
4. Bank charge R185.
5. Customer EFT reversal R4,000.
6. Supplier sends new bank details with an urgent payment request.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why should a supplier payment not be posted to the expense again if the invoice was already captured?
2. What is a debit order?
3. Why can card settlements differ from gross sales?
4. What is a bank-feed duplicate risk?
5. How should a reversal be treated?
6. Why are card receipts still needed when a statement exists?
7. What should happen when supplier banking details change?
8. Why should bank charges be reviewed regularly?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. The original invoice already created the expense/asset and creditor liability.
2. A collection authorised against the bank account, often recurring.
3. Merchant or processor fees may be deducted before deposit.
4. An imported transaction may duplicate a manually captured transaction.
5. Trace it to the original entry and reverse the correct accounting effect.
6. The statement proves the charge but often not its business purpose or tax evidence.
7. Use the approved independent verification and authorisation process.
8. They may be new transactions requiring capture and reconciliation.

## Key Takeaways
- Electronic banking entries must be matched to their underlying business purpose.
- Card settlements may require gross sales and fees to be separated.
- Reversals should be traced to original transactions.
- Bank feeds improve speed but can create duplicates if matching controls are weak.
- Changed banking details require strong verification.
