# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 4: Cashbooks, Petty Cash & Bank Reconciliation
### Lesson 1: Cashbook Structure and Cash/Bank Transactions

**Estimated study time:** 50–55 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the purpose of a cashbook.
2. Distinguish cash receipts from cash payments.
3. Record common bank and cash transactions using double-entry logic.
4. Explain the relationship between the cashbook and Bank ledger account.
5. Apply basic controls to cash and bank transaction capture.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Cash and bank movements affect almost every business. The cashbook provides a structured record of money received and paid. Even where accounting software replaces a traditional manual cashbook, the same logic remains important because every receipt and payment must be classified, supported and reconciled.

## Key Terms
- **Cashbook** — Accounting record used to capture cash and bank receipts and payments.
- **Cash receipt** — Money received by the business.
- **Cash payment** — Money paid by the business.
- **Bank column** — Cashbook column used for bank-account movements.
- **Contra entry** — Transfer between cash and bank within the same business, where relevant to the system.
- **Reference** — Identifier linking a cashbook entry to supporting evidence.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. The cashbook as both journal and account
In traditional systems, the cashbook acts as a book of first entry and also provides the running bank/cash position. Modern software may record the same information through banking screens, receipts and payments modules.

### 2. Receipts
Examples include:
- customer EFT receipts;
- cash sales;
- owner capital introduced;
- loan proceeds;
- refunds received;
- interest received.

The bookkeeper must identify why money was received. A deposit into the bank is not automatically sales income.

### 3. Payments
Examples include:
- supplier payments;
- rent;
- utilities;
- wages or payroll payments;
- asset purchases;
- bank charges;
- loan repayments.

Again, the bank movement alone does not determine the correct account.

### 4. Double-entry logic
If a customer pays R6,000 toward a recorded debtor balance:
- Debit Bank R6,000.
- Credit Debtors R6,000.

If the business pays rent of R4,500:
- Debit Rent Expense R4,500.
- Credit Bank R4,500.

### 5. Cashbook columns
A manual cashbook may contain date, reference, details, bank/cash amount and analysis columns. Some systems split receipts and payments into separate books.

### 6. Supporting evidence
Every cashbook entry should be traceable to appropriate evidence such as bank statement, receipt, remittance, supplier invoice or approved payment record.

### 7. Unknown bank entries
If a bank statement shows an unexplained R12,000 deposit, do not classify it as sales merely to complete the cashbook. Investigate the source and hold it in an approved suspense/unallocated process only if policy permits.

### 8. Control points
Useful controls include:
- daily or regular bank-feed review;
- unique references;
- duplicate detection;
- approval for payments;
- restricted banking access;
- reconciliation to bank statements;
- review of unusual or high-value entries.

## Worked Example
Opening Bank balance: R25,000 Dr.

Transactions:
- Customer receipt R8,000.
- Supplier payment R5,500.
- Rent payment R4,000.
- Owner contributes R3,000.

Closing bank balance in the books:
R25,000 + R8,000 - R5,500 - R4,000 + R3,000 = **R26,500 Dr**.

The cashbook balance must later be reconciled to the bank statement.

## Practical Activity
Prepare a simple receipts/payments cashbook for these transactions:
1. Opening Bank R10,000.
2. Customer receipt R4,500.
3. Electricity paid R1,200.
4. Supplier paid R2,300.
5. Owner introduces R5,000.
6. Bank charge R180.

Calculate the closing cashbook bank balance and identify the opposite ledger account for each transaction.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. A record used to capture money received and paid.
2. It may be capital, loan proceeds, debt collection, refund or another type of receipt.
3. Debit Bank; Credit Debtors.
4. Debit Rent Expense; Credit Bank.
5. They link entries to evidence and support audit trails.
6. Investigate before classification.
7. Examples: payment approval, duplicate detection, restricted banking access, regular review and reconciliation.
8. The business records and bank records may contain timing differences or errors.

## Key Takeaways
- The cashbook controls receipts and payments.
- Bank movements must be analysed, not guessed.
- Every cashbook entry follows double-entry logic.
- Strong references and evidence support traceability.
- Reconciliation is the independent check that completes bank control.
