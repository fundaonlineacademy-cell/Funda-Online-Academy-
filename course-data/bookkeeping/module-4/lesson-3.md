# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 4: Cashbooks, Petty Cash & Bank Reconciliation
### Lesson 3: Petty Cash and the Imprest System

**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the purpose of petty cash.
2. Describe the imprest system.
3. Record and reconcile petty-cash vouchers and cash on hand.
4. Identify shortages, overages and unsupported claims.
5. Apply practical controls to a petty-cash fund.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Petty cash is designed for small, approved business expenses where normal payment methods are impractical. Because physical cash is easy to misuse or lose, the fund needs stronger evidence and reconciliation controls than its small size may suggest.

## Key Terms
- **Petty cash** — Small controlled cash fund for minor approved expenses.
- **Imprest system** — System in which the petty-cash float is restored to an approved fixed amount after valid spending is reconciled.
- **Petty-cash voucher** — Record supporting money issued from the fund.
- **Float** — Approved amount maintained in petty cash.
- **Replenishment** — Amount added to restore the fund after approved spending.
- **Shortage/overage** — Difference between expected and actual cash/evidence.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Why petty cash exists
Typical uses may include small local purchases, minor parking charges or urgent low-value office items. It should not become a way to bypass normal procurement or payment approval.

### 2. The imprest principle
Assume an approved float of R2,000. If valid vouchers total R1,250 and cash on hand is R750, the fund reconciles exactly:
R1,250 + R750 = R2,000.

The normal replenishment would be R1,250, restoring cash to R2,000.

### 3. Petty-cash vouchers
A useful voucher commonly records date, amount, purpose, recipient, supporting receipt, account classification and required approval.

### 4. Supporting evidence
Receipts or approved alternative evidence should support expenditure. A missing receipt must follow the organisation's exception process; do not fabricate evidence.

### 5. Shortages and overages
If vouchers total R1,300 and cash on hand is R650 against a R2,000 float, only R1,950 is accounted for. The unexplained R50 difference must be investigated and handled according to policy.

### 6. Replenishment
Replenishment should be based on validated spending and authorised differences, not simply the amount the custodian asks for.

### 7. Segregation and custody
The person holding cash should keep it secure and restrict access. Where possible, review or authorisation should involve another appropriate person.

### 8. Surprise counts
Periodic unannounced cash counts can strengthen control by verifying actual cash and supporting vouchers.

### 9. Prohibited practices
Avoid personal loans from petty cash, unsigned vouchers, post-dated reconstruction of evidence, splitting large purchases to avoid limits, or leaving the cash box unlocked.

## Worked Example
Float: R1,500.

Cash on hand: R420.
Valid vouchers:
- Parking R180
- Stationery R460
- Courier R440

Voucher total = R1,080.
Cash + vouchers = R1,500.
The fund reconciles and the approved replenishment is R1,080.

## Practical Activity

## Key Takeaways
- Petty cash is small in value but high in control risk.
- The imprest system links spending, evidence and replenishment.
- Cash plus valid vouchers should reconcile to the approved float.
- Differences require investigation.
- Strong custody and review controls reduce misuse.
