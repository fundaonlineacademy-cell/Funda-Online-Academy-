# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 5: Debtors, Creditors, Invoicing & Credit Control
### Lesson 1: Debtors and Creditors in the Bookkeeping System

**Estimated study time:** 50–55 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the difference between debtors and creditors.
2. Describe how credit sales and credit purchases affect the accounts.
3. Explain the relationship between control accounts and individual customer/supplier balances.
4. Identify common debtor and creditor source documents.
5. Recognise the importance of accurate allocations and reconciliations.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Businesses often buy and sell on credit. Bookkeepers must track who owes the business and whom the business owes. Weak debtor records can lead to missed collections or incorrect customer statements, while weak creditor records can lead to duplicate payments, supplier disputes or missed obligations.

## Key Terms
- **Debtor / Accounts Receivable** — Customer who owes the business money.
- **Creditor / Accounts Payable** — Supplier to whom the business owes money.
- **Credit sale** — Sale where payment will be received later.
- **Credit purchase** — Purchase where payment will be made later.
- **Control account** — General-ledger summary of detailed customer or supplier balances.
- **Subsidiary ledger** — Detailed records for individual debtors or creditors.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Credit sales create debtors
If goods or services worth R8,000 are sold on credit:
- Debit Debtors R8,000.
- Credit Sales R8,000.

The customer now owes the business R8,000.

### 2. Customer payments reduce debtors
If the customer later pays R5,000:
- Debit Bank R5,000.
- Credit Debtors R5,000.

The remaining debtor balance is R3,000.

### 3. Credit purchases create creditors
If the business receives a supplier invoice for R6,500:
- Debit the relevant expense or asset.
- Credit Creditors R6,500.

### 4. Supplier payments reduce creditors
If R4,000 is later paid to that supplier:
- Debit Creditors R4,000.
- Credit Bank R4,000.

### 5. Individual balances matter
The Debtors Control account may show R120,000, but management needs to know which customers make up that amount. Subsidiary ledgers provide this detail.

### 6. Typical debtor documents
These include:
- sales invoices;
- customer credit notes;
- receipts/remittances;
- customer statements;
- correspondence on disputes.

### 7. Typical creditor documents
These include:
- supplier invoices;
- supplier credit notes;
- payment remittances;
- supplier statements;
- purchase and receiving evidence.

### 8. Allocation accuracy
A correct receipt allocated to the wrong customer creates two problems: one customer appears overpaid and another appears overdue. Accurate allocation is as important as correct total capture.

### 9. Reconciliation
Control-account totals should agree with the sum of individual balances. Supplier statements and customer records should also be reconciled where appropriate.

## Worked Example
Opening Debtors Control: R20,000 Dr.
Credit sales: R12,000 Dr.
Customer receipts: R9,000 Cr.
Credit notes: R1,500 Cr.
Closing Debtors Control = **R21,500 Dr**.

The total of all individual customer balances should also be R21,500.

## Key Takeaways
- Debtors are amounts owed to the business; creditors are amounts owed by the business.
- Credit sales and purchases create receivable/payable balances.
- Payments reduce those balances rather than creating the original income/expense again.
- Individual allocations matter.
- Control accounts and subsidiary records should reconcile.
