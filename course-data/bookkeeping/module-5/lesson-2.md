# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 5: Debtors, Creditors, Invoicing & Credit Control
### Lesson 2: Customer Invoicing and Sales-account Control

**Estimated study time:** 50–55 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the bookkeeping purpose of a customer invoice.
2. Identify key invoice information required for accurate customer accounting.
3. Record credit sales and customer credit notes correctly.
4. Apply invoice numbering, approval and duplicate controls.
5. Reconcile invoice records to sales and debtor accounts.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Customer invoices create amounts that the business expects to collect. If invoices are missing, duplicated, allocated to the wrong customer or issued at the wrong amount, both revenue and debtor balances can become unreliable.

## Key Terms
- **Sales invoice** — Document issued to a customer for goods or services supplied.
- **Invoice number** — Unique reference identifying the invoice.
- **Credit note** — Document reducing or reversing an amount previously invoiced.
- **Customer account** — Subsidiary record showing invoices, receipts, credits and balance for one customer.
- **Cut-off** — Recording transactions in the correct accounting period.
- **Duplicate invoice** — Same sale recorded more than once.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Invoice as source evidence
A sales invoice normally identifies the customer, date, invoice number, goods/services, amounts, applicable tax information, payment terms and seller details according to business and legal requirements.

### 2. Credit sale entry
Ignoring VAT for a simple example, an invoice of R12,000 creates:
- Debit Debtors R12,000.
- Credit Sales R12,000.

### 3. Customer master data
The invoice must be linked to the correct customer. Similar names or duplicate customer accounts can create misallocation and statement problems.

### 4. Sequential numbering
Unique invoice numbers improve traceability and help identify gaps or duplicates. Cancelled invoice numbers should remain explainable rather than silently reused if the system controls them.

### 5. Pricing and approval
The bookkeeper should not invent selling prices or discounts. Use authorised price lists, quotations, contracts or approvals. Material manual changes require the relevant authority.

### 6. Credit notes
A customer credit note may be issued for approved returns, overbilling or pricing corrections. It normally reduces Debtors and Sales or the relevant income account, with tax treatment handled according to applicable rules.

### 7. Cut-off
An invoice should reflect the correct transaction period. Creating an invoice before the goods/service has genuinely been supplied, or delaying valid invoices to a later period, can distort financial reporting.

### 8. Invoice-register checks
Useful controls include:
- missing invoice numbers;
- duplicate numbers;
- unusually high values;
- manual discounts;
- invoices to inactive customers;
- credit notes without original references;
- invoice totals compared to sales reports.

### 9. Sales control reconciliation
The sum of customer invoices and credit notes should reconcile to the relevant sales and debtor-control reports for the period, allowing for system design and tax postings.

## Worked Example
Customer A is invoiced R9,200. A later approved correction reduces the invoice by R700.

Initial entry:
- Debit Debtors R9,200.
- Credit Sales R9,200.

Credit note:
- Debit Sales R700.
- Credit Debtors R700.

Net customer balance from this transaction = **R8,500**.

## Practical Activity
Create an invoice-control checklist covering customer identity, unique number, date, amount, price approval, delivery/service evidence, tax fields, payment terms and posting status. Then explain what should happen when invoice 1055 appears twice in the system.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. A debtor/receivable balance and sales income.
2. They support traceability and duplicate/gap detection.
3. A document reducing an approved amount previously billed.
4. Discounts affect revenue and customer balances and may require commercial authority.
5. Recording transactions in the correct accounting period.
6. Reusing or hiding numbers can weaken the audit trail.
7. Examples: duplicates, missing numbers, unusual amounts, unsupported credits or manual discounts.
8. Relevant sales and debtor-control records.

## Key Takeaways
- Accurate invoicing is the foundation of debtor control.
- Customer identity, amount and invoice number must be correct.
- Credit notes need authority and traceability to original transactions.
- Cut-off affects reporting quality.
- Invoice registers should be actively reviewed for exceptions.
