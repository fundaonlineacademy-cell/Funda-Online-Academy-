# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 5: Debtors, Creditors, Invoicing & Credit Control
### Lesson 7: Credit Control, Collections and Dispute Handling

**Estimated study time:** 50–55 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the purpose of credit control.
2. Use age analysis to prioritise collection follow-up.
3. Communicate professionally with customers about overdue balances.
4. Distinguish genuine disputes from simple non-payment.
5. Escalate credit-limit, write-off and legal-collection decisions appropriately.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A sale only supports cash flow when the customer pays. Credit control helps the business collect money on time while maintaining professional customer relationships. Bookkeepers may support this process by producing accurate ageing information, recording promises and identifying disputes, but major credit decisions belong to authorised roles.

## Key Terms
- **Credit control** — Processes used to manage customer credit and collect amounts due.
- **Collection follow-up** — Contact or action seeking payment of overdue balances.
- **Promise to pay** — Customer commitment to pay a stated amount by a stated date.
- **Credit limit** — Approved maximum customer exposure.
- **Dispute** — Customer challenge to an invoice, amount or service.
- **Write-off** — Authorised removal of an amount considered irrecoverable according to policy and accounting requirements.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Start from accurate records
Do not chase a customer using an unreliable statement. Confirm invoices, receipts, credit notes and prior disputes first.

### 2. Prioritise intelligently
Age, amount, customer risk, promises already made and business impact can guide follow-up. A R100,000 balance 90 days overdue may deserve more urgent attention than a small recently due amount.

### 3. Professional collection communication
State the invoice/reference, amount, due date and requested action clearly. Avoid threatening language unless formal authorised wording is required.

### 4. Record promises
If a customer promises R15,000 by Friday, record the amount, date and person who made the commitment. Follow up if the promise is missed.

### 5. Disputed invoices
When a customer disputes an invoice, identify the specific issue: price, quantity, delivery, service, duplicate billing or missing credit. Route the dispute to the responsible function and keep the amount visibly disputed rather than repeatedly treating it as ordinary collection.

### 6. Credit limits
Bookkeepers may flag customers above limits but should not extend or override limits unless authorised.

### 7. Payment plans and concessions
Extended terms, settlement discounts or instalment plans require the appropriate authority. Record the approved arrangement accurately.

### 8. Escalation
Escalate material overdue balances, repeated broken promises, significant disputes or suspected fraud through the business's credit-control process.

### 9. Write-offs
A write-off is not simply deleting a balance. It requires authorised approval and correct accounting treatment. Supporting evidence should remain traceable.

## Worked Example
Customer Z owes R32,000: R8,000 current, R12,000 30 days and R12,000 90+ days. The customer disputes R5,000 of the oldest invoice due to a pricing difference and promises to pay the remaining R7,000 overdue portion on Friday.

The bookkeeper records the dispute separately, routes it for resolution, records the R7,000 promise and follows up on Friday. The disputed R5,000 remains visible but is not treated as an ordinary broken payment promise until the dispute is resolved.

## Practical Activity
Use an age analysis with five customers and classify each as routine, follow-up, dispute, credit-limit exception or escalation. Draft one professional overdue-payment email and one internal dispute-escalation note.

## Key Takeaways
- Good credit control depends on accurate debtor records.
- Ageing helps prioritise but does not replace judgement.
- Collection communication should be factual and professional.
- Disputes need resolution workflows, not repeated generic reminders.
- Credit limits, concessions and write-offs require authority.
