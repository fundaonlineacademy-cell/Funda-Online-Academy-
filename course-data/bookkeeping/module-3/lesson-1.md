# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 3: Journals, Ledgers & Trial Balance
### Lesson 1: Journals and the Flow of Accounting Information

**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the purpose of journals in bookkeeping.
2. Distinguish routine transaction journals from the general journal.
3. Describe how information flows from source documents to journals and ledgers.
4. Identify the key fields needed in a journal entry.
5. Explain why journal references and narration support the audit trail.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A journal organises transaction information before or as it enters the ledger. In manual bookkeeping, journals reduce the need to write every transaction directly into individual ledger accounts. In computerised systems, journal logic still exists even when the software hides the traditional books behind sales, purchase and banking screens.

Understanding journals helps a bookkeeper see where accounting information comes from, how it should be grouped and why certain adjustments require a formal journal entry rather than a normal supplier or customer transaction.

## Key Terms
- **Journal** — Chronological accounting record in which transactions are first recorded or grouped before posting to ledger accounts.
- **Special journal** — Journal designed for recurring transaction types, such as sales, purchases or cash receipts.
- **General journal** — Journal commonly used for non-routine entries, corrections and adjustments.
- **Narration** — Brief explanation of why a journal entry was made.
- **Journal reference** — Identifier connecting an entry to supporting evidence and ledger posting.
- **Posting** — Transferring journal information to the relevant ledger accounts.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Why journals exist
Businesses can process hundreds or thousands of transactions. Journals provide a structured chronological record so similar transactions can be captured consistently.

Traditional journals may include:
- sales journal;
- purchases journal;
- cash receipts journal;
- cash payments journal;
- sales returns/credit-note journal;
- purchase returns/credit-note journal;
- general journal.

The exact names and design vary between systems.

### 2. Source evidence comes first
A journal entry should be supported by source evidence or an authorised accounting instruction.

For example:
- sales invoice → sales journal;
- supplier invoice → purchases journal;
- bank receipt → cash receipts journal;
- supplier EFT → cash payments journal;
- approved correction → general journal.

The journal is not the evidence itself; it records the accounting effect of evidence.

### 3. What a useful journal entry contains
Common fields include:
- date;
- journal number/reference;
- source-document reference;
- account(s) debited;
- account(s) credited;
- amounts;
- narration/explanation;
- preparer/approver where required.

A vague journal reading only “adjustment” makes later review difficult. A better narration might be `Correct supplier invoice INV-445 posted to Repairs instead of Equipment; approved correction REF-22`.

### 4. Special journals improve routine processing
If a business makes many credit sales, placing them in a sales journal allows the transactions to be grouped systematically. Customer details may update subsidiary accounts while total sales/debtors information posts to the general ledger.

The same concept applies in software: a sales invoice screen may automatically create the underlying debit and credit posting.

### 5. The general journal
The general journal is usually reserved for entries that do not fit normal transaction modules or require adjustment. Examples may include:
- correcting a misclassification;
- recording an approved accrual;
- depreciation entry;
- opening balance adjustment;
- transfer between ledger accounts.

Because manual journals can bypass normal sales/purchase workflows, access to them should be controlled.

### 6. Balanced journal entries
Every journal entry must preserve double-entry:

**Total debits = Total credits.**

An entry can involve more than two accounts. For example, a VAT-inclusive supplier transaction may debit an expense and input-VAT account while crediting creditors for the full amount.

### 7. Posting to ledgers
Once journal information is posted, each ledger account receives its part of the transaction. The ledger then accumulates balances over time.

This creates the flow:
**Source Document → Journal → Ledger → Trial Balance → Reports**

### 8. Journal controls
Useful controls include:
- sequential journal references;
- required narration;
- supporting attachments;
- approval for material/manual entries;
- restricted journal-posting rights;
- review of unusual entries;
- period controls preventing unauthorised backdating.

### 9. Corrections should be traceable
Do not erase an incorrect journal in a way that destroys the audit trail if the system provides controlled reversal/correction functionality. Use the authorised correction process so the original entry and correction remain understandable.

## Worked Example
A bookkeeper accidentally records R3,200 of office equipment as Repairs Expense. The payment itself is correct, but the account classification is wrong.

If authorised policy requires a general journal correction:
- Debit Office Equipment R3,200.
- Credit Repairs Expense R3,200.

Narration: `Reclassify equipment purchase INV-991 from Repairs Expense to Office Equipment; approved per REF-31.`

The correction does not change the bank because the cash movement was already recorded correctly.

## Practical Activity
For each item, identify the likely journal or transaction module:
1. Credit sale invoice.
2. Supplier invoice on credit.
3. Customer EFT receipt.
4. Supplier EFT payment.
5. Approved correction moving an expense from one account to another.
6. Period-end depreciation adjustment.

Then write a suitable narration for items 5 and 6.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. A chronological record used to capture or group accounting transactions.
2. Special journals handle recurring transaction types; the general journal handles non-routine entries and adjustments.
3. To prove the economic basis and authority for the entry.
4. A concise explanation of the journal purpose.
5. They must be equal.
6. Journals can directly change ledger balances and may bypass normal workflows.
7. Transferring journal effects to ledger accounts.
8. So another authorised person can understand what was changed and why.

## Key Takeaways
- Journals organise transaction information before ledger accumulation.
- Routine transactions often use specialised journals/modules.
- The general journal is powerful and should be controlled.
- Journal entries need references, support and meaningful narration.
- Debits must equal credits.
- Corrections should remain traceable.
