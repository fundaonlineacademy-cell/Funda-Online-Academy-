# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 2: Source Documents & Transaction Analysis
### Lesson 1: Source Documents and Why They Matter

**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the purpose of source documents in bookkeeping.
2. Recognise common internal and external source documents.
3. Identify the information a bookkeeper should check before capture.
4. Distinguish an original business event from the accounting record created from it.
5. Explain how source documents support authorisation, reconciliation and audit trails.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A bookkeeping entry should not begin with a guess. It should be supported by evidence showing what happened, when it happened, who was involved and how much was involved. Source documents provide that evidence.

Without reliable source documents, a bookkeeper may capture the wrong amount, duplicate a transaction, pay the wrong supplier or classify an expense incorrectly. Good source-document control therefore protects both the accuracy of the books and the business's ability to explain its transactions later.

## Key Terms
- **Source document** — Evidence created or received because a business transaction occurred.
- **Internal document** — Document created within the business, such as an approved petty-cash voucher.
- **External document** — Document received from or issued to an outside party, such as a supplier invoice.
- **Supporting evidence** — Additional information proving or explaining a transaction.
- **Reference number** — Identifier used to trace a document or transaction.
- **Authorisation** — Required approval for a transaction or action.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Source documents are the starting evidence
Examples include:
- sales invoices;
- supplier invoices;
- credit notes;
- receipts;
- EFT confirmations;
- bank statements;
- petty-cash vouchers;
- purchase orders;
- delivery notes;
- expense claims.

Different documents answer different questions. A purchase order may show what was approved, a supplier invoice shows what was billed, a delivery note may show what was received and a bank record shows what was paid.

### 2. One transaction may require several documents
A supplier payment of R14,500 may have a complete evidence chain:
1. approved purchase request;
2. supplier quotation or purchase order;
3. delivery/receipt evidence;
4. supplier invoice;
5. approval for payment;
6. bank payment confirmation;
7. accounting-system entry.

A bank payment alone proves money left the account but does not necessarily prove why the payment was valid.

### 3. Check the basics before capture
The bookkeeper should check relevant fields such as:
- document date;
- document number;
- supplier/customer name;
- amount and arithmetic;
- description of goods/services;
- approval where required;
- VAT information where relevant;
- duplicate risk;
- account or project reference.

The exact checks depend on the type of document and organisation.

### 4. Distinguish evidence from the accounting entry
An invoice is not the ledger. It is evidence used to create or support an accounting entry.

If an invoice says R6,900 but the system contains R9,600, the system entry does not become correct merely because it was saved successfully. The source and accounting record must be reconciled.

### 5. Watch for duplicates
Duplicate source documents can create duplicate expenses and payments.

Useful checks include:
- supplier name;
- invoice number;
- invoice date;
- amount;
- purchase reference;
- whether a credit note replaced the original.

A duplicate invoice number warning should be investigated, not automatically overridden.

### 6. Missing documents
A missing source document does not justify inventing one. Follow the authorised exception process.

Depending on the transaction, the business may request a copy, obtain alternate evidence, record an approved declaration or refer the matter to a manager/accountant. The bookkeeper should not create false receipts.

### 7. Source documents support reconciliation
Reconciliations often depend on source evidence. A supplier statement may show an invoice not found in the accounting system. The bookkeeper checks whether the invoice was never received, not captured, disputed or already credited.

### 8. Source documents and audit trails
A strong audit trail allows another authorised person to start from an accounting entry and find the supporting evidence—or start from the source document and find the accounting entry.

Meaningful references, consistent filing and system attachments make this easier.

### 9. Confidentiality still applies
Source documents may contain bank details, identity information, addresses and commercially sensitive prices. Store and share them through authorised systems.

## Worked Example
A business receives supplier invoice INV-804 for R5,750. Before capture, the bookkeeper checks the supplier name, invoice number, date, purchase reference, amount and evidence that the goods were received. The accounting system warns that INV-804 already exists.

The bookkeeper does not override the warning. A search shows the invoice was captured two days earlier. The second copy is filed as duplicate evidence without creating another liability.

## Practical Activity
For each transaction, list the source evidence you would expect:
1. Office stationery bought on credit.
2. Cash sale to a customer.
3. Supplier paid by EFT.
4. Employee reimbursed for approved travel expenditure.
5. Customer returns goods and receives a credit.

Then list three fields you would verify before capture.

## Key Takeaways
- Source documents are the evidence foundation of bookkeeping.
- Check documents before capture, not after errors occur.
- One transaction can have a chain of supporting evidence.
- Duplicate warnings require investigation.
- Missing evidence should use an authorised exception process.
- Strong filing makes the audit trail easier to follow.
