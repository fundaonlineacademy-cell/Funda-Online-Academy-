# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 1: Bookkeeping Foundations
### Lesson 4: The Bookkeeping Cycle: From Transaction to Financial Reports

**Estimated study time:** 45–55 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the main stages of the bookkeeping cycle.
2. Describe how source documents flow into journals, ledgers and a trial balance.
3. Explain the purpose of reconciliations and period-end checks.
4. Distinguish routine transaction processing from period-end adjustments.
5. Describe how bookkeeping records support basic financial reporting.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Bookkeeping is a process, not a collection of unrelated tasks. A transaction begins with evidence, moves through capture and classification, accumulates in ledger accounts and eventually contributes to reports used by owners, managers and accountants.

If one stage is weak, later reports can be wrong even if the software produces them without error messages. Understanding the full cycle helps a bookkeeper know what should happen next, what should be checked and where an error may have entered the system.

## Key Terms
- **Bookkeeping cycle** — Sequence of activities used to record, classify, check and report financial transactions.
- **Journal** — Book or system record in which transactions are initially grouped or recorded.
- **Ledger** — Collection of accounts showing transaction movements and balances.
- **Trial balance** — List of ledger balances used to check debit and credit equality.
- **Adjustment** — Period-end or correcting entry made to present records more accurately.
- **Closing process** — Period-end process that completes records for a reporting period.
- **Financial report** — Structured information showing financial performance, position or other accounting results.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Stage 1: A transaction occurs
The cycle begins when something financially relevant happens. Examples include:
- a sale to a customer;
- a supplier purchase;
- a bank payment;
- a cash receipt;
- a payroll transaction;
- an asset purchase;
- an owner contribution.

The bookkeeper first needs evidence and context. A number in a bank statement alone may not explain what the payment was for.

### 2. Stage 2: Source documents are collected and checked
The source document supports the transaction. Examples include invoices, receipts, credit notes, payment confirmations, purchase orders and bank statements.

Before capture, the bookkeeper should check relevant details such as:
- date;
- supplier or customer;
- amount;
- reference;
- approval;
- tax information where applicable;
- whether the document appears complete.

An incorrect source document should not be blindly captured merely because it looks official.

### 3. Stage 3: The transaction is analysed
The bookkeeper identifies which accounts are affected and how.

For example, if a business buys office stationery for R1,200 cash:
- stationery expense increases;
- bank decreases.

If the business buys inventory for R9,000 on credit:
- inventory or purchases increases according to the system used;
- creditors increases.

This analysis determines the double-entry.

### 4. Stage 4: The transaction is recorded
In a manual system, transactions may first be entered into appropriate journals or cashbooks. In computerised accounting software, the user may enter the transaction through a sales, purchase, banking or journal screen.

The software interface changes, but the accounting logic does not. The system still needs to identify the accounts affected.

### 5. Stage 5: Transactions accumulate in ledgers
Ledger accounts collect related entries over time.

For example:
- the Bank account accumulates receipts and payments;
- the Rent Expense account accumulates rent charges;
- a customer account accumulates invoices, credit notes and receipts;
- a supplier account accumulates supplier invoices, credits and payments.

The ledger therefore shows both movement and balance.

### 6. Stage 6: Reconciliations are performed
Reconciliation compares records that should relate to each other.

Common examples include:
- cashbook to bank statement;
- debtors control account to customer balances;
- creditors control account to supplier balances;
- payroll control totals to payment records.

A reconciliation difference should be investigated. It should not be forced to zero merely to make the report look correct.

### 7. Stage 7: A trial balance is reviewed
The trial balance lists ledger balances. In a double-entry system, total debits should equal total credits.

A balanced trial balance is important, but it does not prove that every transaction is correct. A transaction posted to the wrong expense account could still keep the trial balance balanced.

The trial balance is therefore a control check, not a guarantee of perfect records.

### 8. Stage 8: Adjustments and period-end checks
Before reports are finalised, the records may need adjustments. Examples can include:
- accruals;
- prepayments;
- depreciation;
- bad-debt adjustments;
- corrections of mispostings.

Some adjustments may require accountant or manager input depending on the business and the bookkeeper's role.

### 9. Stage 9: Reports are prepared
Once records are sufficiently complete and checked, financial reports can be produced. At an introductory level, these may include:
- income statement / statement of profit or loss;
- balance sheet / statement of financial position;
- debtor and creditor age analyses;
- cash-flow or cash-position reports;
- management schedules.

Reports are only as reliable as the records underneath them.

### 10. Stage 10: Records are filed and carried forward
The period does not simply “end”. Supporting evidence, reconciliations, approvals and reports should be retained according to the organisation's recordkeeping requirements.

Opening balances for the next period must connect correctly to the closing balances from the prior period.

## Worked Example
A cleaning-services business completes a job for R6,000 on credit.

The cycle may look like this:
1. Approved customer invoice is created.
2. Invoice details are checked.
3. Transaction is analysed: Debtors increases; Sales/Service Income increases.
4. Transaction is captured.
5. Customer and income ledgers update.
6. Debtors records are later reconciled.
7. Ledger balances feed into the trial balance.
8. Period-end checks confirm outstanding customer balances.
9. Financial reports include the income and debtor balance.
10. Invoice and related records remain filed and traceable.

When the customer later pays, that is a new transaction in the cycle: Bank increases and Debtors decreases.

## Key Takeaways
- Bookkeeping follows a controlled cycle from evidence to reports.
- Capture is only one stage; analysis and reconciliation are equally important.
- A trial balance is a valuable control but not proof of perfect accuracy.
- Period-end adjustments improve the completeness of reports.
- Reliable reports depend on reliable transaction records and supporting evidence.
