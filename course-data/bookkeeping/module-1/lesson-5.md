# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 1: Bookkeeping Foundations
### Lesson 5: Ethics, Accuracy, Confidentiality & Professional Conduct

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain why ethical conduct is essential in bookkeeping.
2. Apply practical confidentiality and integrity principles.
3. Distinguish an honest correction from deliberate manipulation.
4. Recognise conflicts of interest and inappropriate pressure.
5. Escalate suspected fraud, misconduct or material errors through authorised channels.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A bookkeeper works close to money, records, banking information, payroll data and business performance. That access creates responsibility. Even technically accurate bookkeeping can become unreliable if the person processing the records hides errors, changes documents without authority, shares confidential information or allows pressure to override professional judgement.

Ethics in bookkeeping is therefore practical. It affects how you respond when a manager asks you to backdate a document, when you discover a duplicate payment, when a friend asks about another employee's salary or when a transaction does not make sense.

## Key Terms
- **Integrity** — Acting honestly and consistently with professional and organisational standards.
- **Confidentiality** — Protecting information from unnecessary or unauthorised disclosure.
- **Objectivity** — Avoiding bias, personal interest or pressure that distorts judgement.
- **Conflict of interest** — Situation where personal interests could interfere with professional responsibilities.
- **Professional competence** — Performing only work you are sufficiently trained and authorised to perform.
- **Fraud** — Intentional deception for unlawful or improper gain.
- **Error** — Unintentional mistake in processing or recording information.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Integrity means records must reflect reality
A bookkeeping record should represent what actually happened. Changing a transaction simply to make a report look better undermines the reliability of the books.

Examples of unethical conduct include:
- creating a false supplier invoice;
- hiding a cash shortage;
- changing a date to move an expense into another period without proper basis;
- inventing an approval;
- deleting evidence to conceal an error;
- knowingly capturing a personal expense as a business expense.

A bookkeeper should never make a false entry because someone senior requested it.

### 2. Errors must be corrected transparently
Mistakes happen. Professional conduct is shown by how they are handled.

A good correction process normally includes:
- identifying the incorrect entry;
- confirming the correct information;
- following the approved correction method;
- retaining an audit trail where required;
- notifying or obtaining approval from the appropriate person if the error is material.

The objective is not to hide the mistake but to restore accurate records.

### 3. Confidentiality is wider than passwords
Bookkeepers may see:
- employee pay information;
- customer account balances;
- supplier bank details;
- cash-flow problems;
- profitability information;
- personal identity and contact information.

Confidentiality means using this information only for legitimate work purposes and sharing it only with authorised people.

Do not:
- discuss payroll in public areas;
- send finance files to personal email accounts without authorisation;
- leave bank details visible on an unattended screen;
- share login credentials;
- give a colleague access merely because they are curious.

### 4. Objectivity under pressure
Bookkeepers can face pressure to “just process it”. A supplier payment may be urgent, a manager may want a report immediately or an owner may insist that an unsupported transaction is legitimate.

Urgency does not remove the need for controls.

If a payment change normally requires independent verification, follow that process. If an approval is required, obtain it. If an amount cannot be supported, escalate rather than invent evidence.

### 5. Conflicts of interest
A conflict may arise when the bookkeeper has a personal relationship or financial interest connected to a transaction.

Example: the bookkeeper's relative owns a supplier being considered for payment. The relationship does not automatically mean wrongdoing, but it should be disclosed according to policy if it could affect impartial processing or approval.

The bookkeeper should not use their role to favour personal interests.

### 6. Competence and role boundaries
Professional conduct includes knowing when not to guess.

A bookkeeper may be competent to capture transactions and prepare reconciliations but not authorised to decide a complex tax treatment or prepare specialist accounting adjustments independently.

Useful professional language is:
> “I can prepare the supporting records, but this treatment needs confirmation from the authorised accountant or tax practitioner.”

### 7. Fraud awareness without becoming an investigator
Bookkeepers are often well placed to notice unusual patterns, but they should follow organisational escalation procedures rather than conduct risky investigations on their own.

Warning signs may include:
- duplicated supplier bank accounts;
- unexplained changes to payment details;
- missing supporting documents;
- repeated manual overrides;
- unusual round-number payments;
- payments just below approval thresholds;
- transactions involving unknown suppliers.

A warning sign is not proof of fraud. It is a reason to verify and escalate appropriately.

### 8. Protecting the audit trail
Individual user accounts, controlled corrections and retained approval evidence help preserve accountability.

Shared passwords weaken the audit trail because the system can no longer reliably show who performed an action.

## Worked Example
A bookkeeper receives an email appearing to come from a supplier asking that future payments be made to a new bank account. The email says payment is urgent and gives new details.

A poor response is to change the banking information immediately because the supplier seems familiar.

A professional response is to follow the organisation's approved supplier-detail verification process, independently confirm the change through an authorised channel and retain evidence of the verification. If anything appears suspicious, escalate to the appropriate manager or finance/security function.

## Practical Activity
For each situation, decide whether it is mainly an error, ethical issue, confidentiality issue, conflict of interest, control weakness or possible fraud indicator:
1. A supplier invoice is accidentally captured twice.
2. A manager asks you to delete an expense because “the profit looks too low”.
3. A colleague asks you what another employee earns.
4. Your cousin owns a supplier and you are asked to prepare the supplier payment batch.
5. A vendor changes banking details one hour before payment.

Write the safest professional response for each.

## Key Takeaways
- Ethical bookkeeping protects the reliability of financial records.
- Honest errors should be corrected, not hidden.
- Confidentiality applies to both financial and personal information.
- Pressure from senior people does not make false entries acceptable.
- Fraud indicators require controlled verification and escalation, not assumptions.
- Professional competence includes knowing your limits.
