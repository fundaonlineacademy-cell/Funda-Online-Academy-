# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 1: Bookkeeping Foundations
### Lesson 3: Business Types, Owners' Equity & Bookkeeping Implications

**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Recognise common business forms used in South Africa.
2. Explain how the owner's relationship to the business affects bookkeeping records.
3. Distinguish capital introduced, drawings and business expenses.
4. Explain why business and personal transactions should be separated.
5. Identify when legal, tax or accounting questions must be referred to an authorised specialist.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Bookkeeping does not happen in isolation. The way a business is organised affects how owners contribute money, withdraw value, approve transactions and interpret equity. A sole proprietor, partnership and company are not identical from a legal or accounting perspective.

A bookkeeper does not need to act as a company-law or tax adviser, but they must understand enough to record transactions correctly and avoid mixing personal and business activity.

## Key Terms
- **Sole proprietor** — Individual who operates a business in their own capacity.
- **Partnership** — Business arrangement in which two or more partners conduct business together under agreed terms.
- **Company** — Separate legal entity incorporated under company law.
- **Capital contribution** — Value introduced into the business by an owner or shareholder according to the relevant structure.
- **Drawings** — Value withdrawn by an owner from an unincorporated business for personal use.
- **Share capital / contributed equity** — Equity introduced to a company according to its legal structure.
- **Owner's current account / loan account** — Account that may track amounts owed between an owner or related party and the business depending on the structure and accounting arrangements.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Why business structure matters to a bookkeeper
The same bank payment can require different treatment depending on what it represents. A transfer from an owner's personal account into a sole proprietorship may represent capital introduced. A transfer into a company bank account may instead be share capital, a shareholder loan or another authorised transaction.

The bookkeeper must therefore understand the business context before choosing an account.

### 2. Sole proprietorships
In a sole proprietorship, the owner and business are closely connected legally, but bookkeeping should still separate business activity from personal activity.

For bookkeeping purposes, the owner may introduce money or assets as capital. If the owner takes business money for personal use, that withdrawal is normally recorded as drawings rather than a normal business expense.

Example: the owner withdraws R2,000 from the business bank account to pay for private groceries. This should not be recorded as “office expenses”. It is a personal withdrawal from the business.

### 3. Partnerships
A partnership may have more than one partner contributing capital, sharing profits and making authorised withdrawals. The bookkeeping system needs enough detail to identify which partner's equity or current account is affected.

A partnership agreement may determine responsibilities, profit-sharing arrangements and authority. The bookkeeper should follow approved instructions rather than assume that every partner has identical rights to withdraw or approve funds.

### 4. Companies
A company is a separate legal entity. The company's money is not automatically the personal money of its directors or shareholders.

Payments made by or to directors and shareholders may require careful classification. Depending on the facts and authorised accounting treatment, an amount might be salary, reimbursement, dividend, shareholder loan movement, capital contribution or another type of transaction.

A bookkeeper should not guess. Where the correct treatment depends on legal, tax or accounting judgement, the matter should be referred to the accountant, tax practitioner or authorised finance manager.

### 5. Separate business and personal records
Even in a very small business, mixing personal and business transactions creates problems. It can make bank reconciliation harder, distort expenses and reduce the reliability of financial reports.

Good practice includes:
- using a dedicated business bank account where appropriate;
- keeping business invoices and receipts separate from personal documents;
- recording owner contributions and withdrawals clearly;
- avoiding personal purchases through business accounts unless properly authorised and classified;
- documenting reimbursements.

### 6. Capital versus income
Capital introduced is not sales income. If an owner deposits R40,000 to start the business, the bank increases and the owner's equity increases. The business has not earned R40,000 from customers.

Similarly, a shareholder loan received by a company is not automatically revenue. It may create an amount owed to the shareholder.

### 7. Drawings versus expenses
A business expense is incurred for the purpose of business operations. Drawings are personal value taken by an owner from an unincorporated business.

Incorrectly recording drawings as expenses can understate profit and produce misleading reports.

### 8. Reimbursements and supporting evidence
Sometimes an owner or employee pays a genuine business cost personally and then claims reimbursement. The bookkeeper should confirm the business purpose, supporting document, approval and correct account before processing.

The fact that a person is an owner does not remove the need for evidence and control.

### 9. Authority still matters
The business structure does not answer every approval question. A director may be legally important but still be subject to internal approval limits. A partner may have agreed authority limits. A finance policy may require two approvals for certain payments.

The bookkeeper should follow both the accounting logic and the approved operational controls.

## Worked Example
Thando operates a small design business as a sole proprietor. During the month:
- she deposits R25,000 of personal savings into the business bank account;
- the business earns R12,000 from clients;
- she withdraws R1,500 for personal use;
- she buys printer ink for R900 for the business.

Correct classification:
- R25,000: capital introduced, not income;
- R12,000: business income;
- R1,500: drawings, not office expense;
- R900: business expense, supported by the relevant document.

## Key Takeaways
- Business structure influences how owner-related transactions are recorded.
- Capital, income, drawings and expenses are not interchangeable.
- Company funds are not simply personal funds of directors or shareholders.
- Personal and business records should be clearly separated.
- Uncertain owner, shareholder or partner transactions should be clarified rather than guessed.
