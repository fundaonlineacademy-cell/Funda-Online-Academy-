# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 1: Bookkeeping Foundations
### Lesson 2: The Accounting Equation & Double-Entry Principles

**Estimated study time:** 50–60 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the accounting equation.
2. Distinguish assets, liabilities and owners' equity.
3. Explain why every transaction has at least two accounting effects.
4. Apply basic debit and credit logic to common transactions.
5. Check whether a simple transaction keeps the accounting equation in balance.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Double-entry bookkeeping is the core logic behind modern accounting systems. Whether a business uses a handwritten ledger, spreadsheet, QuickBooks or another accounting package, the same principle applies: every transaction affects at least two parts of the financial records.

If this logic is understood, bookkeeping becomes more than memorising which button to click. You can reason through transactions, spot incorrect postings and understand why a trial balance should balance.

## Key Terms
- **Asset** — Resource controlled by the business from which future economic benefit is expected.
- **Liability** — Present obligation of the business to another party.
- **Owners' equity** — The owner's residual interest in the business after liabilities are deducted from assets.
- **Debit** — Left side of an account.
- **Credit** — Right side of an account.
- **Double-entry** — System in which every transaction has equal debit and credit effects.
- **Accounting equation** — Assets = Liabilities + Owners' Equity.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. The accounting equation
The basic accounting equation is:

**Assets = Liabilities + Owners' Equity**

This equation shows where the resources of the business came from. Assets are financed either by amounts owed to outside parties or by the owners' interest in the business.

Suppose a new business begins when its owner deposits R50,000 into the business bank account. The business now has a bank asset of R50,000 and owners' equity of R50,000.

Assets = R50,000  
Liabilities = R0  
Owners' Equity = R50,000

The equation balances.

### 2. Transactions change the equation
Now assume the business buys a laptop for R12,000 cash. One asset decreases because bank falls by R12,000, while another asset increases because equipment rises by R12,000.

The total assets remain unchanged. The transaction affects two accounts even though no liability or equity changed.

If instead the laptop is bought on credit, equipment increases by R12,000 and a supplier liability increases by R12,000.

### 3. Income and expenses affect equity
Income normally increases owners' equity because it increases the value generated by the business. Expenses normally reduce owners' equity because resources are consumed in earning income.

For example, if a business earns R5,000 cash from services:
- Bank increases by R5,000.
- Income increases by R5,000.
- The income ultimately increases equity.

If the business then pays R1,200 for electricity:
- Bank decreases by R1,200.
- Electricity expense increases by R1,200.
- The expense ultimately reduces equity.

### 4. Debit and credit logic
Debit does not automatically mean “bad” and credit does not automatically mean “good”. They are simply the left and right sides of an account.

A useful introductory guide is:

| Account type | Increase | Decrease |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assets | Debit | Credit |
| Expenses | Debit | Credit |
| Liabilities | Credit | Debit |
| Owners' equity | Credit | Debit |
| Income | Credit | Debit |

This guide should be understood rather than memorised mechanically.

### 5. Applying double-entry
Consider a business purchasing stock for R8,000 cash.

If the stock is recorded as inventory:
- Debit Inventory R8,000 because the asset increases.
- Credit Bank R8,000 because the bank asset decreases.

Total debits = R8,000. Total credits = R8,000.

Now consider a customer paying R6,500 that was previously owed:
- Debit Bank R6,500 because bank increases.
- Credit Debtors / Accounts Receivable R6,500 because the customer asset decreases.

The customer payment is not new income if the sale was already recorded earlier. It is the settlement of an existing debtor balance.

### 6. A common beginner mistake: confusing cash with income
Not every receipt is income and not every payment is an expense.

Examples:
- Money borrowed from a bank increases cash but also creates a liability. It is not sales income.
- Buying equipment uses cash but creates an asset. The full purchase is not automatically an expense at the moment of purchase.
- Receiving payment from a debtor increases bank but decreases debtors. It does not record the sale again.

Understanding the accounting equation helps prevent these errors.

### 7. Another common mistake: treating debits as negative
A debit can increase an asset or expense. A credit can increase income, equity or a liability. The meaning depends on the type of account.

The correct question is therefore not “Is this debit good or bad?” but “What type of account is this, and did it increase or decrease?”

## Worked Example
A small printing business has the following transactions:
1. Owner deposits R30,000 into the business bank account.
2. Business buys equipment for R10,000 cash.
3. Business buys printing supplies for R4,000 on credit.
4. Business earns R7,500 cash from customers.
5. Business pays R1,000 to the supplier.

Effects:
1. Bank +R30,000; Equity +R30,000.
2. Equipment +R10,000; Bank -R10,000.
3. Supplies/Inventory +R4,000; Creditors +R4,000.
4. Bank +R7,500; Income +R7,500.
5. Creditors -R1,000; Bank -R1,000.

Every transaction has at least two effects, and the accounting equation remains balanced.

## Key Takeaways
- Every transaction has at least two accounting effects.
- The accounting equation must remain balanced.
- Debits and credits are account sides, not positive and negative labels.
- Assets and expenses normally increase with debits.
- Liabilities, income and owners' equity normally increase with credits.
- Understanding transaction substance prevents duplicate or incorrect income and expense entries.
