# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 1: Bookkeeping Foundations
### Lesson 7: Designing a Practical Chart of Accounts

**Estimated study time:** 45–55 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the purpose of a chart of accounts.
2. Group accounts into assets, liabilities, equity, income and expenses.
3. Design practical account names and codes for a small business.
4. Avoid common problems such as excessive detail, duplicate accounts and mixed classifications.
5. Explain how a well-designed chart of accounts improves reporting and control.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A bookkeeping system needs an organised structure. The chart of accounts is the list of accounts used to classify transactions. If the structure is clear, similar transactions are recorded consistently and reports are easier to understand. If it is badly designed, the same type of cost may be spread across several accounts, important information may be hidden and year-end cleanup becomes more difficult.

The aim is not to create the largest possible list of accounts. A practical chart of accounts gives enough detail for useful management and reporting without making routine bookkeeping unnecessarily complicated.

## Key Terms
- **Chart of accounts** — Organised list of accounts available in the bookkeeping system.
- **Account code** — Number or alphanumeric reference used to identify an account.
- **General ledger account** — Account that accumulates transactions of a particular type.
- **Control account** — Summary ledger account linked to detailed subsidiary records, such as debtors or creditors.
- **Subaccount** — More detailed account grouped beneath a broader category where the system supports it.
- **Natural account** — Account identifying the nature of a transaction, such as Rent, Electricity or Salaries.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Start with the accounting equation
A chart of accounts normally groups accounts according to the main financial-statement categories:
- Assets
- Liabilities
- Owners' Equity
- Income
- Expenses

These categories reflect the accounting equation and the way financial reports are prepared.

A small service business might use accounts such as:

**Assets:** Bank, Petty Cash, Debtors, Office Equipment.  
**Liabilities:** Creditors, Loan Payable.  
**Equity:** Owner's Capital, Drawings where appropriate.  
**Income:** Service Revenue.  
**Expenses:** Rent, Electricity, Telephone, Stationery, Wages, Bank Charges.

### 2. Design for the actual business
The chart should reflect what the business does.

A salon may need Hair Product Sales and Treatment Income. A construction business may need Materials, Subcontractors and Equipment Hire. An online retailer may need Product Sales, Courier Costs and Payment Gateway Fees.

Avoid copying a huge generic chart without considering whether those accounts are useful to the business.

### 3. Use clear account names
An account name should tell a competent user what belongs there.

Weak names include:
- General
- Other Stuff
- Miscellaneous 2
- Costs

Better names include:
- Courier Expense
- Merchant Fees
- Office Stationery
- Repairs & Maintenance

A “Miscellaneous” account may occasionally be necessary, but frequent use can hide poor classification.

### 4. Use numbering consistently
Many businesses use account codes. One possible internal structure might reserve number ranges for different account types, for example:
- 1000–1999 Assets
- 2000–2999 Liabilities
- 3000–3999 Equity
- 4000–4999 Income
- 5000–6999 Expenses

This is an example, not a universal legal rule. The organisation may use a different numbering system. Consistency matters more than copying a particular sequence.

Leave reasonable gaps in a numbering structure so new accounts can be added later without renumbering everything.

### 5. Avoid duplicate accounts
Duplicate accounts create inconsistent data.

Suppose one bookkeeper records courier charges under `Delivery Expense`, another uses `Courier Fees`, and a third uses `Transport`. Management can no longer see the real total without combining several accounts.

Before creating a new account:
1. search the existing chart;
2. check whether a suitable account already exists;
3. confirm the correct classification;
4. obtain approval if the organisation controls account creation.

### 6. Do not create excessive detail
Too little detail makes reports vague, but too much detail creates clutter.

For example, it may be unnecessary for a small office to create separate accounts for blue pens, black pens, printer paper and envelopes. A single `Office Stationery` account may provide enough information.

Create separate accounts when the distinction is genuinely useful for reporting, control, tax treatment or management decisions.

### 7. Separate balance-sheet and income-statement accounts
A common bookkeeping error is to confuse an asset with an expense or a liability with income.

Examples:
- Bank is an asset, not income.
- A bank loan is a liability, not sales revenue.
- Equipment may be an asset rather than an immediate ordinary expense depending on the business's accounting policy.
- Customer money owed is a debtor asset.

Correct account classification supports correct reporting.

### 8. Use control accounts appropriately
Debtors and creditors often use control accounts in the general ledger with detailed customer or supplier balances in subsidiary records.

The total of customer balances should reconcile to the debtors control account. The total of supplier balances should reconcile to the creditors control account.

Do not create a separate general-ledger income account for every customer merely because customers are different. Customer detail normally belongs in the customer ledger or subledger.

### 9. Protect the chart of accounts from uncontrolled changes
Creating, renaming, merging or deactivating accounts can affect historical reports and integrations. Changes should therefore follow authorised procedures.

Before changing an account, consider:
- existing transactions;
- reports that use the account;
- opening balances;
- VAT/tax mappings where relevant;
- payroll or software integrations;
- whether the account should be deactivated rather than deleted.

### 10. Review the chart periodically
As a business changes, some accounts become unnecessary and new categories become important. Periodic review helps keep the chart useful without constantly redesigning it.

## Worked Example
A small digital-marketing agency currently uses these expense accounts:
- Advertising
- Ads
- Facebook Ads
- Online Advertising
- Paid Media

Most of these are being used for the same business purpose. Management cannot quickly see the total paid-media spend.

A controlled review may decide to use one primary account called `Paid Advertising Expense`, with project or campaign detail captured elsewhere if needed. Existing balances should be handled through the authorised accounting process rather than simply deleting historical accounts.

## Practical Activity
Design a basic chart of accounts for a small South African office-cleaning business. Include at least:
- 4 assets;
- 3 liabilities/equity accounts;
- 3 income accounts;
- 8 expense accounts.

Give each account a clear name and optional code. Then explain why you did not create a separate account for every customer, supplier or small item purchased.

## Key Takeaways
- The chart of accounts is the classification backbone of the bookkeeping system.
- Account names should be clear, consistent and useful.
- Avoid duplicate or unnecessary accounts.
- Use account codes systematically, not randomly.
- Separate detailed customer/supplier records from general-ledger categories.
- Changes to the chart should be authorised and controlled.
