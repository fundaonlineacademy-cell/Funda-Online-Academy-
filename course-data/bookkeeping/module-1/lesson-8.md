# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 1: Bookkeeping Foundations
### Lesson 8: Internal Controls, Error Prevention & Fraud Awareness

**Estimated study time:** 50–55 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the purpose of internal controls in bookkeeping.
2. Apply practical preventive, detective and corrective controls.
3. Describe segregation of duties and approval controls.
4. Recognise common bookkeeping errors and fraud warning signs.
5. Escalate unusual transactions without making unsupported accusations.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Bookkeeping systems deal with cash, payments, customer balances, supplier details and sensitive financial records. Even honest staff can make mistakes, and dishonest activity can occur when controls are weak. Internal controls reduce these risks by making important steps visible, authorised and reviewable.

A strong control environment does not assume that every employee is dishonest. It creates reliable processes so that mistakes are prevented where possible, detected quickly when they occur and corrected through an audit trail.

## Key Terms
- **Internal control** — Process or procedure designed to protect assets, improve record reliability and support authorised operations.
- **Preventive control** — Control intended to stop an error or unauthorised action before it occurs.
- **Detective control** — Control intended to identify an error or unusual activity after or while it occurs.
- **Corrective control** — Action used to fix an identified problem and reduce recurrence.
- **Segregation of duties** — Separating incompatible responsibilities among different people where practical.
- **Authorisation** — Approved permission for a transaction or action.
- **Fraud indicator** — Unusual fact or pattern that may justify further verification but is not proof of fraud.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. What internal controls are designed to protect
Bookkeeping controls help an organisation protect:
- cash and bank accounts;
- inventory and equipment;
- supplier and customer information;
- payroll information;
- the accuracy of accounting records;
- approval authority;
- evidence supporting transactions.

Controls should be proportionate to the size and risk of the business. A small business may not have enough staff for perfect segregation, but it can still introduce compensating reviews and approvals.

### 2. Preventive controls
Preventive controls reduce the chance that a problem occurs.

Examples include:
- requiring approval before payment;
- restricting access to banking systems;
- verifying supplier bank-detail changes independently;
- using unique user accounts and strong authentication;
- limiting who can create suppliers or change master data;
- using approved document sequences;
- training staff on transaction procedures.

A payment limit is useful only if the system or process actually enforces it.

### 3. Detective controls
Detective controls identify problems that were not prevented.

Examples include:
- bank reconciliation;
- debtor and creditor reconciliation;
- review of duplicate invoice numbers;
- exception reports;
- comparing payment batches to approved supporting documents;
- reviewing unusual journal entries;
- physical cash counts;
- management review of budget-versus-actual results.

A reconciliation is therefore more than a bookkeeping exercise. It is also an internal control.

### 4. Corrective controls
Once a problem is found, the organisation needs a controlled response.

Corrective actions may include:
- reversing or correcting an entry;
- recovering an overpayment;
- changing system access;
- updating a procedure;
- retraining staff;
- strengthening approval or verification steps.

The correction should preserve evidence rather than erase the history of what happened.

### 5. Segregation of duties
A useful control principle is to avoid allowing one person to control an entire high-risk transaction from beginning to end.

For example, where practical, different people may be responsible for:
1. creating or changing a supplier;
2. approving a supplier invoice;
3. preparing the payment;
4. authorising the payment;
5. reconciling the bank account.

This makes it harder for one person to create a false supplier, approve an invoice, pay it and then hide the transaction.

Small businesses may have limited staff. In that case, an owner or external accountant might independently review bank statements, supplier changes or reconciliations as a compensating control.

### 6. Common bookkeeping errors
Frequent errors include:
- duplicate capture;
- transposed digits;
- wrong account allocation;
- wrong customer or supplier;
- incorrect date or period;
- omitted transaction;
- incorrect VAT coding;
- posting a receipt twice;
- failing to match a credit note.

Useful error-prevention methods include clear source references, system validation, checklists, review and regular reconciliation.

### 7. Fraud awareness
Fraud involves intentional deception. A bookkeeper may notice unusual activity but should not label a person as fraudulent without evidence and authority.

Warning signs can include:
- sudden supplier bank-detail changes;
- multiple suppliers using the same bank account;
- repeated payments just below approval limits;
- invoices without purchase evidence;
- duplicated or altered supporting documents;
- unusual manual journal entries;
- unexplained cash shortages;
- payments to unfamiliar suppliers;
- pressure to bypass normal controls.

The correct response is usually to stop where authorised, preserve evidence, verify facts and escalate through the proper channel.

### 8. Supplier master-data controls
Supplier details are especially sensitive because changing bank information can redirect payments.

A strong process may require:
- documented change request;
- independent verification using a trusted contact route;
- approval by an authorised person;
- change log;
- review before the next payment.

Do not rely only on the email requesting the change to verify itself.

### 9. Cash and petty-cash controls
Cash is vulnerable because it can be physically removed.

Controls may include:
- limited float amount;
- numbered vouchers;
- receipts for expenditure;
- approval limits;
- periodic cash counts;
- reconciliation to the imprest balance;
- secure storage.

A shortage should be recorded and investigated through the approved process, not silently covered by changing the records.

### 10. Access and audit trails
Every user should work through their own authorised account. Shared credentials weaken accountability and may expose the organisation to unauthorised changes.

System permissions should match job responsibilities. Someone who only prepares transactions may not need authority to approve them.

## Worked Example
A bookkeeper prepares a supplier payment batch of R86,000. One invoice for R18,500 has a new bank account that was changed earlier that morning after an email request.

A weak process simply pays the new account.

A stronger process flags the changed master data, checks the supplier-change evidence, independently confirms the new bank account through an authorised contact method, obtains the required approval and records the verification before payment release.

The control protects both the supplier and the business.

## Key Takeaways
- Internal controls protect both money and the reliability of financial records.
- Preventive, detective and corrective controls work together.
- Segregation of duties reduces transaction-control risk.
- Reconciliations are important detective controls.
- Fraud warning signs require verification and escalation, not unsupported accusations.
- Corrections should preserve the audit trail.
