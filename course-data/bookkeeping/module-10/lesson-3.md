# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 10: Practical Bookkeeping Project
### Lesson 3: Process Source Documents and Routine Transactions

**Estimated study time:** 55–65 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Process a mixed batch of simulated source documents.
2. Analyse each transaction before capture.
3. Apply duplicate, approval and classification checks.
4. Record customer, supplier, cash and bank transactions consistently.
5. Maintain a clear evidence trail for routine bookkeeping entries.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Real bookkeeping rarely arrives in neat topic-by-topic order. A daily batch may contain supplier invoices, customer receipts, bank charges, card purchases, petty-cash vouchers and credit notes. The bookkeeper must identify what each document represents and route it to the correct accounting treatment without losing control of the evidence.

## Key Terms
- **Batch** — Group of transactions processed together.
- **Transaction analysis** — Determining what happened economically and which accounts are affected.
- **Duplicate check** — Review to prevent the same transaction being captured more than once.
- **Allocation** — Linking a transaction to the correct customer, supplier, account or reference.
- **Exception** — Item that cannot be processed normally and requires investigation or approval.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Prepare the batch
Number or reference each source document. Update the evidence index before or during processing. Confirm that documents are legible and contain enough information to analyse.

### 2. Analyse before capture
For every document ask:
- What happened?
- Which party is involved?
- Is this cash, credit or another settlement method?
- Which accounts increase or decrease?
- Is VAT treatment supplied or clear within the simulation?
- Is approval required?
- Could this be a duplicate?

### 3. Credit sales
A supported credit sale generally increases Debtors and Sales. If project VAT applies, split the transaction according to the supplied VAT assumptions.

Do not record a customer receipt as new sales if the original invoice already created revenue.

### 4. Customer receipts
Allocate receipts to the correct customer and invoice where possible. Unidentified receipts should remain visible as exceptions rather than being guessed into a customer account.

### 5. Supplier invoices
A valid supplier invoice on credit generally increases an expense or asset and increases Creditors. Check supplier name, invoice number, date, amount, description, approval and duplicate risk.

### 6. Supplier payments
If the supplier invoice was already recorded, the payment normally reduces Creditors and Bank. Do not create the expense again.

### 7. Cash and petty cash
Petty-cash entries require valid evidence and should reduce the petty-cash balance. Maintain the imprest or project control and investigate differences rather than silently adjusting them.

### 8. Bank charges and electronic items
Bank charges appearing on the bank statement may need to be added to the cashbook if not already recorded. Use the bank statement as evidence and classify the charge appropriately.

### 9. Credit notes
A customer or supplier credit note reverses or reduces part of an earlier transaction. Link it to the correct account and original transaction where practical.

### 10. Exception control
Examples include:
- missing invoice;
- duplicate document number;
- amount mismatch;
- unclear account classification;
- unapproved expense;
- unidentified receipt;
- changed supplier banking details.

Record the exception, owner, next action and deadline.

## Worked Example
The batch contains:
- Customer Invoice C101: R6,900;
- Customer EFT receipt: R4,000 against C101;
- Supplier Invoice S445: R2,300 for office consumables;
- Supplier EFT payment: R1,800 against an existing balance;
- Bank charge: R115.

Ignoring VAT for this example:
- C101: Dr Debtors R6,900 / Cr Sales R6,900.
- Receipt: Dr Bank R4,000 / Cr Debtors R4,000.
- Supplier invoice: Dr Consumables R2,300 / Cr Creditors R2,300.
- Supplier payment: Dr Creditors R1,800 / Cr Bank R1,800.
- Bank charge: Dr Bank Charges R115 / Cr Bank R115.

Every entry is referenced back to evidence.

## Practical Activity
Process a fictional batch of at least 15 transactions containing:
- 3 customer invoices;
- 3 customer receipts;
- 3 supplier invoices;
- 2 supplier payments;
- 2 petty-cash items;
- 1 bank charge;
- 1 credit note.

Create an exception register for at least two deliberately incomplete items. Do not resolve them by guessing.

## Knowledge Check
1. What questions should be asked before capture?
2. How is a customer receipt different from a sale?
3. Why should duplicate invoice numbers be investigated?
4. What happens to Creditors when an already-recorded supplier invoice is paid?
5. How should unidentified receipts be treated?
6. Why should bank charges be reviewed against the cashbook?
7. What is the role of a credit note?
8. What information should an exception register contain?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. What happened, parties, accounts, evidence, approval, VAT and duplicate risk.
2. A sale creates revenue/receivable; a receipt settles an amount already due where applicable.
3. They may indicate duplicate capture or duplicate payment risk.
4. Creditors decreases and Bank decreases.
5. Keep them visible and investigate before allocation.
6. Charges may appear at bank before being captured internally.
7. It reduces or reverses part of an earlier transaction.
8. Issue, owner, next action, deadline and status.

## Key Takeaways
- Process evidence, not assumptions.
- Analyse every transaction before choosing accounts.
- Receipts and payments must not duplicate the original income or expense.
- Exceptions should remain visible until resolved.
- Every entry should be traceable back to source evidence.
