# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 2: Source Documents & Transaction Analysis
### Lesson 5: Analysing Transactions into Accounts

**Estimated study time:** 50–60 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Analyse a transaction before capture.
2. Identify the account types affected by common business events.
3. Distinguish assets, liabilities, equity, income and expenses in practical examples.
4. Apply debit and credit logic to routine transactions.
5. Recognise transactions that need clarification rather than automatic coding.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A bookkeeping system cannot decide the economic meaning of every transaction for you. Software may suggest an account based on previous entries, bank rules or automation, but the bookkeeper remains responsible for checking whether the classification makes sense.

Transaction analysis is the bridge between the source document and the accounting entry. It asks: What happened? Which accounts changed? Did they increase or decrease? What evidence supports that conclusion?

## Key Terms
- **Transaction analysis** — Determining the financial effect of a business event before or during recording.
- **Account classification** — Assigning a transaction to the appropriate account or category.
- **Debit** — Left side of an account.
- **Credit** — Right side of an account.
- **Capital expenditure** — Spending that creates or improves an asset according to applicable accounting policy rather than being treated entirely as a routine period expense.
- **Revenue expenditure** — Ordinary expenditure consumed in operating the business.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Use a structured analysis process
For each transaction ask:
1. What happened economically?
2. Which accounts are affected?
3. What type of accounts are they?
4. Did each account increase or decrease?
5. What debit and credit follow from that?
6. Is the evidence sufficient?

This method is safer than choosing an account based only on the wording in the bank statement.

### 2. Asset purchases
Suppose the business buys a printer for R6,500 cash and the accounting policy treats it as equipment.

Effect:
- Equipment asset increases → Debit Equipment R6,500.
- Bank asset decreases → Credit Bank R6,500.

The payment of cash does not automatically make the full amount an ordinary expense.

### 3. Routine expenses
The business pays R3,000 office rent by EFT.

Effect:
- Rent Expense increases → Debit Rent Expense R3,000.
- Bank decreases → Credit Bank R3,000.

### 4. Credit purchases
The business receives a R4,800 supplier invoice for office supplies to be paid later.

Effect:
- Relevant expense/asset increases → Debit the appropriate account.
- Creditors liability increases → Credit Creditors R4,800.

When the supplier is later paid:
- Creditors decreases → Debit Creditors.
- Bank decreases → Credit Bank.

The payment does not create the expense a second time if the invoice was already recorded.

### 5. Credit sales
A service business invoices a customer R9,000.

Effect:
- Debtors increases → Debit Debtors R9,000.
- Service Income increases → Credit Service Income R9,000.

When the customer pays:
- Debit Bank.
- Credit Debtors.

### 6. Owner funding and withdrawals
Owner introduces R20,000 to a sole proprietorship:
- Debit Bank.
- Credit Owner's Capital.

Owner withdraws R1,000 for personal use:
- Debit Drawings.
- Credit Bank.

Do not use sales or business-expense accounts simply because cash entered or left the bank.

### 7. Loans
The business receives R100,000 from a lender:
- Debit Bank R100,000.
- Credit Loan Liability R100,000.

When a loan instalment is paid, part may reduce the loan principal and part may represent interest expense according to the lender statement and accounting records. Do not code the entire instalment automatically as loan expense.

### 8. Deposits and advances
Money received before goods or services are earned may not automatically be current income. The correct treatment depends on the nature of the transaction and the entity's accounting policy.

Likewise, a deposit paid to a supplier may be an advance/asset rather than an immediate expense.

When the facts are unclear, obtain clarification.

### 9. Repairs versus assets
A R900 repair to existing office furniture is different from buying new furniture for R15,000. One may be a repair expense; the other may be an asset purchase according to policy.

The bookkeeper should follow approved capitalisation and accounting policies rather than deciding based only on amount.

### 10. Bank descriptions can mislead
Bank descriptions such as “PAYMENT,” “TRANSFER” or a person's name do not provide enough classification detail.

Use source documents and business context. If the bank shows R7,200 paid to “ABC,” determine whether ABC is a supplier, landlord, employee, lender or something else before coding.

## Worked Example
A business bank statement shows:
- R12,000 received from “Mokoena Trading”;
- R5,500 paid to “OfficePro”;
- R2,000 transferred by owner.

Investigation shows:
- Mokoena Trading paid an existing debtor invoice: Debit Bank / Credit Debtors.
- OfficePro invoice was previously captured: Debit Creditors / Credit Bank.
- Owner introduced additional capital: Debit Bank / Credit Capital.

If all three were coded directly from the bank statement as income or expenses, the records would be wrong.

## Practical Activity
Analyse these transactions and identify the debit and credit:
1. Cash sale R2,000.
2. Supplier invoice for electricity R1,800, unpaid.
3. Payment of that supplier invoice later.
4. Owner contributes R10,000.
5. Customer pays R3,500 previously owed.
6. Bank loan of R50,000 received.
7. Equipment bought for R8,000 cash.

## Key Takeaways
- Analyse the economic event before selecting an account.
- Cash movement and income/expense recognition are not the same thing.
- Payments can settle previously recorded liabilities.
- Receipts can settle previously recorded debtors.
- Source evidence and business context are essential for correct coding.
