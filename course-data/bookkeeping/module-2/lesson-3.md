# Professional Certificate in Bookkeeping
## Module 2: Source Documents & Transaction Analysis
### Lesson 3: Supplier Invoices, Debit Notes & Purchase Documents

**Estimated study time:** 50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Recognise key supplier-side documents.
2. Explain how supplier invoices affect creditor and purchase/expense records.
3. Distinguish supplier credit notes from debit-note or dispute documentation.
4. Match purchasing evidence before payment processing.
5. Apply basic duplicate, approval and supplier-statement controls.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Supplier documents create obligations for the business. If an invoice is captured twice, paid without approval or posted to the wrong supplier, the business can lose money and produce incorrect creditor balances. A reliable bookkeeper therefore treats supplier processing as a controlled evidence chain rather than simply entering invoices as they arrive.

## Key Terms
- **Supplier invoice** — Document from a supplier requesting payment for goods or services supplied.
- **Creditor / accounts payable** — Amount owed by the business to a supplier.
- **Purchase order (PO)** — Authorised order issued to a supplier under the organisation's purchasing process.
- **Delivery note / goods received evidence** — Evidence that goods were received.
- **Supplier credit note** — Document from a supplier reducing an amount previously billed.
- **Debit note** — Document used in some systems/business processes to notify or record a debit/claim against a supplier account; treatment depends on the process.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Supplier invoices create liabilities
When a valid supplier invoice is recorded on credit, the business normally recognises the relevant asset or expense and increases creditors/accounts payable.

Before capture, the bookkeeper should confirm appropriate details such as:
- correct supplier;
- invoice number and date;
- description and quantity;
- arithmetic;
- purchase order or approval reference where required;
- evidence of receipt or service completion;
- VAT details where relevant;
- whether the invoice was already captured.

### 2. The purchasing evidence chain
A strong purchasing process may include:
1. purchase request;
2. approval;
3. purchase order;
4. goods/service receipt;
5. supplier invoice;
6. invoice verification;
7. payment approval;
8. payment evidence.

Not every small business uses a formal PO system, but the control purpose remains: prove that the purchase was legitimate, received and correctly billed.

### 3. Three-way matching
Where used, a three-way match compares:
- purchase order;
- goods received/delivery evidence;
- supplier invoice.

If the PO approved 20 boxes at R100 each, the delivery evidence shows 20 received, but the supplier invoice charges 25, the discrepancy should be resolved before payment rather than quietly captured as correct.

### 4. Supplier credit notes
A supplier may issue a credit note when:
- goods are returned;
- a price was overcharged;
- an agreed rebate applies;
- an invoice is partly reversed.

The credit note reduces the amount owed to the supplier and should be matched to the relevant account/transaction.

A common error is to leave supplier credits unallocated while paying the gross invoice balance, resulting in overpayment.

### 5. Debit notes and claims
Terminology varies between organisations and software. A buyer may raise a debit note or supplier claim when requesting a reduction for returned goods, shortages or other approved reasons.

The bookkeeper should follow the organisation's procedure and should not assume that every internally raised debit note has already been accepted by the supplier. Reconciliation may be needed before the creditor balance is final.

### 6. Duplicate invoice prevention
Controls may include:
- unique supplier + invoice-number validation;
- duplicate amount/date reports;
- checking scanned copies against originals;
- restricting overrides of duplicate warnings.

A supplier may legitimately issue two invoices for the same amount on the same date, so amount matching alone is not proof of duplication. Investigate the full evidence.

### 7. Supplier statements
A supplier statement helps compare the supplier's view of the account with the business's creditor ledger.

Differences may arise from:
- invoice not received or not captured;
- credit note missing;
- payment not allocated by supplier;
- payment captured to wrong supplier;
- timing difference;
- disputed transaction.

The statement is a reconciliation tool, not a replacement for invoices.

### 8. Payment preparation
Before an invoice is included in a payment batch, the bookkeeper should confirm it is valid, due and appropriately approved. Supplier bank details should be taken from controlled master data, not casually copied from an unexpected email.

### 9. Filing and audit trail
A supplier transaction should be traceable from ledger entry back to invoice and relevant approval/receipt evidence. Electronic attachments or indexed folders can support this.

## Worked Example
A business orders cleaning materials for R6,900. The PO is for 30 units. Delivery evidence shows only 28 units arrived, while the supplier invoice bills all 30.

The bookkeeper does not simply pay the full invoice. The quantity difference is referred to procurement/supplier for resolution. Depending on the agreed outcome, the supplier may issue a corrected invoice or credit note, or the remaining units may be delivered. The accounting record should reflect the approved outcome.

## Practical Activity
For each situation, state the control action:
1. Supplier invoice has no PO where the business normally requires one.
2. Invoice number already exists in the system.
3. Supplier statement contains an invoice missing from the ledger.
4. Credit note exists but has not been allocated.
5. Supplier emails new bank details immediately before payment.

## Key Takeaways
- Supplier processing must connect approval, receipt, invoice and payment evidence.
- Duplicate invoices and quantity/price differences require investigation.
- Supplier credits must be captured and allocated correctly.
- Statements are reconciliation tools, not replacement invoices.
- Controlled supplier master data is critical before payment.
