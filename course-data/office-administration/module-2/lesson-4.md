# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 2: Professional Communication
### Lesson 4: Business Writing: Memos, Notices & Routine Correspondence

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Select an appropriate format for routine workplace correspondence.
2. Structure memos, notices and business letters clearly.
3. Write concise administrative messages using plain professional language.
4. Proofread correspondence for accuracy, tone and completeness.
5. Apply document standards and approval controls before distribution.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Administrators regularly turn instructions and information into documents other people must understand and act on. A poorly written notice can send employees to the wrong venue; an unclear letter can damage a customer relationship. Good business writing makes the purpose, facts and required action easy to identify.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Memo** — Internal written communication used to inform, request or record information.
- **Notice** — Short communication intended to bring information to the attention of a defined audience.
- **Correspondence** — Written communication exchanged for business purposes.
- **Plain language** — Clear wording designed to be understood without unnecessary complexity.
- **Proofreading** — Final checking for errors, accuracy and presentation.
- **Template** — Approved reusable document structure.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Match the format to the purpose
Use the organisation's approved format. A memo may suit an internal procedural reminder, a notice may announce a planned office closure, and formal correspondence may be appropriate for external stakeholders.

Do not choose a format simply because it looks impressive. Choose one that supports the communication purpose and record requirements.

### 2. Write for the reader
Identify what the reader needs to know. Put essential facts prominently: purpose, dates, locations, reference information, action and deadlines.

Avoid unnecessary jargon. Technical language may be required in specialist contexts, but routine administrative communication should generally be understandable to its intended audience.

### 3. Use a logical structure
A useful structure is: purpose first, essential context second, required action third, and contact or next-step information last.

Headings and bullets can improve readability when several details must be scanned quickly.

### 4. Memos
An internal memo normally identifies the intended recipients, originator, date and subject according to the organisation's template. The body should explain the issue and action clearly.

A memo is not automatically an approval merely because it is formal. Approval must come from the authorised person or process.

### 5. Notices
Notices should answer practical questions. Who is affected? What is happening? When? Where? What should people do? Who can answer questions?

Remove expired notices according to office practice so outdated instructions do not remain visible.

### 6. Routine external correspondence
External letters or formal messages should use accurate names, titles, addresses and references. Maintain a respectful tone and avoid commitments outside organisational authority.

Where a standard template exists, use the current approved version rather than copying an old letter that may contain outdated details.

### 7. Proofreading
Proofreading should check more than spelling. Verify names, dates, times, amounts, telephone numbers, references, attachments, required actions and signatory or approval information.

Read from the receiver's perspective: could the message reasonably be misunderstood?

### 8. Approval and distribution
Some correspondence can be issued routinely; other documents require manager approval or signature. Know the difference.

After approval, distribute through authorised channels and retain the required record. Do not change an approved document materially after sign-off without returning it for appropriate review.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
An office will close at 13:00 on Friday for scheduled electrical maintenance. A useful staff notice states the date, closure time, who is affected, any remote-work or access instruction authorised by management, when normal operations resume and where questions should be directed. A vague notice saying “Office closing early Friday” creates avoidable uncertainty.

## Practical Activity
Draft three short pieces of correspondence:
1. an internal memo reminding staff of a revised stationery-order deadline;
2. a notice announcing a temporary meeting-room closure;
3. a professional external letter acknowledging receipt of a customer document without promising an outcome.

Proofread each using a checklist for purpose, audience, dates, names, action, deadline, tone, approval and distribution.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why should document format match purpose?
2. What is plain language?
3. What information should a workplace notice normally make clear?
4. Does a formal memo automatically authorise a decision?
5. Why should old correspondence not be copied blindly?
6. What should proofreading verify besides spelling?
7. Why must material changes after approval be controlled?
8. What is the administrator's responsibility after distribution where records are required?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Different formats support different audiences, actions and record needs.
2. Clear wording that avoids unnecessary complexity and helps the intended reader understand.
3. What is happening, who is affected, when/where, required action and contact information.
4. No. Authority comes from the authorised role or process.
5. It may contain outdated names, facts, branding or requirements.
6. Names, dates, amounts, references, actions, deadlines, attachments and approval details.
7. The approved version must remain controlled and significant changes may alter the authorised message.
8. Retain or file the required evidence according to organisational practice.

## Key Takeaways
- Select formats according to purpose and audience.
- Put essential information and required action where readers can find it quickly.
- Plain professional language usually improves administrative communication.
- Proofreading includes factual and procedural accuracy.
- Use current templates and respect approval controls.
- Distribution and recordkeeping complete the correspondence workflow.
