# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 2: Professional Communication
### Lesson 1: Communication Principles in the Workplace

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the purpose of effective workplace communication.
2. Select an appropriate communication channel for different administrative situations.
3. Structure messages for clarity, accuracy and action.
4. Recognise common communication barriers and reduce misunderstandings.
5. Use confirmation and follow-up to close communication loops.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Administration depends on communication. A perfectly organised diary is ineffective if participants receive the wrong time. A correctly filed customer complaint still fails service if nobody responsible is told to act. Professional administrators therefore need more than good language: they need to understand purpose, audience, channel, timing and confirmation.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Sender** — Person or source communicating a message.
- **Receiver** — Person or group for whom the message is intended.
- **Channel** — Method used to communicate, such as email, telephone, meeting or messaging platform.
- **Feedback** — Response showing how a message was received or understood.
- **Communication barrier** — Anything that interferes with accurate understanding.
- **Confirmation** — Verification of important information or agreement.
- **Communication record** — Evidence or documentation of relevant communication.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Begin with purpose
Before communicating, identify what the receiver needs to know or do. Is the purpose to inform, request, confirm, instruct, clarify, escalate or record?

A message with no clear purpose creates unnecessary back-and-forth. “Meeting changed” is weaker than “Tomorrow's supplier meeting has moved from 10:00 to 11:30 in Boardroom 2. Please confirm receipt.”

### 2. Know the audience
The same information may need different presentation for different audiences. A manager may need a concise decision summary, while a new employee may need detailed steps. A customer should not be expected to understand internal abbreviations.

Consider the receiver's role, existing knowledge and what action is required.

### 3. Choose the right channel
Email is useful for information that needs a written record or attachments. A telephone call may be better for a time-sensitive clarification. A meeting may be appropriate for complex discussion. Approved instant messaging can support quick coordination.

Sensitive information requires additional judgement. Use only authorised channels and follow organisational privacy and security requirements.

### 4. Structure clear messages
Useful workplace messages normally answer the receiver's key questions: What is this about? What has happened or is needed? What action is required? By when? Who should be contacted for clarification?

Put important information where it is easy to find. Avoid hiding a deadline at the end of a long paragraph.

### 5. Accuracy before speed
Fast communication that contains the wrong date, amount, name or attachment creates more work. Verify critical details before sending. When relaying information, distinguish confirmed facts from assumptions.

If you do not know, say that you will verify rather than invent an answer.

### 6. Communication barriers
Barriers include unclear language, noise, poor connections, jargon, assumptions, language differences, emotional tension, incomplete information and information overload.

Administrators can reduce barriers by asking clarifying questions, summarising key points, using plain language and confirming critical information.

### 7. Listening is active work
Listening means more than remaining silent. Focus on the speaker, identify the main request, ask relevant questions and confirm important details.

When taking a complicated telephone request, summarise: “To confirm, you need the signed quotation emailed before 15:00 today; is that correct?”

### 8. Tone and professionalism
Tone should fit the situation. Professional communication can be warm and human without becoming careless. Avoid sarcasm, insults and emotional responses when dealing with conflict.

Written messages deserve particular care because tone can be misinterpreted without facial expression or voice.

### 9. Close the communication loop
Important messages often require confirmation or follow-up. Sending information does not prove that the receiver saw, understood or acted on it.

The required level of confirmation depends on risk. A routine office notice may need no individual acknowledgement; a changed travel booking or urgent executive meeting may require explicit confirmation.

### 10. Keep appropriate records
Some communications should be retained because they contain approvals, commitments, decisions or important instructions. Follow organisational records and retention practices rather than keeping everything indefinitely or deleting important evidence casually.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A manager tells an administrator verbally, “Move tomorrow's client meeting to later.” The instruction is incomplete. A professional administrator clarifies the new time, checks whether the client and other attendees are affected, updates the authorised calendar, communicates the change through the appropriate channel and confirms critical participants received it.

The administrator turns a vague instruction into controlled communication.

## Practical Activity
For each scenario, choose the best primary communication channel and explain why:
1. An urgent meeting time changes in 30 minutes.
2. A manager approves a supplier quotation and the approval needs a record.
3. A customer gives a complex complaint by telephone.
4. A team needs to discuss a new office process with several unresolved questions.
5. You need a colleague to confirm one missing figure in a report.

Then draft the key information each communication should contain.

## Knowledge Check
1. What should you identify before choosing how to communicate?
2. Why might the same information be presented differently to a manager and a new employee?
3. Name two factors that influence channel choice.
4. Why should critical details be verified before sending?
5. What is a communication barrier?
6. How can an administrator demonstrate active listening?
7. Why can written tone require special care?
8. When is communication confirmation particularly important?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. The purpose, audience and required action.
2. Their roles and existing knowledge differ, so they may require different levels of detail.
3. Examples include urgency, complexity, sensitivity, need for a written record and available authorised channels.
4. Errors create confusion, risk and rework.
5. Anything that interferes with accurate transmission or understanding of a message.
6. Focus, ask clarifying questions and summarise important information for confirmation.
7. Written messages lack vocal and facial cues and can therefore be interpreted differently than intended.
8. When the information is urgent, high-impact, sensitive or connected to an important commitment.

## Key Takeaways
- Effective communication begins with purpose and audience.
- Select channels according to urgency, complexity, sensitivity and record requirements.
- Clear messages make required action and deadlines visible.
- Accuracy matters as much as speed.
- Active listening and confirmation reduce misunderstandings.
- Important communication loops should be followed through rather than assumed complete.
