# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 2: Professional Communication
### Lesson 3: Business Email & Digital Communication

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Structure professional business emails.
2. Use To, Cc, Bcc and Reply All with appropriate judgement.
3. Manage attachments, links and sensitive information safely.
4. Write clear requests, confirmations and follow-ups.
5. Apply professional standards to workplace messaging platforms.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Email and messaging are central to modern administration. They are fast, searchable and useful for sharing documents, but a careless click can send confidential information to the wrong person or create confusion across an entire team. Strong digital communication requires deliberate recipient selection, clear writing and careful handling of information.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **To** — Primary recipient or recipients expected to read or act.
- **Cc** — Additional recipient included for relevant visibility.
- **Bcc** — Recipient hidden from other recipients; use according to purpose and policy.
- **Reply All** — Sends a response to the sender and all included recipients.
- **Attachment** — A file sent with a digital message.
- **Phishing** — Deceptive communication intended to steal information, credentials or trigger unsafe actions.
- **Thread** — A sequence of related messages grouped together.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Make the subject useful
A clear subject helps the receiver understand purpose and priority. “Meeting” is vague. “Action required: confirm 12 September supplier meeting by 15:00” is more informative when that level of detail is appropriate.

Avoid misleading labels such as URGENT when the message is routine.

### 2. Put the purpose early
Business readers often scan. State the main purpose near the beginning, then provide necessary context and the required action.

For example: “Please approve the attached venue quotation by 14:00 Wednesday so we can secure the booking.” The reader immediately knows what is required and why the deadline matters.

### 3. Use recipients deliberately
The To field should contain people directly involved. Cc is useful when someone genuinely needs visibility but is not the main actor. Avoid copying senior managers merely to pressure colleagues.

Use Reply All only when everyone needs the response. Unnecessary group replies create noise and may expose information beyond those who need it.

Bcc can be appropriate in specific authorised situations, such as protecting recipient addresses in certain bulk communications, but it should not be used deceptively. Follow organisational policy.

### 4. Attachments and links
Before sending, check that the correct file is attached, it opens properly where practical, and the version is current. Consider whether the recipient is authorised to receive it.

Use approved file-sharing methods for large or sensitive documents. Be cautious with unexpected links and attachments received from others; suspicious communication should follow security procedures.

### 5. Tone
Email tone should be professional and proportionate. Short does not need to mean rude. Avoid writing an emotional response while angry. For sensitive disagreements, a call or meeting may be more effective than a long argumentative email, followed by an appropriate written record if required.

### 6. Requests and deadlines
Make requests specific. “Please send this soon” is unclear. State what is needed and by when, with enough context for the recipient to act.

Do not create artificial emergencies through poor planning. Where a deadline is genuinely urgent, explain the relevant operational reason.

### 7. Confirmations
Use email to confirm arrangements, decisions or actions where a record is useful. A meeting confirmation may include date, time, location/link, attendees and required preparation.

If an important verbal decision needs a record, an appropriate follow-up email can summarise the agreed action without pretending that the email itself created approval.

### 8. Follow-up professionally
Before chasing someone, check when the request was sent and whether the deadline has actually passed. A useful follow-up references the original request, states the outstanding action and explains any approaching impact.

Avoid sending repeated messages every few minutes unless the situation genuinely requires immediate escalation.

### 9. Messaging platforms
Workplace chat tools are useful for quick coordination, but they still require professionalism. Use appropriate channels, avoid unnecessary after-hours messages unless the role and urgency justify them, and move complex or sensitive discussions to a better channel where needed.

Important approvals should be recorded in an authorised manner according to organisational practice.

### 10. Digital permanence and confidentiality
Assume business messages may be retained, forwarded or reviewed. Do not write content that would be inappropriate if read by an authorised manager, auditor or affected stakeholder.

Check autocomplete recipients carefully. A similar name can lead to serious misdirection of information.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
An administrator needs approval for a R4,800 catering quotation by 13:00 to hold a booking. A strong email uses a specific subject, addresses the authorised approver, states the approval required, identifies the deadline and reason, attaches the correct quotation, and copies only people who genuinely need visibility. After approval, the administrator records the decision and completes the next workflow step.

## Practical Activity
Draft three emails: a meeting confirmation, a request for missing information with a deadline, and a polite follow-up after the deadline has passed. For each, identify who belongs in To and Cc and explain whether an attachment is necessary.

Then review each draft for subject clarity, required action, deadline, tone, recipient accuracy and confidentiality.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. The topic and, where appropriate, the required action or timing.
2. When another person genuinely needs relevant visibility but is not the primary actor.
3. Not every recipient needs every response, and unnecessary replies create noise or disclosure risk.
4. Correct file, current version, authorised recipient; also check accessibility/opening where appropriate.
5. A clear required action, relevant context and deadline.
6. When the matter is complex, sensitive or likely to be misunderstood in writing.
7. The original request, outstanding action and relevant deadline or impact.
8. Similar names can cause confidential or incorrect information to be sent to the wrong person.

## Key Takeaways
- Strong business email makes purpose and action easy to identify.
- Recipient selection is a professional judgement, not a habit.
- Verify attachments, links, versions and authority before sending.
- Clear deadlines reduce ambiguity.
- Digital messaging should remain professional and secure.
- Important communication should support the wider administrative workflow.
