# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 2: Professional Communication
### Lesson 8: Communication Records, Follow-up & Escalation

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Identify workplace communications that require records or follow-up.
2. Create concise records of decisions, commitments and messages.
3. Track communication actions to closure.
4. Escalate unanswered or high-risk communication appropriately.
5. Apply confidentiality and records-management principles to communication evidence.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Communication can sound successful in the moment and still fail operationally. A customer may be promised an update that nobody records. A manager may approve a change verbally, but the implementation team never receives it. Administrators create continuity by recording important commitments and following them until the communication produces the required action.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Communication record** — Evidence of a relevant message, decision, instruction or interaction.
- **Action item** — Specific task arising from communication.
- **Owner** — Person responsible for completing an action.
- **Due date** — Date or time by which an action should be completed.
- **Escalation trigger** — Defined condition that indicates a matter should be referred.
- **Closure** — Confirmation that the required communication-related action has been completed.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Decide what needs a record
Not every greeting or routine conversation needs documentation. Records are more likely to be important when communication contains approvals, deadlines, customer complaints, financial commitments, changes to arrangements, formal instructions, incidents or decisions.

Follow organisational rules for what must be retained and where.

### 2. Record facts, not a transcript
A useful record captures what matters: date/time, people involved, key issue, decision or commitment, action owner and deadline. Unless required, there is rarely a need to reproduce every word spoken.

Avoid personal commentary that does not contribute to the business record.

### 3. Convert communication into action
When a meeting or call produces work, create actionable entries. “Finance to investigate” is weaker than “Thando/Finance to confirm invoice status by 12:00 Tuesday.”

An action should have enough ownership and timing to be followed up.

### 4. Follow-up points
Schedule follow-up before the final outcome becomes late. If a response is due Friday and another process depends on it, checking only late Friday may leave no recovery time.

The appropriate follow-up interval depends on urgency and context.

### 5. Escalation triggers
Examples include missed deadlines, repeated unanswered requests, high-impact complaints, confidential information sent incorrectly, unresolved dependencies or an approaching executive commitment.

Escalation should not be used to embarrass someone. Its purpose is to obtain the authority, attention or support required to protect the outcome.

### 6. Write useful escalations
A concise escalation answers:
- What is the issue?
- What has already been done?
- What is the impact or deadline?
- What decision/support is required?
- What evidence is available?

This is more useful than forwarding a long email chain with no explanation.

### 7. Close the loop
When the action is complete, notify people who need the outcome and update the task or record. Closure may also require filing correspondence or attaching evidence to a system record.

Do not continue unnecessary reminders after closure.

### 8. Confidentiality
Communication records can contain personal or commercially sensitive information. Store them only in authorised systems, apply access restrictions and avoid duplicating sensitive records unnecessarily.

Do not use private personal accounts to create an unofficial archive of workplace correspondence.

### 9. Handover continuity
Outstanding communications should be included in handovers where another employee needs to act. Provide enough context, current status, next action and deadline.

### 10. PA-level communication control
Executive support produces many commitments from meetings, calls and email. A PA who can reliably capture, route and follow up those commitments becomes a control point for executive effectiveness. The PA must still respect ownership: following up an action does not mean taking over the authorised person's decision.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
During a call, a supplier agrees to send a corrected quotation by 11:00 Thursday so a manager can approve it before a 15:00 booking cut-off. The administrator records the commitment, schedules a follow-up before the approval window becomes too narrow, receives and checks the quotation, routes it for approval, communicates the outcome and files the required evidence. The call becomes a controlled workflow rather than a memory.

## Practical Activity
Create a communication follow-up register with columns: Date, Stakeholder, Subject, Commitment/Decision, Owner, Due Date, Follow-up Date, Status, Escalation Trigger and Record Location. Add eight realistic entries. For two missed deadlines, draft a concise escalation using issue, action taken, impact and decision/support required.

## Key Takeaways
- Important communication should create usable evidence where required.
- Convert discussions into clear actions with owners and deadlines.
- Follow up early enough to protect the final commitment.
- Escalation should be concise, factual and purposeful.
- Close communication loops and update records when outcomes are achieved.
- Protect communication records according to confidentiality and records requirements.
