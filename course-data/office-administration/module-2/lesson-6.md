# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 2: Professional Communication
### Lesson 6: Communicating with Managers, Colleagues & Clients

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Adapt communication to managers, colleagues and external clients.
2. Provide concise status updates and decision-ready information.
3. Coordinate work across colleagues without misrepresenting authority.
4. Communicate service expectations to clients professionally.
5. Protect confidentiality while keeping stakeholders appropriately informed.

## Why This Lesson Matters
An administrator may communicate with a manager, supplier, customer and colleague within minutes. The underlying standards remain the same—accuracy, respect and clarity—but the information each person needs can differ. Strong administrators adapt without becoming inconsistent or unprofessional.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Stakeholder** — A person or group with an interest in or influence on an activity.
- **Status update** — Concise information about current progress, issues and next actions.
- **Decision-ready information** — Information organised so an authorised person can make a decision efficiently.
- **Service expectation** — What a customer or stakeholder can reasonably expect regarding process, timing or response.
- **Need-to-know** — Limiting information to people who require it for legitimate work purposes.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Communicating upward
Managers often need concise information that helps them decide or act. A useful update states the issue, current status, deadline, risk and decision needed.

Instead of “The venue is a problem,” say: “The preferred venue is unavailable on 18 September. Venue B is available at R1,200 more. We need a decision by 14:00 to hold the booking.”

### 2. Do not hide bad news
Administrators sometimes delay reporting a problem because they hope to fix it first. Resolve routine matters independently where appropriate, but communicate early when a significant commitment or decision is at risk.

Managers need time to choose alternatives.

### 3. Communicating with colleagues
Peer communication should be cooperative and specific. When requesting work, explain what is needed, by when and why where relevant. Avoid using a manager's name as a threat: “The director wants this now” should not be used to create false authority.

### 4. Coordinating without commanding
Administrators frequently coordinate people who do not report to them. Influence comes from clear requests, shared deadlines, follow-up and escalation—not pretending to be their supervisor.

If another department misses an agreed input, follow up professionally and escalate through the correct route if the overall commitment is at risk.

### 5. Communicating with clients
Clients need clear, respectful information about what can happen next. Avoid internal jargon and blame. Do not expose internal disputes or confidential staff details.

If a process will take three working days, state the approved expectation rather than promising same-day completion simply to please the customer.

### 6. Managing expectations
Good service is not the same as saying yes to everything. Accurate expectations build more trust than unrealistic promises.

Where you do not control the outcome, explain the next step: “I have referred the matter to the accounts team and will provide an update by 15:00,” rather than “It will definitely be paid today.”

### 7. Confidentiality and audience
Different stakeholders may legitimately receive different information. A colleague helping organise a meeting may need attendee names but not the confidential reason for an executive discussion.

Share enough for the task, through authorised channels, without unnecessary disclosure.

### 8. Status updates
A strong update can use four elements:
1. **Status:** where the task stands;
2. **Issue:** what is blocking or changing;
3. **Impact:** why it matters;
4. **Next action/decision:** what will happen or what is required.

This structure reduces long unfocused explanations.

### 9. Professional disagreement
If a colleague or manager appears to be working from incorrect information, raise the concern respectfully and provide evidence. Do not knowingly proceed with an avoidable error simply because challenging it feels uncomfortable.

Authority still matters: after appropriate clarification, final decisions belong to the authorised role unless they conflict with law, safety or organisational controls requiring further escalation.

### 10. PA communication
A PA often communicates on behalf of an executive in administrative matters. The PA must distinguish between relaying an approved message and inventing the executive's position. Statements attributed to an executive should be accurate and authorised.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A client asks when a delayed order will arrive. Operations has not yet confirmed a delivery date. A poor response is “It will arrive tomorrow” simply to calm the client. A better response explains that the delivery date is being confirmed, provides the next update time and follows up internally. This manages expectations without inventing certainty.

## Practical Activity
Write three versions of a status update about a delayed meeting pack: one for the manager who must decide whether to postpone distribution, one for a colleague whose report is still missing, and one for attendees who only need to know when the pack will be issued. Compare what information is necessary for each audience.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. It clearly states the issue, relevant facts, impact, timing and decision required.
2. Early information gives authorised people more time and options to respond.
3. Through clear requests, agreed deadlines, follow-up and appropriate escalation.
4. It creates false expectations and can damage trust when the promise fails.
5. Sharing information only with people who require it for legitimate work purposes.
6. Status, issue, impact and next action/decision.
7. Respectfully, using facts or evidence and the appropriate authority/escalation route.
8. Inventing, exaggerating or misrepresenting the executive's instructions or position.

## Key Takeaways
- Adapt detail and format to stakeholder needs while keeping standards consistent.
- Managers benefit from concise, decision-ready information.
- Coordination does not require pretending to have supervisory authority.
- Accurate expectations are better than pleasing but unreliable promises.
- Share information according to legitimate need and authority.
- A PA must represent executive communication accurately.
