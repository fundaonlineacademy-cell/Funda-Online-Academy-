# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 2: Professional Communication
### Lesson 5: Listening, Questioning & Clarifying Information

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Use active listening in administrative interactions.
2. Select open, closed and probing questions appropriately.
3. Clarify vague or incomplete instructions before acting.
4. Summarise information to confirm shared understanding.
5. Avoid assumptions that create administrative errors.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Many administrative errors begin before the task starts. “Book the usual room,” “send it to everyone,” or “get this done quickly” may sound understandable until the administrator discovers that people had different assumptions. Skilled administrators clarify important details without turning every simple request into an interrogation.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Active listening** — Deliberately focusing on, interpreting and confirming a message.
- **Open question** — A question that invites explanation rather than a one-word answer.
- **Closed question** — A question seeking a specific or limited answer.
- **Probing question** — A follow-up question used to obtain necessary detail.
- **Paraphrase** — Restating information in your own words to confirm meaning.
- **Assumption** — Something treated as true without sufficient confirmation.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Listen for the outcome
When receiving an instruction, identify the desired result, deadline, people involved and any constraints. Do not focus only on individual words.

If a manager says, “Please arrange a meeting with Finance next week,” useful details may include purpose, required attendees, duration, urgency and whether the manager has preferred time windows.

### 2. Open questions
Open questions help gather context: “What outcome do you need from the meeting?” or “What happened after the customer received the order?” They are useful when the situation is not yet well understood.

### 3. Closed questions
Closed questions confirm specifics: “Is 14:00 acceptable?” “Should I include the Finance Manager?” They are useful when choices are known and a precise answer is required.

### 4. Probing questions
Probing questions fill gaps. If a caller says an invoice is wrong, ask which invoice, which amount is disputed and what information they expected. Keep questions relevant and respectful.

### 5. Clarify ambiguous language
Words such as soon, later, everyone, normal and urgent can mean different things. Clarify when the difference matters.

“By soon, do you need this before today's 15:00 client call?” converts vague timing into an actionable deadline.

### 6. Paraphrase and summarise
After complex instructions, summarise the key points: “To confirm, I will move the meeting to Thursday at 10:00, keep the same attendees and send the revised pack by Wednesday afternoon.” This gives the speaker an opportunity to correct misunderstandings.

### 7. Avoid pretending to understand
Employees sometimes fear that asking a question makes them look inexperienced. Acting on a major assumption can be far more damaging. Ask concise questions at the appropriate time.

However, first check information already available to you. Repeatedly asking a manager questions that are answered in the diary or procedure wastes time.

### 8. Listen under pressure
An upset customer may give information in an unstructured way. Allow them to explain, identify facts, ask focused questions and summarise the issue. Avoid arguing over minor details before understanding the core problem.

### 9. Record important details
For tasks with deadlines, commitments or sensitive information, capture relevant details in the approved system. Memory should not be the only control after a conversation ends.

### 10. PA-level clarification
A PA often receives brief instructions from a busy executive. Over time, knowledge of preferences reduces unnecessary questions, but the PA must still clarify matters where assumptions could create significant risk, cost or reputational impact.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A manager says, “Please arrange transport for our guests tomorrow.” Before booking, the administrator establishes how many guests, collection point, destination, required arrival time, any accessibility or luggage requirements, approved supplier and budget/approval process. A few focused questions prevent an unsuitable booking.

## Practical Activity
Rewrite these vague instructions as clarification questions:
1. “Send the report to everyone.”
2. “Book somewhere nice for lunch.”
3. “Call the supplier urgently.”
4. “Use the latest figures.”
5. “Move my afternoon meetings.”

For each, identify what could go wrong if the administrator simply guessed.

4. Why should vague timing words sometimes be clarified?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Focusing on, interpreting and confirming the message rather than merely hearing words.
2. When context or explanation is needed.
3. When a specific fact or choice must be confirmed.
4. Different people may interpret them differently, creating missed expectations.
5. To verify that both people understand the same action and details.
6. No. Appropriate clarification prevents errors.
7. Efficient administrators use existing records and do not unnecessarily interrupt others.
8. When assumptions could create material risk, cost, confidentiality or reputational consequences.

## Key Takeaways
- Listen for the required outcome, not only the words used.
- Use different question types according to the information needed.
- Clarify ambiguous details that affect action.
- Summarising complex instructions reduces misunderstandings.
- Good judgement balances clarification with independent use of available information.
