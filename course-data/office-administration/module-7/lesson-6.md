# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 7: Office Technology, Information & Confidentiality
### Lesson 6: Information Security, Passwords & Access Control

**Estimated study time:** 50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain basic information-security responsibilities of office staff.
2. Use passwords, authentication and access controls appropriately.
3. Recognise phishing, social-engineering and suspicious access requests.
4. Protect devices and information in everyday office work.
5. Report security incidents promptly through approved channels.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Administrative staff handle valuable information and often have access to calendars, customer records, shared drives and executive correspondence. Security failures frequently begin with ordinary actions: a shared password, unlocked laptop or convincing phishing email. Every office employee contributes to information security.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Authentication** — Process used to verify a user's identity.
- **Multi-factor authentication (MFA)** — Authentication using more than one factor, such as password plus a code or device.
- **Access control** — Rules limiting who can view or change information or systems.
- **Phishing** — Deceptive communication designed to obtain credentials, money or sensitive information.
- **Malware** — Malicious software designed to disrupt, damage or gain unauthorised access.
- **Security incident** — Event that may compromise systems, accounts or information.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Security is part of administration
Information security is not only an IT responsibility. Administrators decide where files are stored, who receives messages, how visitors are handled and whether suspicious requests are challenged.

### 2. Password discipline
Use organisation-approved password practices. Do not share passwords or reuse work credentials in unauthorised personal services. Avoid writing credentials where others can see them.

### 3. Multi-factor authentication
MFA adds another verification step and can reduce the risk created by a stolen password. Never approve an unexpected authentication prompt simply because it appears on your phone.

### 4. Access control
Access should reflect job responsibilities. If you accidentally gain access to information you should not have, report it rather than exploring or sharing it.

### 5. Lock devices
Lock your computer when stepping away, especially in shared or public-facing areas. Keep mobile devices and storage media physically controlled according to workplace policy.

### 6. Recognise phishing
Warning signs may include unusual urgency, requests for passwords, unexpected payment changes, suspicious links, strange sender addresses or messages that pressure you to bypass normal procedure.

No single sign proves fraud, but unusual requests should trigger verification.

### 7. Safe link and attachment handling
Do not open unexpected attachments or enter credentials through suspicious links. Use approved reporting methods for suspicious messages rather than forwarding them widely.

### 8. Social engineering
Attackers can use phone calls, visitors or messaging—not just email. Verify identity and authority before disclosing information or changing normal processes.

### 9. Incident response
If you believe you clicked a malicious link, exposed a password, sent sensitive information incorrectly or lost a device, report it promptly. Early reporting gives security teams more opportunity to reduce harm.

Do not hide an incident because you are embarrassed.

### 10. PA-level security
PAs may have delegated access to executive calendars and inboxes. Use your own authorised account and delegated permissions rather than sharing the executive's credentials. This protects accountability and audit trails.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A PA receives a message appearing to come from the CEO: “I'm in a meeting. Buy gift cards immediately and send me the codes.” The request is unusual and pressures the PA to bypass normal purchasing procedure. The PA verifies through an approved channel and reports the suspicious message rather than acting on urgency alone.

## Practical Activity
Create a security checklist for an administrator covering passwords, MFA, screens, email, visitors, shared drives, mobile devices and incident reporting. Then identify the warning signs in five simulated phishing or social-engineering scenarios.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why is information security an administrative responsibility as well as an IT responsibility?
2. Why should passwords not be shared?
3. What is MFA?
4. What should you do if you unexpectedly receive an MFA approval prompt?
5. Name three phishing warning signs.
6. Why should unexpected attachments be treated carefully?
7. What should happen after a possible security incident?
8. Why should a PA use delegated access instead of an executive's password?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Administrators make daily decisions about access, storage, communication and disclosure.
2. Sharing destroys individual accountability and increases unauthorised access risk.
3. Authentication requiring more than one verification factor.
4. Do not approve it; follow the organisation's security/reporting procedure.
5. Examples include unusual urgency, credential requests, changed payment details, suspicious links or sender anomalies.
6. They may contain malicious content or lead to credential theft.
7. Report promptly through the authorised incident process.
8. Delegated access preserves individual identity, permissions and auditability.

## Key Takeaways
- Office security depends on everyday staff behaviour.
- Keep credentials private and use approved authentication controls.
- Unusual urgency should trigger verification, not shortcuts.
- Lock devices and protect access in public/shared areas.
- Report security incidents quickly.
