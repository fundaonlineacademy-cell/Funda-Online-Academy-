# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 1: Office Administration Foundations
### Lesson 4: Core Administrative Workflows & Daily Routines

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain why routine administrative work should be organised as workflows.
2. Build a simple daily control routine for office tasks.
3. Record incoming requests and track them to completion.
4. Use handovers, checklists and status information effectively.
5. Identify common workflow failures and apply practical controls.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Administrative work often consists of many small requests arriving from different directions. Without a control system, urgent-looking work can push important commitments aside, messages can disappear and unfinished tasks can be mistaken for completed work.

A daily routine does not make the administrator inflexible. It creates enough structure to respond to change without losing control.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Workflow** — A defined sequence through which work moves from request to completion.
- **Task register** — A list or system used to record work, owners, deadlines and status.
- **Status** — The current stage of a task, such as new, in progress, waiting or complete.
- **Handover** — Structured transfer of outstanding information or responsibility.
- **Checklist** — A list used to verify required actions or controls.
- **Dependency** — Information or action required from another person before a task can proceed.
- **Close-out** — Confirming completion, communicating the outcome and storing required records.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Think in workflows
A workflow normally has a trigger, actions, decision points and an end state. For example, an incoming visitor request may involve receiving the visitor, verifying the appointment, contacting the host, applying access procedure, recording arrival and later recording departure.

Thinking in workflows helps administrators identify missing steps and responsibilities.

### 2. Start the day with control
A useful morning routine may include:
- checking the diary and today's meetings;
- reviewing urgent email and messages;
- checking outstanding tasks from the previous day;
- identifying deadlines and dependencies;
- confirming important visitors or bookings;
- reviewing any handover information;
- planning focused time for document or record work.

The exact routine should match the role and workplace.

### 3. Capture work when it arrives
Relying on memory becomes unreliable when requests multiply. Tasks should be captured in an approved system such as a task manager, diary, workflow system, ticketing platform or controlled register.

Useful fields include task, requester, date received, deadline, priority, next action, responsible person and status. Sensitive information should only be captured in authorised systems.

### 4. Use meaningful statuses
A task marked “in progress” for three weeks tells a manager very little. Better statuses can distinguish:
- **New** — not yet reviewed;
- **Actioning** — currently being worked on;
- **Waiting** — dependent on another person or event;
- **Escalated** — referred for decision or intervention;
- **Completed** — required work finished;
- **Closed** — outcome communicated and required records filed.

The organisation may use different labels, but statuses should help people understand what is actually happening.

### 5. Control dependencies
Many tasks depend on other people. If meeting minutes require a manager's approval, record that the task is waiting for approval and set a follow-up point. Do not remove it from the task list simply because your first action is finished.

A dependency should have an owner and, where possible, a follow-up date.

### 6. Build checkpoints into recurring work
Recurring tasks are ideal for checklists. A meeting-room opening checklist, month-end filing checklist or new-employee administration checklist reduces reliance on memory.

Checklists should focus on meaningful controls. A list with fifty unnecessary boxes may be ignored. Review checklists when procedures change.

### 7. Manage interruptions
Administrators are frequently interrupted by calls, visitors and urgent requests. Before abandoning current work, record where you stopped and any next action. When possible, group routine activities such as filing or non-urgent email processing into planned periods.

True emergencies should interrupt routine work. Everything described as “urgent” by a requester is not automatically the organisation's highest priority; prioritisation is addressed more deeply later in this module.

### 8. End-of-day close-out
Before finishing the day, review outstanding work. Ask:
- What is incomplete?
- What is waiting on someone else?
- What needs to happen first tomorrow?
- Has anything urgent not been communicated?
- Are sensitive documents secured?
- Does another person need a handover?

This prevents the next day from starting with hidden problems.

### 9. Handovers
A useful handover states what is outstanding, current status, next action, deadline, key contacts and any important risk. “Please deal with the supplier” is weaker than “Supplier X is waiting for confirmation of PO 418; Finance requested the signed approval; manager returns at 10:00; supplier expects an update by noon.”

### 10. Improve workflows
If the same administrative problem occurs repeatedly, examine the process rather than treating every occurrence as unrelated. Repeated missing attachments may indicate that an email template or checklist is needed. Repeated booking conflicts may show that calendar permissions or scheduling rules are unclear.

Administrators can contribute to improvement by documenting patterns and suggesting practical controls through the appropriate manager.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
At 08:30 an administrator receives a request to arrange a courier collection by 15:00. The package will only be ready after a colleague supplies signed documents.

A controlled workflow records the collection request and deadline, confirms the courier requirements, records the signed documents as a dependency, follows up before the cut-off time, books the collection once requirements are satisfied, communicates the booking details and retains the reference number. The task is closed only after the required outcome has been confirmed according to procedure.

## Practical Activity
Design a daily task register with these columns: Task, Requester, Date Received, Deadline, Priority, Dependency, Next Action, Status and Follow-up Time. Populate it with at least eight realistic office tasks. At least two tasks must be waiting on another person and one must require escalation.

Then write a five-step end-of-day routine for reviewing the register.

## Knowledge Check
1. What are the basic elements of a workflow?
2. Why should administrators capture requests instead of relying only on memory?
3. What is the difference between “completed” and “closed” in a controlled workflow?
4. What is a dependency?
5. Why are recurring tasks suitable for checklists?
6. What should you do before switching away from an interrupted task?
7. What information makes a handover useful?
8. What should happen when the same workflow problem occurs repeatedly?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. A trigger, required actions or decisions and a defined completion point.
2. Capturing work reduces forgotten commitments and makes deadlines and status visible.
3. Completed means the required work is done; closed can additionally confirm communication and record filing where required.
4. Something another person or event must provide before the task can continue.
5. The same important controls must be completed repeatedly, so a checklist reduces reliance on memory.
6. Record the current position and next action so work can be resumed accurately.
7. Outstanding item, current status, next action, deadline, relevant contacts and risks.
8. Review the process, identify the root cause and propose an appropriate control or improvement.

## Key Takeaways
- Administrative workflows turn scattered requests into controlled work.
- Daily routines create visibility over deadlines and outstanding tasks.
- Dependencies must remain tracked until resolved.
- Checklists strengthen recurring processes.
- Handovers should transfer actionable information, not vague instructions.
- End-of-day review prevents hidden work from becoming tomorrow's crisis.
- Repeated failures should trigger process improvement.
