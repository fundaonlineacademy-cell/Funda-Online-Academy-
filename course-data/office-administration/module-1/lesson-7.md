# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 1: Office Administration Foundations
### Lesson 7: Professional Office Etiquette

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Apply professional etiquette in face-to-face, telephone and digital interactions.
2. Introduce people and receive visitors appropriately.
3. Use shared office spaces and communication channels respectfully.
4. Recognise etiquette problems that can damage trust or confidentiality.
5. Adapt professional behaviour to different workplace contexts.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Office etiquette is the practical behaviour that makes professional interaction respectful, efficient and comfortable. Administrators are often highly visible representatives of an organisation. Their tone, responsiveness and judgement influence how customers, colleagues and managers experience the workplace.

Etiquette is not about rigid formality. It is about understanding context, respecting other people's time and boundaries, and communicating in a way that supports the organisation.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Etiquette** — Accepted standards of courteous and appropriate behaviour.
- **Professional tone** — Communication that is respectful, clear and suitable for the workplace.
- **Courtesy** — Consideration and respect shown to others.
- **Discretion** — Careful judgement about what should be said, shared or discussed.
- **Shared space** — A workplace area or resource used by multiple people.
- **Netiquette** — Appropriate behaviour in digital communication.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. First impressions
Reception areas, telephone calls and email replies may be a person's first contact with an organisation. A professional greeting should acknowledge the person, identify the organisation or employee where appropriate and establish how assistance can be provided.

Professional warmth is valuable, but avoid becoming so informal that important information is lost or boundaries become unclear.

### 2. Receiving visitors
A visitor should be acknowledged promptly. Follow workplace procedures for identity, appointments, access, visitor records and host notification. Do not announce confidential details loudly across a reception area.

If a host is delayed, provide an appropriate update rather than leaving the visitor uncertain. Never promise access that you are not authorised to provide.

### 3. Introductions
When introducing people, use names and roles accurately. If you are uncertain how someone prefers to be addressed, a polite question is better than guessing repeatedly.

An administrator supporting a meeting should know the key attendees and be prepared to guide visitors without pretending to know information they do not have.

### 4. Telephone etiquette
Answer according to organisational standards. Listen before transferring the caller. Confirm names, numbers and messages accurately. Explain before placing someone on hold and avoid unnecessary transfers.

If the requested employee is unavailable, offer the approved alternatives: take a message, identify another appropriate contact or explain when an update can reasonably be expected.

### 5. Email and messaging etiquette
Use a clear subject line, appropriate greeting, concise message and professional closing where suitable. Check recipients and attachments before sending. Avoid copying large groups unnecessarily.

Instant messaging can be less formal than email, but professionalism still applies. Do not assume that a colleague is available simply because a status indicator shows online.

### 6. Meetings and shared spaces
Arrive prepared and on time. Avoid interrupting meetings unnecessarily. Keep shared spaces orderly and consider noise levels, especially when others are concentrating or speaking with customers.

Meeting rooms should be left ready for the next users according to office procedure. Shared equipment should be returned or reported if faulty.

### 7. Respectful disagreement
Professional etiquette does not require silence when something is wrong. Raise concerns respectfully, focus on the issue and use the appropriate channel. Publicly embarrassing a colleague over a correctable mistake often damages cooperation without improving the work.

### 8. Gossip and discretion
Administrators may overhear conversations or see information because of where they sit and what they handle. Repeating private information as office gossip can destroy trust even if the information was not formally labelled confidential.

Discretion means recognising that not everything you know needs to be repeated.

### 9. Cultural and individual awareness
South African workplaces can include people from many linguistic, cultural and professional backgrounds. Avoid assumptions about names, titles, language ability or preferred forms of address. Respect organisational standards while remaining open to reasonable individual differences.

### 10. Etiquette for a PA
A PA may interact with executives, employees, clients, suppliers and external stakeholders on the same day. The PA should be confident without being arrogant, helpful without overpromising, and discreet without becoming uncommunicative.

The goal is to make interactions easier while protecting the executive's time, information and professional relationships.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A visitor arrives twenty minutes early for a meeting with a director who is still in another meeting. A weak response is: “She's busy. Sit there.” A professional response acknowledges the visitor, confirms the appointment, follows access procedure, explains that the director is currently occupied, indicates what will happen next and notifies the appropriate person of the visitor's arrival.

## Practical Activity
Write professional responses for these situations:
1. A caller asks for an employee who is unavailable.
2. A visitor becomes impatient because a meeting is running late.
3. A colleague sends a message marked urgent but gives no context.
4. You notice confidential employee information on a shared printer.
5. Two colleagues begin discussing a private workplace dispute at reception.

For each, identify the etiquette principle and any procedural or confidentiality issue involved.

## Knowledge Check

2. What should you do before giving a visitor access?
3. What information should be confirmed when taking a telephone message?
4. Why should email recipients be checked before sending?
5. Does respectful disagreement mean avoiding all disagreement?
6. Why is gossip particularly risky for administrative staff?
7. How should cultural differences be approached professionally?
8. What balance should a PA aim for when dealing with stakeholders?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Administrators are visible representatives of the organisation and their behaviour affects trust and service quality.
2. Follow the organisation's appointment, identity and access procedures.
3. At minimum, accurately capture the caller's identity, contact details, message and any relevant timing information according to procedure.
4. To prevent misdirected, unnecessary or confidential communication.
5. No. Concerns can be raised respectfully through appropriate channels.
6. Administrators often encounter private information, so indiscretion can seriously damage trust.
7. Avoid assumptions, communicate respectfully and adapt appropriately within organisational standards.
8. Confidence with courtesy, helpfulness with authority limits, and discretion with effective communication.

## Key Takeaways
- Etiquette supports both professionalism and operational efficiency.
- Visitors and callers should be acknowledged and handled according to procedure.
- Digital communication requires the same judgement as face-to-face communication.
- Shared spaces and colleagues' time deserve consideration.
- Discretion is essential for administrative trust.
- Professional behaviour should be respectful across cultural and organisational differences.
