# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 1: Office Administration Foundations
### Lesson 3: Office Structures, Roles & Reporting Lines

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain how organisational structure affects administrative work.
2. Identify common office roles and their responsibilities.
3. Interpret basic reporting lines and authority levels.
4. Route information and requests to appropriate people.
5. Distinguish reporting, collaboration and escalation relationships.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Administrators work across departments and often receive requests that belong to someone else. Knowing who is responsible for what prevents delays, unauthorised decisions and information being sent to the wrong person.

A new administrator who understands the organisation chart, reporting lines and approval structure can route work more effectively and recognise when a matter needs escalation.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Organisational structure** — The arrangement of roles, departments, responsibilities and authority in an organisation.
- **Reporting line** — The formal relationship showing who reports to whom.
- **Department** — A functional area such as finance, operations, human resources or marketing.
- **Authority** — The formal permission to make particular decisions or approve actions.
- **Delegation** — Assigning responsibility or authority for specified work to another person.
- **Escalation path** — The approved route for referring an issue beyond the current level.
- **Organogram** — A diagram showing organisational roles and reporting relationships.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Why organisations use structure
As organisations grow, one person cannot manage every decision. Work is divided among functions, teams and levels of responsibility. Structure helps employees understand who owns a process, who approves decisions and where information should go.

A small business may have an owner, administrator, sales employee and bookkeeper. A larger company may have executives, departmental managers, supervisors, specialists and several administrative roles.

### 2. Common functional areas
Typical departments include:
- **Operations** — coordinates delivery of the organisation's main products or services;
- **Finance** — manages financial records, payments, budgets and reporting;
- **Human Resources** — supports employment processes and people administration;
- **Sales and Marketing** — develops demand and customer relationships;
- **Customer Service** — handles enquiries, support and complaints;
- **Information Technology** — supports systems, devices and technology controls;
- **Administration** — coordinates documents, communication, schedules, records and office processes.

Names vary between organisations, so administrators should learn the actual structure rather than assume every company is identical.

### 3. Reporting lines
A reporting line normally identifies the person responsible for supervising an employee's work. If an administrator reports to an office manager, that manager may allocate work, review performance and approve certain requests.

Reporting to one person does not mean communicating only with that person. Administrators frequently collaborate across departments. The important point is understanding the difference between collaboration and authority.

### 4. Authority and approval
A task may require several roles. For example, an administrator may collect quotations, a department manager may recommend a supplier and an authorised finance or executive role may approve the purchase.

The administrator should not confuse preparing information with approving the decision. Good records should show who requested, checked and approved important actions where the organisation requires this.

### 5. Routing requests correctly
When a request arrives, ask:
1. What is being requested?
2. Which process or department owns it?
3. Who has authority to decide?
4. Is there a deadline or urgency?
5. What information must accompany the referral?
6. Does the request contain confidential information?

Forwarding every problem to the most senior person is not good escalation. Routine issues should follow the normal process unless urgency, risk or policy requires otherwise.

### 6. Collaboration across departments
Administrative work often crosses boundaries. Planning a staff meeting may involve HR for attendance information, IT for equipment, finance for approved costs and facilities for the venue.

A skilled administrator coordinates these contributions while keeping ownership clear. “I sent the email” is not enough if the administrator is responsible for coordinating the final result.

### 7. Delegation
Managers may delegate tasks, but delegation should be understood clearly. The administrator needs to know the required result, deadline, available authority and any limits.

If a manager says, “Please organise refreshments for Friday's meeting within the approved budget,” the administrator may have authority to make routine arrangements. That does not automatically authorise changing the meeting venue or approving an unrelated expense.

### 8. Escalation paths
Escalation is appropriate when the issue exceeds authority, carries significant risk, remains unresolved, affects safety or confidentiality, or requires a decision from a higher level.

Useful escalation is concise. State the issue, what has already been done, what decision is required and any deadline. Avoid sending a long history without identifying what the receiving person needs to decide.

### 9. The PA and organisational awareness
A Personal Assistant needs strong organisational awareness because executives interact with many stakeholders. The PA should know key reporting relationships, major responsibilities and appropriate communication routes without pretending to hold the executive's authority.

This awareness helps the PA protect executive time by resolving routine routing questions while escalating matters that genuinely require executive attention.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A supplier contacts reception asking why an invoice has not been paid. The receptionist should not promise payment tomorrow without authority. A better workflow is to record the supplier and invoice details, check the approved internal route, refer the query to accounts payable or the responsible finance contact, provide the supplier with an appropriate acknowledgement, and escalate if the matter is urgent or remains unresolved according to procedure.

The administrator supports resolution without making a financial commitment they are not authorised to make.

## Practical Activity
Draw a simple organogram for an imaginary company containing: Managing Director, Operations Manager, Finance Manager, HR Officer, Sales Team, Office Administrator, Receptionist and Personal Assistant. Then route these requests to the most appropriate role:
- leave-form question;
- unpaid supplier invoice;
- customer sales enquiry;
- request for a meeting with the Managing Director;
- broken office printer;
- operational delivery delay.

For each, state whether you would resolve, refer or escalate it.

## Knowledge Check

2. What is a reporting line?
3. Why is collaboration different from authority?
4. What should an administrator establish before routing a request?
5. Why is sending every issue directly to the Managing Director poor administration?
6. What information should be clear when work is delegated?
7. When is escalation appropriate?
8. Why does a PA need good organisational awareness?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. It clarifies roles, responsibilities, relationships and authority.
2. It shows the formal supervision relationship between roles.
3. Employees may work together without having authority to approve each other's decisions.
4. The issue, process owner, decision authority, urgency and necessary supporting information.
5. It bypasses normal processes, wastes senior time and may delay the person who can actually resolve the issue.
6. The required result, deadline, authority and limits.
7. When a matter exceeds authority, carries significant risk, remains unresolved or requires a higher-level decision.
8. It helps route stakeholders and information correctly while protecting executive time and authority boundaries.

## Key Takeaways
- Organisational structure guides responsibility and decision-making.
- Reporting lines do not prevent cross-department collaboration.
- Administrators must distinguish preparation and coordination from approval authority.
- Requests should be routed to the correct process owner.
- Escalation should be purposeful and provide the information needed for a decision.
- Organisational awareness becomes increasingly important in PA work.
