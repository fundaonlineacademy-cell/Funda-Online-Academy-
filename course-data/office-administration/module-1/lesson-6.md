# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 1: Office Administration Foundations
### Lesson 6: Work Prioritisation & Time Management

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Distinguish urgency from importance.
2. Prioritise administrative work using deadlines, impact, dependencies and authority.
3. Plan realistic daily work while allowing for interruptions.
4. Manage competing requests professionally.
5. Escalate workload conflicts before commitments fail.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Office administrators rarely receive one task at a time. A visitor may arrive while the telephone rings, a manager may request a document and an existing deadline may already be approaching. Effective time management is therefore not about doing everything immediately. It is about making sound choices, keeping commitments visible and communicating when priorities conflict.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Priority** — The relative order in which work should receive attention.
- **Urgency** — How soon action is required.
- **Importance** — The significance or impact of the work.
- **Deadline** — The time by which an action or result is required.
- **Dependency** — Something that must occur before other work can proceed.
- **Time block** — A planned period reserved for a type of work.
- **Buffer** — Unallocated time kept available for interruptions or unexpected work.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Urgent does not always mean most important
A loudly requested task can feel urgent while a quiet deadline carries greater organisational impact. Administrators should consider more than the requester's tone.

Ask: What is the deadline? What happens if this is late? Who or what depends on it? Is there a legal, financial, customer or operational risk? Has a manager assigned a priority? Can part of the task be delegated or scheduled?

### 2. Use a practical priority method
One useful approach groups work into:
- **Immediate:** requires prompt action because delay creates significant impact;
- **Scheduled high priority:** important work with a known deadline that needs protected time;
- **Routine:** necessary recurring work that can be planned;
- **Waiting:** work blocked by a dependency but still requiring follow-up.

Priority labels should guide action, not become permanent decorations on a task list.

### 3. Plan around fixed commitments
Start with immovable commitments such as meetings, visitor appointments and submission deadlines. Then allocate time for important preparation work. Leave some buffer for calls, unexpected requests and short operational problems.

Planning every minute of an interruption-heavy role usually produces an unrealistic schedule.

### 4. Break large tasks into next actions
“Prepare quarterly meeting” is too broad for daily control. Break it into actions such as confirm attendees, request reports, draft agenda, reserve venue, compile pack and confirm equipment.

Clear next actions make progress easier to track and reveal dependencies earlier.

### 5. Manage email rather than living inside it
Constantly switching to every new email can destroy concentration. Unless the role requires continuous inbox monitoring, process messages at sensible intervals while keeping truly urgent channels clear.

Use flags, folders, tasks or approved workflow tools where useful, but avoid creating a complicated system that takes more time to maintain than the work itself.

### 6. Handle competing requests
Suppose two managers request work for the same deadline and both tasks cannot realistically be completed. Do not silently choose one and allow the other to fail. Clarify urgency, explain the conflict and obtain prioritisation from the appropriate authority where necessary.

A professional message might state the two commitments, estimated time and decision required. This gives the manager useful information rather than simply saying, “I am too busy.”

### 7. Protect focused work
Document preparation, reconciliations and detailed records may require uninterrupted concentration. Where the workplace permits, reserve a suitable period, reduce unnecessary notifications and tell relevant colleagues how urgent matters should reach you.

### 8. Avoid false productivity
Being busy is not the same as producing useful results. Repeatedly reorganising a task list, checking messages every minute or attending unnecessary meetings can consume time without advancing important work.

Review outcomes: Which commitments were completed? Which remain blocked? What created delays? What should change tomorrow?

### 9. Escalate capacity problems early
A workload can genuinely exceed available time. Early escalation allows reprioritisation, deadline changes, delegation or additional support. Late escalation reduces options.

Provide facts: current commitments, deadlines, estimated work and the conflict requiring a decision.

### 10. PA-level priority management
A PA may manage not only personal workload but also an executive's time. This requires understanding strategic priorities, protecting preparation time and recognising that a calendar slot has an opportunity cost. Those advanced skills are developed later in the course, but they begin with disciplined personal time management.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
At 09:00 you have three tasks: prepare a meeting pack for 14:00, order routine stationery by the end of the week and respond to a customer complaint that has already been escalated and requires an update by 10:00.

A sensible sequence is to confirm the complaint facts and provide/escalate the required update first because of the near deadline and customer impact; then protect sufficient time to prepare and check the 14:00 meeting pack; schedule the stationery order for an appropriate routine period. If the complaint becomes complex enough to threaten the meeting pack deadline, communicate the conflict early.

## Practical Activity
Create a list of twelve office tasks with different deadlines and impacts. Assign each to Immediate, Scheduled High Priority, Routine or Waiting. For the five highest-priority tasks, write the next action, estimated time and any dependency. Then create a realistic one-day schedule that includes at least one buffer period.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is the difference between urgency and importance?
2. Why should priority consider impact and dependencies?
3. Why is it useful to break large tasks into next actions?
4. What is a buffer in daily planning?
5. What should you do when two authorised requests conflict and both cannot be completed on time?
6. Why can constant email checking reduce productivity?
7. What information makes workload escalation useful?
8. Why is personal prioritisation an important foundation for PA work?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Urgency concerns how soon action is needed; importance concerns significance or impact.
2. A task can affect other work or create serious consequences even if the requester is not demanding immediate attention.
3. Next actions make work clearer, easier to schedule and easier to track.
4. Time intentionally left available for interruptions or unexpected work.
5. Clarify the conflict and seek prioritisation or another authorised solution early.
6. Frequent switching interrupts concentration and may delay important focused work.
7. Current commitments, deadlines, estimated effort and the specific priority conflict.
8. A PA eventually helps control executive priorities and must first demonstrate disciplined control of their own work.

## Key Takeaways
- Urgent and important are related but not identical.
- Priorities should reflect deadlines, impact, dependencies and authority.
- Fixed commitments and focused work need planned time.
- Buffers make schedules more realistic.
- Competing requests should be clarified rather than silently allowed to fail.
- Capacity problems should be escalated early with useful facts.
- Effective PA support begins with strong personal workload control.
