# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 1: Office Administration Foundations
### Lesson 5: Office Policies, Procedures & Standard Operating Procedures

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Distinguish a policy, procedure and standard operating procedure (SOP).
2. Explain why administrators must work from current approved instructions.
3. Follow a procedure while recognising exceptions that require escalation.
4. Identify weaknesses in unclear or outdated administrative procedures.
5. Apply document-control habits to workplace instructions.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Administrators make many routine decisions every day. Policies and procedures reduce guesswork by explaining organisational expectations and approved ways of working. They help employees produce consistent results even when different people perform the task.

However, following a procedure does not mean switching off judgement. An administrator must know when the normal process applies, when information is missing and when an unusual situation should be escalated.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Policy** — A formal statement of organisational rules, principles or expectations.
- **Procedure** — A defined method or sequence for completing a process.
- **SOP** — A detailed standard operating procedure for performing recurring work consistently.
- **Document control** — Managing approved versions, ownership, review and distribution of controlled documents.
- **Exception** — A situation that does not fit the normal procedure.
- **Approval** — Formal authorisation for an action or decision.
- **Effective date** — The date from which an approved instruction applies.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Policy, procedure and SOP
A policy explains what the organisation requires or the principle it follows. A procedure explains how a process should normally be completed. An SOP often gives more detailed operational steps for a recurring activity.

For example, an organisation may have a visitor-security policy stating that visitors must be controlled. The reception procedure may explain registration and host notification. A detailed SOP may show exactly how the visitor-management system is used.

### 2. Why standardisation matters
If every administrator invents a different process, the organisation receives inconsistent results. Standard procedures can improve quality, training, accountability, continuity and auditability.

They are especially useful for recurring work such as receiving visitors, processing incoming documents, booking meeting rooms, issuing stationery, filing records and handing over duties.

### 3. Use the current approved version
Old procedures can be dangerous. A previous version may contain an outdated approval limit, contact person or system instruction. Controlled procedures should therefore have identifiable ownership and version information where appropriate.

Administrators should use approved locations rather than saving uncontrolled copies on personal desktops and assuming they remain current forever.

### 4. Read before acting
Do not rely only on what another employee remembers. For unfamiliar or high-impact processes, consult the current instruction. Identify required inputs, approvals, deadlines, records and exceptions.

If a step is unclear, ask the process owner rather than inventing an unofficial rule.

### 5. Procedures and authority
A procedure may permit an administrator to perform certain routine actions but require approval for others. For example, an administrator may be allowed to order ordinary stationery from an approved supplier up to a specified internal limit, while larger or unusual purchases require authorisation.

Never assume that performing the administrative work means owning the approval decision.

### 6. Handle exceptions deliberately
Real workplaces produce situations that procedures did not anticipate. Examples include a visitor whose host cannot be reached, an urgent document received after a normal cut-off, a system outage or a request from a senior employee that conflicts with the written process.

Do not quietly bypass the procedure. Identify the conflict and use the approved escalation route. Seniority does not automatically cancel organisational controls.

### 7. Keep evidence where required
Some procedures require evidence such as an approval email, signed form, reference number, register entry or system record. Evidence supports continuity and accountability.

The amount of documentation should match the process. Routine low-risk work may need minimal records, while financial, security or confidential processes may require stronger controls.

### 8. Suggest improvements appropriately
Administrators often notice procedure problems first because they perform the process repeatedly. A procedure may contain duplicate steps, an outdated telephone number or unclear responsibility.

Do not silently rewrite an approved procedure unless authorised. Record the issue and propose the improvement to the owner. Once approved, the controlled version can be updated and communicated.

### 9. Training and handover
Procedures support onboarding and continuity. A new employee should be able to understand the normal process without depending entirely on one colleague's memory.

A good handover identifies where approved procedures are stored and highlights current exceptions or temporary arrangements.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A receptionist receives a visitor who says the Managing Director personally invited them, but there is no appointment in the calendar. The visitor asks to be allowed through because “the MD knows me.”

If procedure requires visitor verification, the receptionist should not bypass it because the visitor sounds confident. The receptionist can verify identity as required, contact the host or authorised alternative and escalate according to the visitor procedure. Professional service and procedural control can operate together.

## Practical Activity
Choose one routine office process, such as booking a meeting room, receiving courier deliveries or ordering stationery. Write:
1. the purpose;
2. required inputs;
3. five to eight normal steps;
4. required approvals;
5. records or evidence;
6. two possible exceptions;
7. the escalation route;
8. how an employee confirms they are using the current version.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is the difference between a policy and a procedure?
2. Why should administrators use current approved instructions?
3. What should you do if a procedure is unclear?
4. Does carrying out a process automatically give approval authority?
5. What is an exception?
6. Why might a process require evidence?
7. Should an administrator silently change an approved procedure that seems inefficient?
8. How do procedures support business continuity?

2. Old versions may contain outdated controls, contacts or requirements.
3. Clarify with the appropriate process owner or authorised person.
4. No. Operational responsibility and decision authority can be separate.
5. A situation that does not fit the normal process or conditions.
6. Evidence supports accountability, continuity and verification that required actions occurred.

8. They preserve approved working knowledge so tasks do not depend only on one person's memory.

## Key Takeaways
- Policies explain organisational requirements; procedures and SOPs explain how work is carried out.
- Use current approved instructions rather than memory or uncontrolled copies.
- Procedures do not remove authority limits.
- Exceptions should be identified and escalated rather than hidden.
- Required evidence forms part of the workflow.
- Administrators can improve procedures by raising documented problems through the correct process.
