# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 6: Personal Assistant Skills
### Lesson 7: Managing Competing Priorities Under Pressure

**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Reassess priorities when new urgent work arrives.
2. Distinguish genuine urgency from pressure or poor planning.
3. Communicate trade-offs and capacity limits professionally.
4. Escalate competing high-impact commitments with useful options.
5. Maintain accuracy and professionalism during busy periods.

## Why This Lesson Matters
PAs often work where several important people need something at the same time. Pressure can lead to rushed promises, hidden errors and constant task-switching. Professional priority management creates enough structure to protect the most important outcomes without pretending that every request can be completed immediately.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Competing priority** — Important task or commitment that conflicts with another demand for limited time or resources.
- **Trade-off** — Consequence of choosing one option over another.
- **Capacity** — Realistic amount of work that can be completed within available time and resources.
- **Reprioritisation** — Deliberate change to task order or timing based on new information.
- **Critical path** — Sequence of dependent work that directly affects completion of an important outcome.
- **Pressure request** — Request presented as urgent, though the actual business impact must still be assessed.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Reassess, do not merely add
When new urgent work arrives, do not simply place it on top of an already full workload. Ask what must move, what can be delegated and what deadline is truly fixed.

### 2. Test urgency
Useful questions include: What happens if this waits? Who is affected? Is there a fixed external deadline? Does another task depend on it? Has authority confirmed the priority?

### 3. Protect high-impact work
A board submission, critical client issue or travel deadline may deserve priority over lower-impact tasks. Keep the business consequence visible rather than responding only to whoever asks most forcefully.

### 4. Communicate trade-offs
If two high-priority tasks cannot both be completed by the requested time, explain the conflict early. “I can finalise the board pack by 14:00 or complete the supplier comparison by 13:30; both cannot be completed accurately without moving one deadline. Which should take priority?”

### 5. Delegate appropriately
Route work to capable authorised colleagues when appropriate. Delegation still requires clarity, timing and follow-up. Do not dump work without context.

### 6. Reduce task-switching
Group similar low-risk tasks and protect focused blocks for complex work. Constant switching increases errors and slows completion.

### 7. Use status visibility
Maintain a simple task view showing critical, scheduled, waiting and completed work. Visibility reduces the mental burden of remembering everything.

### 8. Preserve quality under pressure
Urgency does not remove the need to check names, dates, attachments, approvals or confidentiality. Use short checklists for high-risk tasks when time is limited.

### 9. Escalate capacity risk early
If the workload exceeds realistic capacity, raise it before commitments fail. Provide facts: work outstanding, deadlines, impact and options for reprioritisation or support.

### 10. Recover after peak periods
After an intense period, review delayed routine work and reset the task list. Do not allow urgent work to permanently hide lower-priority commitments that still matter.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A PA must send a board pack by 15:00, arrange same-day travel after a flight cancellation and prepare routine monthly stationery orders. Travel disruption and board pack are high-impact with fixed timing; stationery can move. The PA protects the first two, communicates the stationery delay and avoids trying to complete all three simultaneously with poor quality.

## Practical Activity
List ten tasks with different deadlines and impacts. At 10:00, add three new urgent requests. Re-rank the full list, identify what moves, what can be delegated and which conflict requires executive confirmation. Write one capacity-risk message.

## Knowledge Check
1. What should happen when new urgent work enters a full schedule?
2. How can genuine urgency be tested?
3. Why should loud or senior requesters not automatically determine priority?
4. What is a trade-off?
5. How can task-switching be reduced?
6. Why should quality checks continue under pressure?
7. What should a capacity escalation contain?
8. Why review routine work after a peak period?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Reassess existing work and decide what moves, delegates or escalates.
2. Check impact, fixed deadlines, dependencies and authorised priority.
3. Business consequence matters more than pressure alone.
4. The consequence of choosing one option over another.
5. Group similar tasks and protect focused work blocks.
6. Errors under pressure can create greater delays and risk.
7. Outstanding work, deadlines, impact and realistic options/support needed.
8. Lower-priority tasks may still require completion after urgent work subsides.

## Key Takeaways
- New urgency requires reprioritisation, not unlimited workload growth.
- Test impact and deadlines before treating a request as critical.
- Communicate unavoidable trade-offs early.
- Reduce unnecessary task-switching.
- Protect quality and confidentiality even under pressure.
