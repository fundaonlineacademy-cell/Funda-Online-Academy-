# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 6: Personal Assistant Skills
### Lesson 4: Gatekeeping & Stakeholder Coordination

**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain gatekeeping as a professional coordination function.
2. Assess requests according to purpose, urgency and executive priorities.
3. Route stakeholders without unnecessary obstruction.
4. Protect executive time and confidential information.
5. Escalate exceptions when authority or risk requires it.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A PA often becomes the main access point to a manager or executive. Poor gatekeeping can either overwhelm the executive with routine interruptions or frustrate important stakeholders unnecessarily. Effective gatekeeping protects time while helping legitimate requests reach the right outcome.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Gatekeeping** — Managing access, requests and interruptions according to priorities and authorised instructions.
- **Stakeholder** — Person or group with a legitimate interest in the executive's work or decisions.
- **Screening** — Establishing enough information to route a request appropriately.
- **Escalation** — Referral to higher authority when the issue exceeds the PA's authority or carries significant risk.
- **Alternative route** — Another authorised person, process or time that can address the request.
- **Access boundary** — Agreed limit on when and how the executive can be contacted.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Gatekeeping is not blocking
The goal is not to make access difficult. It is to distinguish what truly needs executive attention from what can be resolved elsewhere, scheduled later or prepared before escalation.

### 2. Understand standing instructions
A PA should know which stakeholders may receive priority access, which matters can interrupt meetings, what can be delegated and which requests must always be referred to the executive.

### 3. Screen professionally
Ask enough to understand purpose, timing, required decision and relevant context. Avoid intrusive questioning that is unrelated to the work.

### 4. Offer alternatives
If the executive is unavailable, identify another authorised route where possible: a manager, department, later appointment, written briefing or callback.

### 5. Protect confidential information
Do not explain private reasons for unavailability or disclose diary details merely to justify a decision. “The director is unavailable at that time; I can offer 15:30 or arrange a response from Operations” may be sufficient.

### 6. Handle pressure
Important-sounding stakeholders may insist on immediate access. Remain courteous, apply the agreed process and avoid being pressured into inventing authority.

### 7. Recognise genuine urgency
Some matters do require interruption: material safety issues, critical customer incidents or other events defined by executive instructions. A good PA knows the escalation triggers.

### 8. Coordinate competing stakeholders
Where several people require executive input, collect the necessary information and help organise priorities. This may involve grouping related matters, preparing decision summaries or identifying which issue has the earliest real deadline.

### 9. Record commitments
If you promise a callback, meeting option or response, record and follow it. Gatekeeping that filters requests but fails follow-through damages trust.

### 10. Maintain relationships
A stakeholder should feel professionally handled even when the answer is not immediate access. Clear expectations, accurate routing and respectful communication protect long-term relationships.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A supplier insists on speaking to the CEO about an unpaid invoice. The PA establishes the invoice reference and urgency, sees that Finance owns the process and confirms the matter is already under review. Rather than interrupting the CEO immediately, the PA routes the issue to Finance, sets a follow-up point and explains the next step. If the invoice threatens a critical service, the PA escalates according to the executive's instructions.

## Practical Activity
Create a gatekeeping decision guide for ten request types, including client complaint, media call, family call, supplier issue, routine sales pitch, safety incident and board member request. For each, indicate: immediate access, schedule, route elsewhere, gather more information or escalate.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is the purpose of gatekeeping?
2. Why should a PA know standing instructions?
3. What information should screening establish?
4. Why are alternative routes useful?
5. What diary information should not be disclosed casually?
6. How should pressure from a demanding stakeholder be handled?
7. What makes an interruption genuinely urgent?
8. Why must promised callbacks be recorded?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. To protect executive attention while routing legitimate requests appropriately.
2. They define priority access, delegation and escalation boundaries.
3. Purpose, timing, required action/decision and relevant context.
4. They help resolve legitimate needs without unnecessary executive interruption.
5. Private reasons for unavailability, sensitive meeting details and other restricted schedule information.
6. Stay courteous, apply the authorised process and do not invent authority.
7. It meets an agreed high-impact or risk trigger, not merely a stakeholder's insistence.
8. Follow-through is essential to maintain trust and prevent lost commitments.

## Key Takeaways
- Gatekeeping should protect time without becoming obstruction.
- Use standing instructions and clear escalation triggers.
- Screen requests with relevant questions only.
- Offer authorised alternatives where possible.
- Protect confidential schedule information.
- Gatekeeping is incomplete without follow-up.
