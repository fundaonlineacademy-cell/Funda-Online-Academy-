# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 6: Personal Assistant Skills
### Lesson 5: Preparing Briefings, Documents & Executive Information

**Estimated study time:** 50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Prepare concise executive briefings from larger information sets.
2. Distinguish facts, options, risks and decisions required.
3. Verify information before presenting it to a manager.
4. Structure supporting documents for fast review.
5. Protect confidential and sensitive executive information.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Executives often receive more information than they can process in detail. A PA adds value by organising information so the manager can understand the issue quickly without losing important facts. The goal is not to hide complexity, but to present it clearly.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Executive briefing** — Concise summary designed to help a manager understand, prepare or decide.
- **Decision required** — Specific choice or approval needed from the executive.
- **Key fact** — Verified information that materially affects the issue.
- **Option** — Available course of action.
- **Risk** — Potential negative consequence or uncertainty relevant to the decision.
- **Recommendation** — Suggested option based on available information, where the PA is expected and authorised to provide one.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Start with the purpose
Clarify why the executive needs the information. Is it for awareness, a meeting, an approval, a negotiation or a decision? The purpose determines what belongs in the briefing.

### 2. Lead with the issue
Do not force the executive to read two pages before learning what the matter is. A useful opening may state the issue, deadline and decision required in a few lines.

### 3. Separate facts from assumptions
Verify figures, dates, names and sources. If something remains uncertain, label it as pending confirmation rather than presenting it as fact.

### 4. Present options clearly
Where a decision is required, identify realistic options and their major implications. Avoid overwhelming the executive with every theoretical possibility.

### 5. Show deadlines and consequences
If a choice must be made by 15:00 to secure a booking, make that visible. Time-sensitive decisions should not be buried in background detail.

### 6. Use supporting documents intelligently
Attach or link the relevant source documents. Use clear filenames and identify which pages or sections matter. Do not send an unorganised bundle of attachments and expect the executive to reconstruct the issue.

### 7. Keep briefings concise but complete
Concise does not mean incomplete. Include enough background to understand the decision, but remove repetition and irrelevant detail.

### 8. Review for bias
A PA may have a preferred option, but should not manipulate facts to force it. Present material trade-offs honestly and distinguish recommendation from fact.

### 9. Confidentiality
Briefings may contain sensitive financial, employee, customer or strategic information. Store and distribute them through authorised systems, using access controls appropriate to the content.

### 10. Prepare for questions
Before presenting the briefing, anticipate obvious questions: cost, deadline, stakeholder impact, previous decision, alternative and source. Have the supporting evidence ready.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
An executive must choose between two venues for a client event. Instead of forwarding four long email chains, the PA prepares a one-page briefing: event requirement, decision deadline, Venue A cost/capacity/cancellation terms, Venue B cost/capacity/cancellation terms, key risk, recommendation if requested, and links to both quotations.

## Practical Activity
Take a fictional 15-message email thread about choosing an office supplier. Create a one-page executive briefing with headings: Issue, Decision Required, Deadline, Key Facts, Options, Risks, Recommendation (if appropriate) and Supporting Documents.

## Knowledge Check
1. What should determine the content of an executive briefing?
2. Why should the issue and decision required appear early?
3. How should unverified information be presented?
4. What makes an option useful in a briefing?
5. Why should deadlines be highly visible?
6. How should supporting documents be organised?
7. What is the risk of presenting a recommendation as if it were fact?
8. Why should a PA anticipate questions before presenting a briefing?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. The purpose for which the executive needs the information.
2. Executives need to understand quickly what requires attention or action.
3. Clearly label it as pending or uncertain rather than as confirmed fact.
4. It is realistic, relevant and includes major implications.
5. Missing a decision window can make an otherwise correct briefing useless.
6. Link or attach only relevant documents with clear identification and references.
7. It can distort decision-making and undermine trust.
8. Preparation speeds discussion and ensures evidence is available.

## Key Takeaways
- Executive information should be decision-ready.
- Lead with the issue, deadline and required action.
- Verify facts and label uncertainty clearly.
- Present realistic options and material risks.
- Organise supporting evidence so it is easy to use.
