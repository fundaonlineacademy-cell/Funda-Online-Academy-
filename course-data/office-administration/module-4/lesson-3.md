# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 4: Customer & Office Front Desk
### Lesson 3: Handling Calls, Messages & Enquiries

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Triage front-desk calls and enquiries efficiently.
2. Capture accurate messages and service requests.
3. Route enquiries to the correct person or department.
4. Protect confidential information while assisting callers and visitors.
5. Track unresolved enquiries to appropriate closure.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Front-desk enquiries often arrive without perfect information. A caller may know only a first name, a visitor may ask for “accounts,” or a customer may describe a problem without a reference number. The receptionist's task is to gather enough information to route the matter correctly without wasting time or exposing unnecessary information.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Triage** — Quickly assessing an enquiry so it can be prioritised and routed appropriately.
- **Enquiry** — Request for information, assistance or action.
- **Message record** — Captured information needed for another person to respond.
- **Routing** — Directing an enquiry to the correct function or person.
- **Callback commitment** — Agreed expectation that someone will respond later.
- **Closure** — Confirmation that the enquiry has reached an appropriate outcome or handoff.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Establish the request
Start by identifying who is contacting the organisation and what they need. Ask concise questions that help route the enquiry. Avoid asking for information that is unrelated to the task.

### 2. Distinguish information from action
Some enquiries can be answered with approved public information, such as office hours or a general email address. Others require an employee to take action, approve something or access restricted records.

Do not treat every question as permission to disclose internal information.

### 3. Route accurately
Know the broad responsibilities of departments and common referral paths. Sending every unclear enquiry to the same senior manager is not efficient.

When uncertain, check an approved directory or ask the relevant internal contact rather than guessing.

### 4. Capture usable messages
A message should allow the recipient to act without having to reconstruct the conversation. Capture name, organisation where relevant, contact details, reference number, purpose, urgency/deadline and any promised next step.

### 5. Avoid false promises
Reception can promise actions within its control, such as forwarding a message or providing an update. It should not guarantee that another employee will approve a refund, call within ten minutes or accept a proposal unless that commitment is authorised.

### 6. Handle repeated enquiries
If a person has already contacted the organisation several times, acknowledge the history and check existing records where authorised. Repeatedly making the customer start from zero creates poor service and can hide a breakdown in follow-up.

### 7. Sensitive enquiries
Requests concerning employee whereabouts, salaries, disciplinary matters, customer records or executive schedules may be restricted. Use authorised verification and referral procedures.

### 8. Prioritise risk and urgency
An ordinary request for a brochure and a call reporting a possible safety incident are not equal. Understand escalation triggers so high-risk enquiries reach the correct person quickly.

### 9. Track unresolved enquiries
A transferred or forwarded enquiry is not necessarily closed. Where the front-desk process includes follow-up, update the message log or task register until the handoff or outcome is confirmed.

### 10. Handover
Outstanding callbacks, expected return calls and unresolved visitor enquiries should be included in handover notes so service does not reset when staff change shifts.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A customer calls saying they have emailed Accounts twice about an invoice but received no response. The receptionist captures the invoice reference and contact details, checks the approved routing path, records that prior attempts were made, sends the enquiry to the responsible function and sets the required follow-up or escalation point. The customer is not simply told to “email again.”

## Practical Activity
Create an enquiry log with fields for date/time, stakeholder, contact, reference, request, department/owner, priority, action taken, follow-up date, status and outcome. Add six realistic enquiries and identify which require immediate escalation.

## Knowledge Check
1. What does triage mean at a front desk?
2. Why should questions be limited to relevant information?
3. What makes a message usable?
4. Why should reception avoid promising another employee's decision?
5. How should repeated enquiries be handled?
6. Name two examples of sensitive enquiries.
7. Why are urgency and risk different from ordinary queue order?
8. What should happen to unresolved enquiries at handover?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Quickly assessing the enquiry so it can be prioritised and routed correctly.
2. To respect privacy, save time and avoid collecting unnecessary information.
3. It contains sufficient accurate details, required action and timing for the recipient to respond.
4. Reception normally does not control that person's authority or availability.
5. Check authorised history, avoid unnecessary repetition and use the appropriate follow-up/escalation path.
6. Examples include employee personal information, executive schedules, salaries or customer account data.
7. High-risk matters may require faster escalation than routine enquiries.
8. Transfer them with enough context, status and next action for continuity.

## Key Takeaways
- Good front-desk service depends on accurate triage and routing.
- Capture messages that another person can act on.
- Do not promise outcomes outside your authority.
- Sensitive enquiries require controlled disclosure and referral.
- Follow-up and handover prevent enquiries from disappearing.
