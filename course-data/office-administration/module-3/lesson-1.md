# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 3: Documents, Filing & Records
### Lesson 1: Business Documents & Document Standards

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Identify common business documents and their administrative purposes.
2. Explain why document standards support professionalism and control.
3. Distinguish content accuracy from presentation quality.
4. Apply basic document checks before circulation.
5. Recognise when templates, approvals and records controls are required.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Documents carry decisions, instructions, evidence and organisational identity. Administrators often create or handle documents that other people rely on without seeing how they were prepared. A wrong date in an agenda, outdated price in a quotation pack or uncontrolled template can therefore create consequences far beyond appearance.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Business document** — A document created or received for organisational purposes.
- **Template** — Approved reusable structure for a document type.
- **Document standard** — Agreed requirement for content, formatting, naming or control.
- **Metadata** — Information describing a file, such as title, author, date or version.
- **Approval** — Authorised confirmation that a document or action may proceed.
- **Controlled document** — A document whose approved version and changes are managed.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Common business documents
Administrators may work with letters, memos, agendas, minutes, reports, forms, quotations, invoices, purchase documents, policies, procedures, presentations and registers. Each has a purpose and may have different approval or retention requirements.

### 2. Purpose before formatting
A document should first achieve its business purpose. Attractive formatting cannot correct inaccurate content. Identify the intended reader, required action and information that must be present.

### 3. Organisational standards
Businesses often define fonts, logos, headings, reference formats, signature blocks and templates. Consistency helps readers recognise official documents and improves professional presentation.

Do not copy branding from an old file if an approved current template exists.

### 4. Content accuracy
Check names, dates, amounts, reference numbers, calculations, attachments and factual statements. Where information comes from another source, verify that the source is current and authorised.

### 5. Presentation quality
Use readable headings, spacing, alignment and page structure. Avoid excessive decorative elements in routine business documents. Tables can improve structured information when used appropriately.

### 6. Document identity
Important documents should be easy to identify. Depending on organisational requirements, this may include title, date, reference, owner, version and status such as draft or approved.

### 7. Draft versus approved
A draft is still under development. Avoid distributing a draft as if it were final. Use clear status labels where required and follow approval processes before official release.

### 8. Templates
Templates save time and support consistency, but they can carry old information forward. Check placeholders, names, dates, addresses, version details and embedded text before use.

### 9. Accessibility and usability
Documents should be usable by the intended audience. Clear headings, meaningful file names and logical reading order improve navigation. Where organisational or accessibility requirements apply, follow them.

### 10. Final document check
Before circulation ask: Is this the correct document? Is the information accurate? Is the version current? Are recipients authorised? Has required approval been obtained? Is the file format appropriate? Is the final copy stored correctly?

## Worked Example / Demonstration
An administrator prepares a meeting agenda using last month's template. The layout is correct, but the old meeting date remains in the footer and one previous attendee remains on the distribution list. This demonstrates why templates reduce formatting effort but do not remove the need for content and recipient checks.

## Practical Activity
Select five business document types. For each, record its purpose, typical audience, critical information, likely approval requirement and one quality risk. Then create a ten-point pre-distribution document checklist.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. The document must first communicate accurate information and support the required business action.
2. Examples include letters, agendas, minutes, reports, forms, quotations and policies.
3. An agreed requirement for how documents are created, presented, named or controlled.
4. Templates may contain outdated text, placeholders or previous details.
5. A draft is still being developed; an approved document has passed the required authorisation process.
6. Examples include names, dates, amounts, references, calculations and attachments.
7. It helps users recognise the document, status, ownership and current version.
8. Accuracy, version, approval, recipients, format and correct storage/distribution requirements.

## Key Takeaways
- Business documents support real organisational actions and decisions.
- Accuracy and purpose come before appearance.
- Standards and templates improve consistency but still require checking.
- Draft and approved documents must not be confused.
- Final document control includes version, approval, recipient and storage checks.
