# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 3: Documents, Filing & Records
### Lesson 3: Filing Systems & Classification

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the purpose of an organised filing system.
2. Compare common filing and classification methods.
3. Select logical file categories and indexing rules.
4. File and retrieve records consistently.
5. Identify confidentiality and access considerations in filing.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A record has little operational value if nobody can find it when needed. Filing systems create predictable locations for information. Good filing is not simply putting documents away; it involves classification, indexing, access control, retrieval and maintenance.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Classification** — Grouping records according to defined categories.
- **Index** — A structured guide used to locate records.
- **Alphabetical filing** — Organising records by names or words in alphabetical order.
- **Numeric filing** — Organising records using assigned numbers.
- **Chronological filing** — Organising records according to dates.
- **Subject filing** — Organising records according to topics or functions.
- **Cross-reference** — A pointer directing a user to another filing location.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Purpose of filing
Filing supports retrieval, evidence, continuity and accountability. A useful system allows authorised people to predict where a record belongs and find it without depending on one employee's memory.

### 2. Alphabetical filing
Alphabetical systems work well for names such as clients or suppliers when naming rules are consistent. Decide how prefixes, company names and duplicate names are treated.

### 3. Numeric filing
Numeric systems can suit records with unique identifiers such as customer, case or invoice numbers. They often require an index so users can connect a name or subject to the correct number.

### 4. Chronological filing
Date-based filing is useful where time sequence is important, such as daily logs or monthly records. It may be combined with another classification method.

### 5. Subject and functional filing
Records can be grouped by topic or business function: HR, Finance, Suppliers, Facilities or Projects. Categories should be clearly defined so employees do not create overlapping folders for the same information.

### 6. Hybrid systems
Many workplaces combine methods. Supplier records might be classified by supplier name and then organised by financial year. The important requirement is consistency.

### 7. Indexing rules
Document the filing logic. If employees disagree whether “The Cape Office Supplies” belongs under T or C, retrieval becomes inconsistent. Define rules and train users.

### 8. Cross-references
Some records logically relate to more than one subject. Rather than creating uncontrolled duplicate originals, use approved cross-references or system links where appropriate.

### 9. Retrieval and return
Physical files removed from storage may need an out-card, register or other tracking method. Electronic systems may record access automatically. The aim is to know where an important record went and ensure it returns to its controlled location.

### 10. Confidentiality
Not every file should be available to every employee. Personnel, financial, customer or executive records may require restricted access. Classification and access control should work together.

### 11. Maintenance
Filing systems deteriorate if employees create duplicate categories, misfile records or keep obsolete material indefinitely. Periodic housekeeping should follow retention and disposal rules rather than arbitrary deletion.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
An office stores supplier records in folders called “Suppliers,” “Vendors,” “Purchasing” and “Accounts Suppliers.” Staff cannot predict where a contract belongs. The office defines a single supplier classification structure, documents naming rules and uses cross-references for related finance records. Retrieval becomes more consistent.

## Practical Activity
Design a filing structure for a small office covering Clients, Suppliers, Finance, Staff Administration, Meetings and Facilities. Choose a classification method for each area, define three naming/indexing rules and explain which areas need restricted access.

## Key Takeaways
- Filing is a controlled retrieval system, not just storage.
- Choose classification methods that fit the records and user needs.
- Consistent indexing rules are essential.
- Track important records that leave their normal location.
- Access controls must reflect confidentiality requirements.
