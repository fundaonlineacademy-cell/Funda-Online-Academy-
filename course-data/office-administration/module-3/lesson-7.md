# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 3: Documents, Filing & Records
### Lesson 7: Data Accuracy, Quality Checks & Audit Trails

**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain why data accuracy is an administrative control.
2. Apply validation and reconciliation checks to routine records.
3. Distinguish source data from derived information.
4. Correct errors without destroying required traceability.
5. Explain the purpose of an audit trail.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Small data errors can create large operational problems. One incorrect bank digit, duplicated invoice or wrong meeting date can affect payments, customers and decisions. Administrators need systematic quality checks rather than relying on the feeling that information “looks right.”

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Validation** — Checking whether data meets defined rules or expected conditions.
- **Verification** — Confirming information against a reliable source.
- **Reconciliation** — Comparing two sets of records to identify and resolve differences.
- **Source document** — Original or authoritative evidence from which information is captured.
- **Audit trail** — Traceable history showing relevant actions, changes or approvals.
- **Exception report** — List or report highlighting records that do not meet expected conditions.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Accuracy dimensions
Good information should be correct, complete, current and appropriately consistent. A correct telephone number stored against the wrong customer is still inaccurate in context.

### 2. Verify against source
Where practical, compare captured information to the authoritative source. Do not repeatedly copy from a spreadsheet that may itself contain transcription errors when the approved source is available.

### 3. Validation checks
Examples include required fields, correct date formats, reasonable numeric ranges and duplicate detection. Software can automate some validation, but human judgement remains necessary.

### 4. Reconciliation
Reconciliation compares related records. An administrator might compare a meeting attendance list with submitted forms, a supplier statement with recorded invoices, or a courier register with collected parcels.

Differences should be investigated rather than forced to match without evidence.

### 5. Spot checks and full checks
High-risk information may require complete verification, while lower-risk bulk data may use sampling or automated controls according to procedure. The checking method should match risk and organisational requirements.

### 6. Corrections
Correct errors through the authorised process. Do not erase evidence or backdate records to hide mistakes. Some systems preserve previous values automatically; paper records may have defined correction methods.

### 7. Audit trails
An audit trail may show who created, changed, approved or accessed a record and when. It supports accountability and investigation. Administrators should not share credentials because this destroys confidence in user-based audit trails.

### 8. Four-eyes checks
Certain high-impact tasks may require a second authorised person to review information. This reduces single-person error but only works when the reviewer actually checks rather than automatically approves.

### 9. Quality-check routines
Create repeatable checks for common work: names, dates, totals, references, attachments, versions, approvals and distribution lists. Checklists reduce omissions.

### 10. Escalating discrepancies
If you cannot explain a material difference, stop the affected process where appropriate and escalate. Do not invent balancing entries or change data merely to remove the discrepancy.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A supplier statement shows R18,750 outstanding while the internal record shows R16,250. The administrator does not alter either amount to make them agree. They compare invoice references, payments and credits, identify a R2,500 invoice missing from the internal list, verify the source document and refer it through the appropriate accounts process.

## Practical Activity
Create a quality checklist for a monthly supplier register with at least ten checks. Include completeness, duplicates, amounts, dates, references, source verification and approval. Then describe how you would investigate a total that differs from the source records.

## Knowledge Check
1. Name four characteristics of good administrative data.
2. What is verification?
3. What is reconciliation?
4. Why should unexplained differences not simply be forced to match?
5. What is an audit trail?
6. Why is credential sharing harmful to traceability?
7. What is a four-eyes check?
8. What should happen when a material discrepancy cannot be explained?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Correct, complete, current and consistent in the relevant context.
2. Confirming information against a reliable or authoritative source.
3. Comparing related records and investigating differences.
4. The difference may reveal a genuine error, omission or control issue.
5. A traceable history of relevant actions, changes, approvals or access.
6. Actions can no longer be reliably attributed to the actual user.
7. A control requiring an appropriate second person to review important work.
8. Pause affected action where appropriate and escalate through the correct process.

## Key Takeaways
- Accuracy requires systematic checking.
- Verify information against authoritative sources.
- Reconciliation investigates differences rather than hiding them.
- Corrections should preserve required traceability.
- Audit trails depend on accountable user actions.
