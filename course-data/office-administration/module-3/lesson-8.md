# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 3: Documents, Filing & Records
### Lesson 8: Records Management Practical Workflow

**Estimated study time:** 50–60 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Apply document and records controls across a complete workflow.
2. Classify, name, store and retrieve business records consistently.
3. Control versions and approvals during document development.
4. Apply quality, access, retention and disposal checkpoints.
5. Design a practical records workflow for a small office.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Records management is most useful when its controls work together. Naming conventions cannot compensate for poor permissions; perfect filing cannot fix an unapproved document; a retention schedule is ineffective if employees keep private duplicate archives. This lesson integrates the module into one practical workflow.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Records workflow** — Connected process controlling records from receipt or creation through final disposition.
- **Authoritative record** — Recognised official or controlled record for a business purpose.
- **Control point** — Stage where a required check, approval or decision occurs.
- **Disposition** — Final records action such as transfer to archive or authorised destruction.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Receive or create
Every workflow starts when information enters the organisation or a new document is created. Identify what the record is, who owns the process and whether it contains sensitive information.

### 2. Classify
Apply the approved category. Classification affects storage, access, retrieval and often retention. Avoid inventing a new category when an appropriate approved one already exists.

### 3. Name and identify
Apply filename or document-identification rules. Include enough information to distinguish the record from similar items while keeping names manageable.

### 4. Draft and collaborate
For working documents, use the approved shared environment. Manage permissions and avoid unnecessary duplicate editable copies. Keep draft status clear.

### 5. Quality check
Verify critical facts, formatting, references and required attachments. Reconcile relevant data. Address exceptions before approval.

### 6. Approve
Route the correct version to the authorised approver. Preserve approval evidence where required. Do not continue editing the approved copy without following change control.

### 7. Distribute
Send or publish only to authorised recipients using the appropriate format and channel. Record distribution when the process requires it.

### 8. Store the authoritative record
Place the final record in its approved location. Remove or control obsolete working copies so users can identify the authoritative version.

### 9. Retrieve and use
Authorised users should be able to locate records using the classification and naming system. Track removal or access where procedure requires it.

### 10. Retain, archive and dispose
Apply the approved retention schedule. Move inactive records to controlled archive where appropriate. Before disposal, check authorisation and any hold. Use secure destruction for sensitive records.

### 11. Monitor the workflow
Look for recurring failures: duplicate folders, missing approvals, unclear filenames, inaccessible records or outdated active versions. Use evidence to improve the process.

### 12. PA application
A PA may control high-value executive records and correspondence. The same workflow applies, with stronger attention to confidentiality, timely retrieval and reliable version identification.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A manager asks an administrator to prepare a quarterly board briefing. The administrator uses the approved template, stores the draft in the restricted shared folder, names it consistently, collects source reports, verifies figures, controls revisions, routes the correct draft for approval, exports the authorised final pack, distributes it only to approved recipients and stores the authoritative copy. Older drafts remain controlled according to procedure rather than competing with the final pack.

## Practical Activity — Build a Records Workflow
Design a records-management workflow for a fictional South African services company. Include:
1. six record categories;
2. folder/classification structure;
3. filename rules;
4. draft/version method;
5. access levels;
6. quality checks;
7. approval point;
8. final storage location;
9. retrieval method;
10. retention-rule source field;
11. hold check;
12. secure disposal process.

Then test your workflow with a supplier contract, staff meeting minutes and customer complaint record. Identify where each enters, who should access it and what control points apply.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why must records controls work together?
2. What should happen immediately after receiving or creating an important record?
3. How does classification affect later stages?
4. Why must the correct version be sent for approval?
5. What is an authoritative record?
6. What should happen to obsolete active copies?
7. What must be checked before disposal?
8. Name three indicators that a records workflow needs improvement.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Weakness in one control can undermine the rest of the lifecycle.
2. Identify its purpose/owner, classification and sensitivity according to procedure.
3. It influences storage, access, retrieval and often retention.
4. Approval of the wrong version leaves uncertainty about what was actually authorised.
5. The recognised official or controlled record for the relevant business purpose.
6. Remove them from active use or clearly control/archive them according to procedure.
7. Retention requirements, disposal authority, holds and appropriate destruction method.
8. Examples include duplicate folders, missing approvals, unclear filenames, inaccessible records and outdated versions in active use.

## Key Takeaways
- Records management is a lifecycle, not an isolated filing task.
- Classification, naming, access, versioning, quality and approval must connect.
- The authoritative record should be identifiable and retrievable.
- Retention and disposal are controlled decisions.
- Administrators should use recurring failures to improve the workflow.
