# Office Administration Certificate: From Admin to PA
## Module 5: Diaries, Meetings & Travel
### Lesson 2: Scheduling, Priorities & Conflict Resolution

**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Assess scheduling requests against priorities, deadlines and authority.
2. Detect and resolve diary conflicts before they affect commitments.
3. Offer practical alternatives when preferred times are unavailable.
4. Escalate priority conflicts that require manager decisions.
5. Communicate schedule changes clearly to affected stakeholders.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Calendar conflicts are not solved by simply choosing the first request received. Some commitments are more important, less movable or more time-sensitive than others. Administrators need a repeatable way to assess priorities while respecting authority and relationships.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Scheduling conflict** — Two or more commitments competing for the same time or required resources.
- **Priority** — Relative importance or urgency of a commitment.
- **Constraint** — Condition limiting scheduling options, such as travel, deadlines or attendee availability.
- **Alternative slot** — Different time proposed when the preferred time cannot be used.
- **Reschedule** — Move an existing commitment to another time.
- **Escalation** — Referral of a conflict to an authorised decision-maker.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Check before accepting
Before adding a new commitment, review existing appointments, preparation time, travel, required attendees and other constraints. A visually empty time slot may not actually be available.

### 2. Understand the priority
Ask what the meeting is for, who must attend, what deadline it supports and how movable it is. A routine internal catch-up and a fixed client tender presentation are not equal simply because both are one hour long.

### 3. Identify fixed versus movable commitments
Some appointments are externally fixed, linked to regulatory or customer deadlines, or involve many difficult-to-coordinate participants. Others may be more flexible. Understand the consequences before moving anything.

### 4. Offer alternatives
When the requested time is unavailable, propose practical options rather than only saying no. Offer one or two realistic alternatives that preserve travel, preparation and attendee requirements.

### 5. Resolve double bookings
If a double booking occurs, establish how it happened, determine which commitments can move and obtain the necessary approval. Communicate the change to all affected people promptly.

### 6. Priority conflicts
An administrator should not guess between two high-level commitments when the manager's judgement is required. Present the conflict concisely: what each meeting is, who is involved, deadlines, consequences and available alternatives.

### 7. Protect preparation time
A schedule can appear conflict-free while leaving no time to prepare. If an executive needs 45 minutes to review a board pack, that time is a real scheduling requirement.

### 8. Last-minute changes
Urgent changes should be checked for ripple effects: room booking, transport, catering, attendee availability, online links and preparation. Update the calendar and communicate through the appropriate channel.

### 9. Record decisions
Where a material scheduling decision is made verbally, update the calendar and any relevant notes promptly. Avoid relying on memory about who agreed to a new time.

### 10. Build scheduling judgement
Over time a PA learns recurring priorities and preferences, but should distinguish established rules from assumptions. Significant trade-offs still require the executive's decision where authority has not been delegated.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A manager is invited to a supplier review at 14:00, but already has a client meeting from 13:30 to 15:00. The supplier can meet at 11:00 or 16:00, while the client has travelled from another province and has no alternative time. The administrator protects the client commitment, proposes the supplier alternatives and confirms the chosen option rather than creating a double booking.

## Practical Activity
Review a sample diary containing: a board meeting, client meeting, staff check-in, travel block, report-writing block and two new meeting requests. Rank which items are fixed, movable or require escalation. Draft a concise conflict note for the manager.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why can an empty calendar slot still be unavailable?
2. What factors help determine meeting priority?
3. Why should alternatives be offered when declining a preferred time?
4. What should happen after a double booking is discovered?
5. When should a priority conflict be escalated?
6. Why is preparation time a real scheduling requirement?
7. What ripple effects can a last-minute change create?
8. Why should a PA distinguish preferences from delegated authority?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Travel, preparation, flexible work or other constraints may already require the time.
2. Purpose, deadline, attendee requirements, external commitments, consequences and movability.
3. It helps maintain relationships and moves scheduling toward a solution.
4. Assess priorities, obtain required authority, update the calendar and communicate changes.
5. When the administrator lacks authority to choose between competing high-impact commitments.
6. Without it, the meeting may proceed but the participant may be unprepared.
7. Rooms, attendees, links, transport, catering and preparation can all be affected.
8. Knowing a preference does not automatically authorise moving significant commitments.

## Key Takeaways
- Scheduling requires judgement, not just free-space searching.
- Assess purpose, consequences, constraints and authority before moving commitments.
- Offer workable alternatives when possible.
- Protect preparation and travel time.
- Escalate high-impact trade-offs when authority is unclear.
