# Module 6 — Stock, Shrinkage & Supply Chain Control
## Lesson 7 — Supplier, Distribution & Stock Exception Management

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- explain the store's role within a wider retail supply chain;
- distinguish supplier, distribution-centre and store-level stock exceptions;
- communicate stock problems using useful evidence;
- prioritise exceptions according to customer and commercial impact;
- track actions to closure; and
- use recurring exceptions to improve supply-chain performance.

## Why This Matters
Store managers do not control every part of the supply chain, but they are responsible for managing the store's response. A late delivery, short shipment or unavailable line can affect customers, sales, promotions and team workload. Good managers turn stock exceptions into structured actions rather than repeated complaints.

## Key Terms
**Supply chain:** The connected activities through which goods and information move from source to customer.

**Distribution centre (DC):** A facility that receives, stores, sorts and dispatches stock to stores or customers.

**Stock exception:** A situation where expected stock flow, quantity, condition, timing or record does not match the required state.

**Service level:** A defined measure of supply performance, such as delivery completeness or timeliness, according to organisational definitions.

**Escalation:** Referring an unresolved or higher-impact issue to the appropriate authority or support function.

**Closure:** Confirming that the exception has been resolved and relevant records or actions are complete.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. The store is part of a network
Stock may move from manufacturers to suppliers, distribution centres, stores, fulfilment locations and customers. Information flows in the opposite and parallel directions through orders, forecasts, receipts, sales, returns and stock records.

A store-level issue can therefore originate elsewhere, while a store process failure can create problems for the wider network.

### 2. Common supply exceptions
Examples include:
- late or missed deliveries;
- short deliveries;
- excess deliveries;
- wrong products;
- damaged goods;
- incorrect labels or product data;
- promotional stock arriving too late;
- system stock without physical availability;
- transfer delays;
- supplier-return delays; and
- persistent out-of-stocks.

### 3. Evidence improves escalation
A message saying "we have no stock" is less useful than a structured exception report containing:
- product code and description;
- expected quantity;
- actual quantity;
- order or delivery reference;
- relevant dates;
- photographs where appropriate;
- customer or promotional impact;
- action already taken; and
- required resolution.

Clear evidence reduces back-and-forth communication.

### 4. Prioritise by impact
Not every exception has equal urgency. A manager may prioritise based on:
- customer demand;
- promotion timing;
- lost-sales risk;
- product criticality;
- value;
- safety or quality concerns;
- number of affected customers; and
- time required to recover.

This allows limited management attention to focus where it matters most.

### 5. Track ownership and deadlines
Exceptions should not disappear into email or verbal promises. An exception log can show:
- issue;
- owner;
- date raised;
- action required;
- target date;
- current status; and
- closure evidence.

The store manager should follow up overdue actions and escalate repeated failure.

### 6. Avoid creating false availability
When supply is delayed, staff should not make unsupported promises to customers. Teams should use verified availability information and approved alternatives, substitutions, transfers or order options where available.

Customer communication should be accurate even when the answer is disappointing.

### 7. Supplier and DC performance trends
One short delivery may be isolated. Repeated shortages from the same route, supplier or product group may require higher-level intervention.

Managers can analyse:
- delivery reliability;
- discrepancy rate;
- damage frequency;
- fill or completeness measures used by the business;
- recurring unavailable lines; and
- time to resolve claims.

The objective is constructive performance improvement supported by evidence.

### 8. Store responsibility remains important
Managers should not blame the supply chain for every availability problem. Before escalating, verify that the store has correctly processed receiving, replenishment, counts, transfers and exceptions. Strong management distinguishes external supply failure from internal execution failure.

## Worked Example
A fictional store has no stock of an advertised kettle on the first morning of a promotion. The manager checks the system, confirms the expected delivery reference, verifies that the delivery was short by 20 units and records the discrepancy. The manager escalates with evidence, checks whether approved transfer or alternative-stock options exist, briefs staff on accurate customer communication and tracks the issue until replacement stock is confirmed.

## Practical Activity
Create a stock-exception log with five fictional incidents. Rank them high, medium or low priority and justify the ranking using customer, sales, safety or operational impact.

## Knowledge Check
1. What information makes a stock escalation useful?
2. Give four common supply-chain stock exceptions.
3. Why should exceptions have an owner and target date?
4. Why should managers verify internal execution before blaming suppliers or a DC?
5. What can repeated exception patterns tell management?

## Feedback
Strong responses emphasise evidence, prioritisation, ownership, accurate customer communication and trend analysis. Exception management is successful only when issues are tracked to genuine resolution.

## Key Takeaways
- The store operates inside a wider supply network.
- Stock exceptions require structured evidence and ownership.
- Priority should reflect customer and commercial impact.
- Internal store controls should be verified before external escalation.
- Repeated exceptions should drive improvement, not repeated firefighting.
