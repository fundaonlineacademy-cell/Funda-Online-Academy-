# Lesson 1 — Stock Flow from Supplier to Customer

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- map the main stages of retail stock flow;
- explain why inventory accuracy and product availability are connected;
- identify ownership and control points across the stock journey;
- distinguish physical stock movement from information movement;
- recognise common causes of stock-flow failure.

## Why This Matters
Retail sales depend on having the right product available in the right condition and location when customers want it. Stock does not simply appear on a shelf. It moves through a chain of planning, ordering, supplier or distribution activity, receiving, storage, replenishment, sale, return and adjustment. Every stage creates information that should agree with the physical product. When the physical and system journeys separate, availability, margin and customer trust can suffer.

## Key Terms
**Supply chain:** the connected activities through which products, information and related resources move from source to customer.

**Inventory:** products held by the business for sale or operational purposes.

**Stock flow:** the physical and system movement of inventory through the retail operation.

**Replenishment:** moving or ordering stock to restore required availability.

**Stock integrity:** confidence that physical stock, system records, condition and location are appropriately aligned.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. The Retail Stock Journey
A simplified journey may be represented as:
**forecast or requirement → order → supplier/distribution → delivery → receiving → backroom/storage → replenishment → selling location → customer transaction → return/adjustment where applicable.**

Retail formats differ. Some stores receive from a central distribution centre, some directly from suppliers and others use mixed channels. Managers should understand their employer's actual flow.

### 2. Physical and Information Flow
Each physical movement should have a corresponding information event. A delivery may be physically present but not correctly received in the system. Stock may be transferred physically without a completed system transfer. Damaged units may be removed from sale but remain recorded as sellable inventory.

These mismatches create "system stock" that customers cannot actually buy or physical stock that the system does not recognise correctly.

### 3. Critical Control Points
Important controls can include:
- authorised ordering;
- delivery identification;
- quantity and condition verification;
- discrepancy recording;
- secure storage;
- location discipline;
- timely replenishment;
- controlled transfers and adjustments;
- transaction accuracy;
- returns and damage processing;
- stock counts and variance investigation.

A manager does not need to perform every task personally but must ensure responsibilities are clear and exceptions are followed through.

### 4. Availability Is an End-to-End Outcome
An empty shelf can have many causes: supplier short delivery, receiving error, incorrect system quantity, stock in the wrong backroom location, delayed replenishment, damage, theft, inaccurate planogram capacity or unexpectedly high demand.

Managers therefore avoid treating every shelf gap as a replenishment employee's failure. They trace the stock journey to find the actual break.

### 5. Stock Has Commercial Consequences
Poor stock flow can create lost sales, excessive markdown, customer complaints, emergency transfers, additional handling, tied-up working capital and shrinkage risk. Conversely, simply holding excessive stock is not a solution. Inventory needs to support demand while being controlled efficiently.

### 6. Management Visibility
Useful management questions include:
- What is due today?
- What was received versus expected?
- Which discrepancies remain unresolved?
- Which high-priority lines are unavailable?
- Is stock sitting in the backroom while the shelf is empty?
- Are damaged or return items correctly segregated and processed?
- Are system and physical records showing recurring mismatches?

These questions turn stock control into a daily management discipline.

## Worked Example
A system shows 18 units of a popular product, but the shelf is empty. The manager checks the backroom and finds 12 units in an incorrect location. Two units are damaged but not yet adjusted, and four units cannot immediately be located. The correct response is not simply to order another 18 units. The manager should replenish the sellable stock, process damage correctly and investigate the remaining discrepancy.

## Practical Activity
Draw a stock-flow map for a fictional clothing or grocery store. At each stage, identify:
1. the physical stock movement;
2. the information or system record;
3. one control;
4. one possible exception;
5. the responsible role.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why must physical and information flow stay aligned?
2. Name four stages in a retail stock journey.
3. Why can an empty shelf have causes outside replenishment?
4. What is stock integrity?
5. Why is excessive inventory not automatically good availability management?

## Feedback
1. Misalignment creates inaccurate records, poor availability and control risk.
2. Examples include ordering, delivery, receiving, storage, replenishment, sale and returns.
3. Supplier, receiving, system, damage, demand or location failures may be involved.
4. Confidence that physical stock, system records, condition and location are appropriately aligned.
5. Excess stock can tie up resources, create handling and markdown risk, and still be in the wrong location or assortment.

## Key Takeaways
- Stock availability is the result of an end-to-end process.
- Physical product and system information must move together.
- Managers trace exceptions to their true control point.
- Inventory affects sales, customer experience, margin and risk.
- Daily visibility and follow-up are essential to stock integrity.
