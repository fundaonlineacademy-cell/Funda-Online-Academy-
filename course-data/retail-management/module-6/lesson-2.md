# Module 6 — Stock, Shrinkage & Supply Chain Control
## Lesson 2 — Receiving Accuracy & Delivery Controls

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- explain why receiving is a high-risk stock-control point;
- apply a disciplined receiving sequence;
- distinguish ordered, delivered, accepted and recorded quantities;
- identify shortages, overages, damages and incorrect items;
- describe appropriate documentation, segregation and escalation controls; and
- use receiving evidence to support accurate inventory records and supplier follow-up.

## Why This Matters
A store cannot maintain reliable stock records if deliveries enter the business without proper control. Receiving is where physical goods, supplier documents, system records and accountability meet. Errors at this point can create stock variances before products even reach the sales floor.

A retail manager therefore treats receiving as a controlled process rather than simply unloading boxes.

## Key Terms
**Purchase order:** An authorised record of what the business intended to buy.

**Delivery note:** A supplier or logistics document describing goods delivered.

**Goods received:** The quantity physically received and accepted by the business.

**Discrepancy:** A difference between what was expected and what was actually received.

**Segregation:** Keeping questionable, damaged or unverified stock separate until a decision is made.

**Chain of accountability:** The evidence showing who handled, checked, approved or changed a transaction.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Receiving is a control point
The receiving area can be busy, especially when deliveries arrive during trading hours. Pressure creates risk: cartons may be accepted without counting, damaged stock may be mixed with saleable stock, paperwork may be signed too early, or system quantities may be captured from documents instead of actual goods.

The manager should design the process so that speed does not replace control.

### 2. The basic receiving sequence
A strong receiving process normally follows a sequence such as:
1. confirm that the delivery is expected and authorised;
2. control access to the receiving area;
3. identify the supplier, vehicle or delivery reference where required;
4. compare delivery documentation with the authorised order;
5. count or verify physical goods according to procedure;
6. inspect relevant goods and packaging for damage or condition concerns;
7. identify shortages, excess quantities, substitutions or incorrect items;
8. separate disputed or damaged stock where required;
9. record only the quantity actually accepted;
10. obtain the required approvals or signatures;
11. move accepted stock promptly to secure storage or replenishment; and
12. retain evidence for reconciliation and supplier follow-up.

Exact procedures vary by retailer, product category and system. The management principle remains: physical reality must agree with authorised records.

### 3. Ordered, delivered, accepted and recorded are not always the same
Suppose a store ordered 30 units. The delivery note states 30, but staff physically count 28. One carton is also damaged and contains two units that cannot be accepted.

A weak process may capture 30 because that is what the paperwork says. A controlled process investigates the difference and records the transaction according to approved procedure.

Managers must teach staff not to make records agree by assumption. Records must reflect verified facts.

### 4. Common receiving discrepancies
Receiving teams may encounter:
- shortages;
- over-deliveries;
- wrong products or variants;
- duplicate deliveries;
- damaged packaging;
- damaged or contaminated goods;
- incorrect prices or references on documents;
- expired or unsuitable date-sensitive stock where relevant;
- missing documents; or
- system/order mismatches.

The correct response is not to improvise. The discrepancy should be documented, contained and escalated through the retailer's approved process.

### 5. Why premature signing is dangerous
A signature can be interpreted as evidence that goods were received. Staff should therefore understand what they are confirming before signing or electronically acknowledging a delivery.

Managers should ensure that receiving responsibilities and authority levels are clear. Where a delivery cannot be fully verified immediately, the retailer's approved exception process should be followed rather than creating misleading confirmation.

### 6. Segregating exceptions
Damaged, disputed, recalled or otherwise questionable stock should not accidentally enter normal saleable inventory. A clearly controlled exception area helps prevent this.

Segregation should be supported by identification and records so that stock does not disappear into an informal holding area.

### 7. Receiving fraud and loss awareness
Receiving controls also reduce opportunities for deliberate loss. Risks can include collusion, false shortages, fictitious receipts, duplicate processing, unauthorised substitutions or stock being diverted before recording.

Managers should focus on prevention through process design: restricted access, independent checks where proportionate, system permissions, evidence, exception reporting and review of unusual patterns. Suspicion should be escalated through authorised channels rather than handled through unsupported accusation.

### 8. Management review
The retail manager should review receiving performance, not only individual deliveries. Useful indicators can include:
- discrepancy frequency;
- supplier shortage trends;
- receiving damages;
- unresolved claims;
- late deliveries;
- processing delays; and
- repeated manual adjustments after receiving.

Patterns reveal whether the problem is supplier performance, process weakness, training, system configuration or possible misconduct.

## Worked Example
A fictional store, Ubuntu Home & Style, receives 24 kettles. The delivery document shows 24. The receiving assistant counts 24 but finds three crushed cartons. Inspection shows one kettle is visibly damaged.

The assistant does not place all 24 into normal stock. The damaged unit is separated, the exception is recorded according to procedure, and the manager ensures the accepted quantity and supplier claim process are handled correctly. This prevents a receiving problem from becoming a customer complaint or unexplained stock adjustment later.

## Practical Activity
Create a receiving checklist for a medium-sized retail store. Include at least 12 checks from delivery arrival to final movement of accepted stock. Add an exception route for shortages, damages and incorrect products.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why should staff compare physical goods with authorised documents?
2. What is a receiving discrepancy?
3. Why should damaged or disputed goods be segregated?
4. What risk arises when staff sign for goods before verification?
5. Name three indicators a manager could review to improve receiving control.

## Feedback
Strong answers should show that receiving accuracy protects inventory records, supplier accountability, customer quality and loss prevention. The learner should recognise that discrepancies must be evidenced and resolved rather than hidden through assumptions or unsupported system adjustments.

## Key Takeaways
- Receiving is one of the store's most important inventory control points.
- Documents do not replace physical verification.
- Ordered, delivered, accepted and recorded quantities may differ.
- Exceptions require segregation, evidence and authorised resolution.
- Managers should review receiving trends, not only individual incidents.
