# Module 6 — Stock, Shrinkage & Supply Chain Control
## Lesson 8 — Stock-Control Review & Improvement Plan

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- conduct an integrated review of store stock control;
- connect receiving, replenishment, counting, shrinkage and exception data;
- distinguish symptoms from root causes;
- prioritise improvement actions;
- assign measurable controls, owners and review dates; and
- present a practical stock-control improvement plan.

## Why This Matters
Stock control is not one task. It is a chain of connected processes. A receiving weakness can later appear as a count variance. A backroom problem can appear as an out-of-stock. A returns-control weakness can appear as shrinkage. Managers therefore need an integrated review rather than isolated fixes.

## Key Terms
**Control review:** A structured assessment of whether operational controls are working as intended.

**Root-cause analysis:** Investigation aimed at identifying why a problem occurred rather than only describing the symptom.

**Corrective action:** Action taken to resolve an identified problem.

**Preventive action:** Action designed to reduce the chance of recurrence.

**Control owner:** The person accountable for carrying out or maintaining a defined control.

**Review date:** A scheduled point when management checks whether the action produced the expected result.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Build the complete stock picture
A useful stock-control review combines multiple evidence sources:
- receiving discrepancies;
- stock-count variances;
- shelf-gap observations;
- backroom conditions;
- damage and write-off reports;
- manual adjustments;
- refund or transaction exceptions where relevant;
- supplier or DC exceptions; and
- customer availability complaints.

No single report tells the whole story.

### 2. Diagnose before fixing
Suppose a store has frequent shelf gaps. Possible responses include ordering more stock, increasing replenishment frequency or changing shelf capacity. But if the real cause is inaccurate inventory records, ordering more may increase excess stock without solving availability.

Managers should ask "why?" repeatedly until the process cause is understood well enough to act.

### 3. Separate symptoms from causes
**Symptom:** Promotional line repeatedly unavailable.

Possible causes:
- supplier short delivery;
- poor receiving record;
- stock in wrong backroom location;
- replenishment not scheduled before peak trading;
- shrinkage; or
- demand above forecast.

The improvement plan should target the verified cause.

### 4. Prioritise actions
A practical priority method considers:
- financial impact;
- customer impact;
- frequency;
- control risk;
- safety implications;
- ease and speed of correction; and
- likelihood of recurrence.

High-impact recurring failures normally deserve stronger management attention than isolated low-value errors.

### 5. Make actions measurable
Weak action: "Improve stock control."

Better action: "Receiving supervisor will complete a documented discrepancy check on every inbound delivery for the next four weeks; store manager will review the exception log every Friday and escalate unresolved supplier discrepancies older than the approved service window."

The second action has a control, owner, frequency and review mechanism.

### 6. Use a control plan
A useful improvement plan can contain:
- issue;
- evidence;
- root cause;
- corrective action;
- preventive control;
- owner;
- target date;
- success measure;
- review date; and
- status.

### 7. Measure whether improvement happened
Examples of measures include:
- fewer receiving discrepancies;
- improved inventory accuracy;
- lower unexplained adjustment frequency;
- fewer shelf gaps;
- lower damage rate;
- faster supplier-claim closure; or
- improved availability on priority lines.

Definitions should remain consistent so that before-and-after comparisons are meaningful.

### 8. Management communication
A stock-control review should end with clear communication. Staff need to know what changes, why it changes and who owns each control. Senior managers may need concise evidence of risk, action and expected benefit.

The manager should avoid overwhelming the team with too many simultaneous controls. A smaller number of well-executed priorities can outperform a long list nobody owns.

## Integrated Scenario
Ubuntu Value Store has the following fictional monthly evidence:
- receiving discrepancies increased from 6 to 14;
- manual stock adjustments increased sharply;
- three promoted lines had repeated shelf gaps;
- damage write-offs increased in one backroom category;
- stock-count variance is concentrated in products received through one high-volume bay.

A weak conclusion would be "staff are careless." A stronger management response investigates the receiving bay, checks staffing and workload, reviews how exceptions are captured, verifies backroom handling and tests whether the same products show discrepancies from receipt through count.

The resulting plan may include revised receiving checks, clearer exception segregation, targeted cycle counts and weekly review of manual adjustments. Each action should have an owner and measure.

1. the three highest-priority risks;
2. evidence supporting each risk;
3. likely root cause to investigate;
4. corrective action;
5. preventive control;
6. owner;
7. success measure; and
8. review date.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why should a stock-control review use several evidence sources?
2. What is the difference between a symptom and a root cause?
3. Give four fields that belong in an improvement plan.
4. Why must success measures use consistent definitions?
5. What makes an action genuinely manageable?

## Feedback
A strong plan links evidence to root cause and then to a specific control. It avoids unsupported blame, vague actions and cosmetic record adjustments. Improvement should be measurable and reviewed after implementation.

## Key Takeaways
- Stock control is an integrated management system.
- Symptoms such as shelf gaps or variances can have several causes.
- Improvement plans need evidence, owners, deadlines and measures.
- Corrective action fixes the current problem; preventive control reduces recurrence.
- Managers must review results after actions are implemented.

## Visual Guidance
Use a continuous-improvement loop: **Observe → Measure → Diagnose → Prioritise → Act → Verify → Standardise → Observe Again**.
