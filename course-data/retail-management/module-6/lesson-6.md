# Module 6 — Stock, Shrinkage & Supply Chain Control
## Lesson 6 — Damages, Returns, Write-offs & Stock Adjustments

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- distinguish damages, customer returns, supplier returns, write-offs and stock adjustments;
- explain why exception stock requires physical and system control;
- apply a basic exception-handling sequence;
- identify risks created by uncontrolled write-offs and adjustments;
- maintain evidence and approval for stock-status changes; and
- use exception trends to improve store operations.

## Why This Matters
Not every product received by a store will ultimately be sold. Goods may be damaged, returned by customers, rejected, sent back to suppliers, written off or adjusted because of verified discrepancies. These events are normal in retail, but weak control can turn normal exceptions into inventory loss and unreliable records.

## Key Terms
**Damage:** Stock that has suffered physical or other condition impairment.

**Customer return:** Goods brought back by a customer and processed under applicable rights, policy and procedure.

**Supplier return:** Goods authorised for return to a supplier or distribution point.

**Write-off:** An authorised removal of stock value or quantity from usable inventory according to the organisation's rules.

**Stock adjustment:** An authorised change to an inventory record to reflect a verified event or discrepancy.

**Quarantine/hold area:** A controlled location for stock that should not enter normal saleable inventory until resolved.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Exceptions need their own control path
A damaged item should not simply be placed in a corner. A returned item should not automatically be put back on the shelf. An unexplained variance should not automatically become a write-off.

Managers need clear routes for different stock statuses.

### 2. A practical exception sequence
A controlled sequence can be:
1. identify the exception;
2. remove or segregate the item where required;
3. verify product and quantity;
4. record the reason and relevant evidence;
5. determine the authorised disposition;
6. obtain required approval;
7. update the system using the correct transaction or reason code;
8. physically move or dispose of stock according to procedure; and
9. reconcile unresolved exceptions.

### 3. Damaged stock
Damage may occur during transport, receiving, storage, replenishment, merchandising or customer handling. Managers should investigate recurring damage rather than accepting it as unavoidable.

For example, repeated crushed packaging may indicate poor stacking. Repeated breakage in one aisle may indicate fixture or handling problems. Trend analysis can therefore reduce future write-offs.

### 4. Customer returns
Returned products require both customer-service and inventory decisions. Depending on the reason, condition, applicable consumer rights and store procedure, an item may be returned to saleable stock, held for inspection, returned to a supplier or processed through another authorised route.

Managers should ensure staff distinguish statutory consumer rights from additional store policies or goodwill arrangements and escalate uncertain cases.

### 5. Supplier returns
Supplier-return stock should remain traceable until the return is completed and any expected credit or replacement is reconciled. A carton physically leaving the store does not by itself prove that the supplier account or inventory record was correctly resolved.

### 6. Write-offs require accountability
Write-offs can be necessary, but they also create risk because stock disappears from saleable inventory. Controls may include:
- approved reason codes;
- manager authorisation;
- photographic or documentary evidence where policy requires;
- controlled disposal;
- witness or second-person verification for selected categories; and
- periodic write-off reports.

Exact controls depend on the retailer and product risk.

### 7. Stock adjustments are not a shortcut
An adjustment should represent a verified event. Excessive manual adjustments can indicate poor receiving, weak transaction processing, bad counting, inadequate training or possible misuse.

Managers should review who adjusts stock, why, how often and for which products.

### 8. Reconcile physical and system status
One of the most common exception-control failures occurs when physical action and system action do not match. For example:
- an item is discarded but remains in system stock;
- stock is returned to a supplier but no return transaction is completed;
- an item is adjusted out but remains physically in the store; or
- a customer return is placed back on the shelf but remains in a non-saleable system status.

Managers must make sure physical status and recorded status move together.

## Worked Example
A fictional homeware store has six damaged glass sets after a shelving incident. The supervisor segregates them, records the product codes and quantities, obtains the required approval and processes the approved damage transaction. The manager then investigates why the incident occurred and changes the storage arrangement. The objective is not only to record the loss but to prevent recurrence.

## Practical Activity
Design an exception register with columns for date, product, quantity, exception type, reason, physical location, evidence reference, approving person, system transaction, final disposition and reconciliation status.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why should exception stock be segregated?
2. What is the difference between a write-off and a stock adjustment?
3. Why should supplier returns be reconciled after goods leave the store?
4. Give three risks of uncontrolled write-offs.
5. What does it mean to align physical and system status?

## Feedback
Strong responses show that exception control requires identification, segregation, evidence, approval, correct system processing and reconciliation. Managers should also use exception trends to prevent repeat losses.

## Key Takeaways
- Damages and returns require controlled inventory treatment.
- Physical movement and system records must agree.
- Write-offs should be authorised and evidenced.
- Manual adjustments require management review.
- Exception trends can reveal preventable operational weaknesses.
