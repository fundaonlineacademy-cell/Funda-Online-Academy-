# Module 6 — Stock, Shrinkage & Supply Chain Control
## Lesson 3 — Inventory Records, Replenishment & Availability

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- explain the relationship between inventory records and physical stock;
- distinguish stock on hand from stock that is genuinely available to sell;
- explain replenishment as a management process;
- identify causes of shelf gaps despite apparent system stock;
- use stock information and floor observation together; and
- design corrective actions that improve product availability without creating uncontrolled stock movement.

## Why This Matters
Retailers earn revenue when customers can buy the products they want. A product may exist somewhere in the business but still be unavailable to the customer because it is in the backroom, incorrectly recorded, damaged, reserved, misplaced or not replenished.

The manager's responsibility is therefore broader than counting stock. The goal is reliable information and controlled movement that places saleable stock where demand occurs.

## Key Terms
**Stock on hand:** The quantity the inventory system records as being held at a location, subject to system definitions.

**Product availability:** Whether a customer can actually obtain the required saleable product when needed.

**Replenishment:** Controlled movement of stock to restore selling locations to appropriate levels.

**Shelf gap:** A selling position where expected product is absent.

**Phantom stock:** A practical term for stock shown in the system but not physically available where expected.

**Inventory accuracy:** The degree to which system records agree with verified physical stock.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Inventory records support decisions
Retail systems may record receipts, sales, transfers, returns, write-offs and adjustments. Managers use these records to make decisions about ordering, replenishment, availability and investigation.

If records are inaccurate, downstream decisions become unreliable. A system may indicate ten units while only six can be found. The store may then fail to reorder because the system falsely suggests adequate stock.

### 2. Physical stock and saleable availability
Not every unit physically present is immediately saleable. Stock can be:
- on the sales floor;
- in the backroom;
- damaged;
- awaiting return;
- reserved for an authorised order;
- under investigation;
- incorrectly located; or
- in another controlled status defined by the retailer.

Managers should understand the system statuses used by their organisation and avoid treating every recorded unit as customer-ready inventory.

### 3. Replenishment connects stockholding to sales
Replenishment means moving appropriate stock to the selling location before or when customer demand requires it. It can be triggered by system rules, minimum levels, scheduled routines, visual observation or a combination of methods.

A good replenishment process considers:
- fast-moving lines;
- promotional products;
- peak periods;
- shelf capacity;
- safety and handling requirements;
- backroom organisation; and
- current demand.

### 4. Why shelf gaps occur
A shelf gap does not automatically mean the supplier failed to deliver. Possible causes include:
- stock is in the backroom;
- incorrect receiving quantity;
- poor replenishment timing;
- stock in the wrong location;
- theft or loss;
- unprocessed damages;
- incorrect system adjustment;
- unexpectedly high demand;
- poor forecasting; or
- supply interruption.

The manager should investigate the cause before choosing the solution.

### 5. The danger of blind adjustments
When physical and system stock differ, changing the system quantity simply to make the discrepancy disappear can destroy the audit trail. Adjustments should follow authorised procedures and use appropriate reason codes, evidence and approval levels.

A stock adjustment is a control event, not a housekeeping shortcut.

### 6. Floor observation plus data
A strong retail manager uses both reports and physical observation. A report may say that stock exists, while a store walk shows an empty shelf. Conversely, a shelf may look full while system records reveal that no replenishment stock remains for tomorrow.

The manager asks:
- What does the system say?
- What does the floor show?
- What does the backroom show?
- What transactions occurred?
- What customer demand is expected?

This creates a more reliable management picture.

### 7. Backroom discipline
Poor backroom organisation damages availability. Stock may be technically present but difficult to locate. Effective backroom control includes clear locations, safe storage, logical grouping, accessible fast movers, controlled exceptions and disciplined movement.

The objective is not a beautiful storeroom for its own sake. The objective is fast, accurate and safe support for selling activity.

### 8. Replenishment priorities
During busy periods, managers may need to prioritise. High-demand, advertised or essential lines may require faster replenishment than low-demand items. Priorities should be evidence-based and aligned with business rules.

Managers should also avoid overfilling. Excess stock on the floor can create congestion, damage, inaccurate displays and safety risks.

## Worked Example
A fictional supermarket system shows 18 units of a promotional cleaning product. The shelf is empty. The manager checks the backroom and finds 12 units in an incorrectly labelled bay. Two units are damaged, and four cannot initially be located.

The manager replenishes the 12 saleable units, processes the damaged stock through the approved exception route and investigates the remaining variance. The response restores availability while preserving control.

## Practical Activity
Choose five products in a fictional retail store and create a replenishment priority table. For each product include demand level, current shelf position, backroom quantity, promotional status, risk of stock-out and required action.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why can stock on hand differ from customer-ready availability?
2. Give four causes of shelf gaps.
3. Why should managers avoid unsupported inventory adjustments?
4. How does backroom discipline affect sales-floor availability?
5. Why should managers combine reports with physical store walks?

## Feedback
A strong answer links accurate inventory records, controlled stock movement and customer availability. Replenishment is not simply filling shelves; it is a managed response to demand using trustworthy information and physical verification.

## Key Takeaways
- Accurate records are essential for ordering and replenishment decisions.
- Stock can be physically present but commercially unavailable.
- Shelf gaps require diagnosis, not assumptions.
- Replenishment should be prioritised using demand and operational evidence.
- System data and physical observation should confirm one another.
