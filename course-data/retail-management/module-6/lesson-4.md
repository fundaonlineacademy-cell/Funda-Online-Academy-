# Module 6 — Stock, Shrinkage & Supply Chain Control
## Lesson 4 — Stock Counts, Cycle Counts & Variance Investigation

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- explain the purpose of stock counts and cycle counts;
- prepare a retail area for reliable counting;
- identify common sources of count error;
- calculate and interpret stock variances;
- investigate discrepancies using evidence; and
- distinguish corrective action from unsupported stock adjustment.

## Why This Matters
Inventory records are only useful when they reasonably reflect reality. Stock counts provide a disciplined way to test that relationship. The count itself, however, is only part of the process. Managers must prepare correctly, protect count integrity and investigate significant differences.

## Key Terms
**Stock count:** A physical verification of inventory quantities.

**Cycle count:** A scheduled count of selected items or locations rather than the entire inventory at once.

**Book stock:** The quantity recorded by the inventory system before verified adjustments.

**Variance:** The difference between verified physical quantity and the relevant system quantity.

**Recount:** A second verification performed when a result requires confirmation.

**Root cause:** The underlying reason that produced a discrepancy.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Why retailers count stock
Stock counts help the business:
- test inventory accuracy;
- identify unexplained losses or gains;
- improve ordering and replenishment;
- verify high-risk or high-value lines;
- identify process failures;
- support financial and operational reporting; and
- strengthen accountability.

A full stocktake may cover the entire store periodically. Cycle counts allow management to verify selected areas more frequently.

### 2. Preparing for a count
Poor preparation produces unreliable results. Before counting, the manager may need to ensure that:
- stock movement is controlled according to the count procedure;
- receiving and transfers are appropriately processed;
- damages and returns are separated;
- locations are organised;
- loose items are identifiable;
- counting responsibilities are assigned;
- count sheets or devices are ready; and
- staff understand units of measure and product variants.

The exact process depends on the retailer's system and stocktake rules.

### 3. Count integrity
Counters should record what they physically verify rather than trying to match expected quantities. Where blind counting is used, counters do not see the book quantity beforehand, reducing the temptation to make the physical result agree with the system.

Managers should also control interruptions and movement during the count. Goods moving between locations while teams count can create duplication or omission.

### 4. Common count errors
Variances can be caused by real stock loss, but also by counting mistakes. Examples include:
- counting the same stock twice;
- missing a location;
- confusing similar product variants;
- counting cartons as units or units as cartons;
- including damaged or quarantined stock incorrectly;
- transposition or data-entry errors;
- stock moving during the count; and
- unprocessed transactions.

A variance should therefore be verified before conclusions are drawn.

### 5. Calculating a basic quantity variance
If book stock is 60 units and verified physical stock is 54 units:

**Quantity variance = Physical stock − Book stock = 54 − 60 = −6 units**

The negative result indicates six fewer units than the relevant system record.

Retailers may also calculate variance value using approved cost or valuation methods. Learners should follow the organisation's financial definitions rather than inventing a value basis.

### 6. Investigating the variance
A useful investigation may include:
1. recounting the item;
2. checking nearby and alternative locations;
3. verifying product codes and variants;
4. reviewing recent receipts;
5. reviewing transfers;
6. checking returns, damages and write-offs;
7. reviewing relevant POS or exception transactions;
8. checking whether timing caused the mismatch; and
9. escalating suspicious patterns through authorised channels.

The objective is to explain the difference where reasonably possible.

### 7. Pattern analysis
One variance may be a simple error. Repeated variances can reveal a control weakness.

For example, if one product category repeatedly shows shortages after deliveries, management should examine receiving, storage, replenishment and transaction processes. If shortages cluster around specific exception transactions, the manager should escalate appropriately without making unsupported accusations.

### 8. Correcting records
Once a variance has been verified and investigated, authorised stock adjustments may be required. Adjustments should use the correct reason, evidence and approval route.

Managers should never teach staff to hide differences merely to achieve a clean report. A clean report created from false adjustments is worse than an honest variance because it removes useful evidence.

## Worked Example
Ubuntu Fashion counts a popular shirt. The system shows 42 units. The first physical count finds 38. A recount finds 39 because one unit was behind a fixture. Transaction review then shows two customer returns physically received but not yet completed in the system. The manager follows the approved process to resolve the transaction timing and investigates the remaining difference rather than immediately posting a three-unit write-off.

## Practical Activity
Create a variance investigation worksheet with sections for book quantity, first count, recount, alternate locations, recent receiving, transfers, damages/returns, transaction exceptions, findings, authorised adjustment and follow-up action.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is the difference between a full stock count and a cycle count?
2. Why should stock movement be controlled during counting?
3. Calculate the variance if physical stock is 77 and book stock is 81.
4. Give four possible causes of a count discrepancy other than theft.
5. Why should an adjustment only occur after appropriate verification?

## Feedback
Question 3 gives a variance of **−4 units** using physical minus book quantity. Strong responses emphasise preparation, accurate counting, investigation and evidence before record correction.

## Key Takeaways
- Stock counts test the reliability of inventory records.
- Count preparation and movement control matter as much as arithmetic.
- Variance does not automatically prove theft.
- Recounts and transaction review help identify root causes.
- Adjustments should be authorised, evidenced and traceable.

## Visual Guidance
Use a flow: **Prepare → Count → Recount → Compare → Investigate → Authorise → Adjust if Required → Prevent Recurrence**.
