# Professional Certificate in Retail Management & Team Leadership
## Module 4: Team Leadership & People Management
### Lesson 2: Setting Expectations, Roles & Performance Standards

**Estimated study time:** 50–60 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Convert store priorities into clear employee expectations.
2. Distinguish tasks, standards and outcomes.
3. Brief roles without creating ambiguity.
4. Check understanding and accountability.
5. Respond constructively when performance expectations are unclear or unmet.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Many performance problems begin before the work starts. An employee may be told to “sort out the floor” without being told which department, what standard is required, what must be completed first or when the manager will check. Vague instructions create inconsistent results and make later accountability difficult.

Retail leaders need to translate business requirements into clear, realistic expectations that employees can understand and execute.

## Key Terms
- **Expectation** — clear statement of what is required.
- **Role clarity** — shared understanding of responsibilities and authority.
- **Performance standard** — required level or quality of work.
- **Outcome** — result that the work is intended to achieve.
- **Priority** — work requiring greater attention because of importance or urgency.
- **Accountability point** — agreed moment or method for checking progress/results.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Separate task, standard and outcome
“Replenish beverages” is a task. “Complete priority gaps before 10:00 using correct rotation and shelf standards” adds a standard. “Restore availability before the morning peak” describes the business outcome.

Strong instructions connect all three.

### 2. Use five questions for clarity
Before assigning work, ensure the employee can answer:
- What must be done?
- Why does it matter?
- What standard applies?
- By when or in what sequence?
- When/how will progress be checked?

### 3. Match responsibility to capability and authority
Do not assign an employee responsibility for an outcome if they lack the required training, system access, equipment or authority. Delegation without resources is poor management.

### 4. Brief the shift, then clarify individual ownership
A team briefing creates shared direction. Individual allocation confirms who owns specific work. Avoid assuming that “everyone” will take responsibility; when ownership is unclear, important work is often missed.

### 5. Confirm understanding
Asking “Do you understand?” often produces an automatic yes. A better method is to ask the employee to summarise the priority or explain how they will approach it. This is especially useful for complex or unfamiliar tasks.

### 6. Define what good performance looks like
Standards may include accuracy, timing, customer behaviour, presentation, safety and process compliance. Wherever possible, use observable criteria rather than vague phrases such as “do better”.

### 7. Monitor without micromanaging
Leaders need checkpoints proportional to risk and employee capability. A new employee doing a high-risk task may need closer supervision. An experienced employee completing familiar work may need only an agreed result check.

### 8. Correct unclear expectations before blaming performance
If the manager gave an ambiguous instruction, the first responsibility is to clarify it. Where an employee knew the standard but did not meet it, establish facts and use the appropriate coaching, performance or conduct process.

## Worked Example
A supervisor tells Lerato, “Make sure the promotion is ready.” At 10:00, signage is up but the promoted products are still in the backroom. Lerato believed “ready” meant installing point-of-sale material.

The instruction should have defined the outcome: promotional stock on the floor, correct tickets displayed, feature space set, system price verified and completion checked before opening. Better briefing prevents avoidable failure.

## Practical Activity
Take five vague retail instructions—such as “clean up”, “watch the tills”, “fix stock”, “help customers” and “prepare closing”—and rewrite each into a clear task + standard + outcome + checkpoint.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is the difference between a task and an outcome?
2. Name the five clarity questions.
3. Why must capability and authority match responsibility?
4. Why is “everyone is responsible” sometimes weak allocation?
5. How can a leader confirm understanding?
6. Give two observable dimensions of a performance standard.
7. What determines the amount of monitoring required?
8. What should a manager do if the original instruction was unclear?

## Feedback / Explanation
1. A task is the work performed; an outcome is the result the work should create.
2. What, why, standard, timing/sequence and checkpoint.
3. Employees need the means and permission to perform assigned work properly.
4. Individual ownership may remain unclear.
5. Ask the employee to summarise the requirement or intended approach.
6. Examples: accuracy, timing, presentation, customer behaviour, safety or compliance.
7. Risk, complexity, employee capability and experience.
8. Clarify the expectation and avoid treating ambiguity as automatic employee failure.

## Key Takeaways
- Clear expectations improve both execution and fair accountability.
- Link tasks to standards and business outcomes.
- Give ownership together with appropriate resources and authority.
- Confirm understanding rather than assuming it.
- Monitor proportionately and correct ambiguity early.
