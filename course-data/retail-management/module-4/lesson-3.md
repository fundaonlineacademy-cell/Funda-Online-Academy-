# Professional Certificate in Retail Management & Team Leadership
## Module 4: Team Leadership & People Management
### Lesson 3: Shift Planning, Delegation & Work Allocation

**Estimated study time:** 50–60 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Build a practical shift plan from workload and trading demand.
2. Allocate people according to priority, skill and risk.
3. Delegate without losing management accountability.
4. Adjust deployment when trading conditions change.
5. Create effective handovers between shifts.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Retail workload rarely arrives evenly. Deliveries, customer peaks, replenishment, breaks, online orders, fitting rooms, checkout demand and housekeeping compete for limited labour. A schedule tells you who is present; a shift plan tells you how available capability will be used.

## Key Terms
- **Deployment** — positioning employees where their capability is required.
- **Delegation** — assigning responsibility for completing work within defined authority.
- **Coverage** — sufficient trained people available for a function or period.
- **Workload** — amount and type of work requiring completion.
- **Contingency** — prepared alternative response to a likely disruption.
- **Handover** — structured transfer of important information and unfinished responsibility.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Start with demand, not habit
Do not copy yesterday's deployment automatically. Review expected traffic, deliveries, promotions, online fulfilment, known absences and priority tasks.

### 2. Identify critical coverage
Some functions require continuous or minimum coverage: checkout, customer service, receiving during deliveries, high-risk stock areas or other company-defined positions. Protect critical coverage before assigning lower-priority tasks.

### 3. Match skill to work
A person may be available but not trained or authorised for a particular task. Keep a practical skills view of the team so you know who can operate tills, receive stock, handle specialised departments, process authorised exceptions or lead a zone.

### 4. Plan breaks and predictable transitions
Breaks are necessary and should be planned within applicable schedules and workplace rules. Poorly sequenced breaks can create sudden service gaps. Leaders should also consider shift start/end overlaps and handover periods.

### 5. Delegate complete packages of work
Good delegation states the expected outcome, authority limit, resources, deadline and checkpoint. Avoid delegating only the activity while withholding information needed to succeed.

### 6. Retain management oversight
Delegation does not mean disappearance. The leader should check high-risk work and respond to exceptions. The purpose is controlled independence, not constant interference.

### 7. Reallocate dynamically
If traffic exceeds forecast, a delivery arrives late or an employee becomes unavailable, revise the plan. A useful plan is flexible enough to respond while still protecting the most important controls.

### 8. End with a disciplined handover
Record significant incomplete tasks, incidents, stock exceptions, customer follow-ups, system problems and other items the next leader needs. A vague “everything is fine” handover creates preventable surprises.

## Worked Example
A store has eight employees on an afternoon shift. Two are checkout-trained, three can replenish, one handles online collections and two are experienced across several functions. A late delivery arrives just as customer traffic increases.

The supervisor protects checkout coverage, keeps online collection capability available, assigns trained staff to priority receiving/replenishment work and postpones a low-priority display tidy. The plan changes, but critical service and control remain protected.

## Practical Activity
Create a fictional Saturday shift plan for 10 employees from 09:00 to 17:00. Include checkout, floor service, replenishment, fitting room/customer service, breaks and one delivery. Add two contingencies: one absence and an unexpected customer peak.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. A schedule shows attendance; a shift plan allocates available people to work and priorities.
2. Functions that require sufficient trained coverage to operate effectively and safely.
3. Availability alone does not mean the employee is trained or authorised.
4. Outcome, authority, resources, timing and checkpoint.
5. No.
6. Examples: unexpected traffic, late delivery, absence, system issue or urgent stock problem.
7. To protect service and operational coverage while employees take required breaks.
8. Important unfinished work, incidents, exceptions, follow-ups and risks.

## Key Takeaways
- Plan labour around demand and risk.
- Protect critical trained coverage.
- Delegate outcomes with clear authority and checkpoints.
- Reallocate when evidence changes.
- Use disciplined handovers to maintain continuity.
