# Module 4 — Team Leadership & People Management
## Lesson 7 — Attendance, Conduct & Fair Process Awareness

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- explain why attendance and conduct are operational management issues;
- distinguish evidence gathering from premature disciplinary judgement;
- apply consistent store-level attendance controls;
- understand the manager's role in fair workplace process;
- recognise matters requiring HR or senior-management escalation.

## Why This Matters
Retail stores depend on people being present when scheduled. An unexplained absence can affect queue length, receiving, security, replenishment and customer service. Conduct problems can also damage trust and operational control. Managers therefore need reliable attendance and conduct management—but they must not replace fair process with anger, assumptions or automatic punishment.

This lesson develops management awareness rather than legal specialisation. Learners should always use the current employer policy, collective agreements where applicable, authorised HR guidance and current South African labour requirements.

## Key Terms
**Attendance control:** the system used to schedule, record, verify and respond appropriately to employee attendance.

**Misconduct:** workplace conduct that may breach an applicable rule or standard.

**Incapacity:** inability to perform required work adequately for reasons that may differ from misconduct and require appropriate assessment.

**Consistency:** applying comparable standards fairly while still considering the facts of each case.

**Procedural fairness:** giving an employee an appropriate opportunity to know and respond to relevant concerns before a consequential decision is made, within the applicable process.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Start with accurate records
Good attendance management begins with correct rosters, clocking or attendance records, approved leave information and reliable communication. If records are poor, managers may accuse employees incorrectly or fail to identify recurring patterns.

Managers should verify whether an absence was reported, whether authorisation exists and whether the employee followed the required notification process. They should avoid making medical, legal or disciplinary conclusions outside their competence.

### 2. Distinguish the issue before choosing a response
Late arrival, unauthorised absence, poor performance, illness-related incapacity and deliberate refusal to follow an instruction are not automatically the same management problem. The manager should establish the facts and use the correct authorised route.

For example, an employee who does not understand a new stock-count process may need training. An employee who understands a reasonable instruction but deliberately refuses it presents a different issue. A health-related limitation may require an incapacity process rather than a misconduct response.

### 3. Fair process protects both people and the business
South Africa's Code of Practice: Dismissal, effective from 4 September 2025, provides guidance on fair reasons and fair procedures for dismissal and repealed the previous Schedule 8 dismissal code. Retail managers do not need to memorise legal wording, but they must understand a core management principle: serious employment decisions require legitimate reasons and an appropriate fair process.

The Code also recognises informal advice and correction for minor violations in suitable circumstances and more serious responses where conduct or repetition warrants them. This does not create an automatic sequence for every case. Managers should follow current organisational procedures and obtain HR or senior guidance where required.

### 4. Consistency requires judgement, not automation
Two cases may look similar but contain different facts. One employee may have reported an emergency through the correct channel while another simply failed to attend. Consistency means using the same standards and decision principles, while considering relevant circumstances and evidence.

Managers should also check their own behaviour for bias. Friendship, frustration, seniority, sales performance or personal disagreement should not determine whether a concern is recorded or escalated.

### 5. Document objectively
A useful management record states dates, times, observable events, the standard discussed, the employee's explanation, agreed actions and follow-up. Avoid emotional labels. “Employee arrived at 09:18 for a 09:00 scheduled shift and explained that…” is more useful than “employee has a terrible attitude.”

### 6. Escalate appropriately
Managers should escalate complex or serious matters according to policy. Examples include suspected fraud, harassment, discrimination, violence, significant safety breaches, repeated serious conduct concerns, incapacity requiring specialist assessment or possible termination of employment.

## Worked Retail Example
A high-performing salesperson arrives late four times. The manager has previously ignored the lateness because the employee exceeds sales targets. Other employees begin complaining about unfair treatment.

The correct response is not to punish the employee suddenly because colleagues complained. The manager should verify records, apply the attendance standard, hear the employee's explanation, follow the authorised process and correct the previous inconsistency going forward. High sales do not remove attendance responsibilities.

## Practical Activity
Design a simple attendance-control checklist for a store manager. Include roster verification, attendance evidence, notification checks, employee explanation, policy reference, action or escalation, documentation and follow-up.

Then explain how you would prevent the checklist from becoming an automatic punishment tool.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why are reliable attendance records important?
2. Why must misconduct and incapacity not automatically be treated the same way?
3. What does consistency mean in people management?
4. What makes a management note objective?
5. Name three matters that may require escalation.

## Feedback
Reliable records support accurate decisions. Different workplace problems require different responses. Consistency uses common standards while considering the facts of each case. Objective notes describe observable facts, explanations and actions rather than personal labels. Serious misconduct, discrimination, violence, fraud or complex incapacity are examples of matters that may require escalation.

## Key Takeaways
- Attendance is both a people and an operational control issue.
- Verify evidence before reaching conclusions.
- Use the correct route for the type of problem involved.
- Fair process and consistency strengthen management credibility.
- Document facts and escalate matters beyond your authority.
