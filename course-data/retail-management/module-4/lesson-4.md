# Professional Certificate in Retail Management & Team Leadership
## Module 4: Team Leadership & People Management
### Lesson 4: Coaching, Feedback & On-the-Job Development

**Estimated study time:** 50–60 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Distinguish coaching from formal disciplinary action.
2. Give specific, evidence-based feedback.
3. Structure a short on-the-job coaching conversation.
4. Use practice and follow-up to build capability.
5. Recognise when coaching is not the appropriate process.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Retail managers develop people in real operating conditions. Employees need to learn product knowledge, service behaviours, replenishment standards, transaction controls and problem-solving. Waiting for formal classroom training is not enough. Managers must coach during normal work while distinguishing genuine capability development from conduct or formal performance matters that require another process.

## Key Terms
- **Coaching** — structured support to improve capability or execution.
- **Feedback** — information about observed performance compared with an expectation.
- **Capability** — knowledge, skill and ability to perform required work.
- **Reinforcement** — recognising and strengthening effective behaviour.
- **Practice** — repeated application of a skill to improve competence.
- **Follow-up** — later check to determine whether improvement has occurred.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Coach observable behaviour
Avoid labels such as “lazy” or “bad attitude” when the actual issue is that a specific standard was not met. Describe what you observed and what the expected behaviour is.

### 2. Use a simple coaching structure
A practical conversation can follow:
1. context — explain the situation;
2. observation — describe what happened;
3. standard — clarify what should happen;
4. employee view — ask for their perspective;
5. practice/action — agree how to improve;
6. follow-up — set a check point.

### 3. Reinforce what is working
Coaching is not only correction. Specific recognition helps employees understand which behaviours to repeat: “You acknowledged the waiting customer, explained the delay and kept them updated—that is the service standard we need.”

### 4. Diagnose before prescribing
A gap may result from lack of knowledge, lack of practice, unclear expectations, missing resources, workload or conduct. Training cannot fix every cause. Ask questions and examine evidence.

### 5. Demonstrate, practise and observe
For practical retail skills, a useful method is explain → demonstrate → employee practises → observe → feedback → repeat. The employee should perform the skill, not only hear about it.

### 6. Make feedback timely and proportionate
Feedback close to the event is easier to understand, provided the timing and setting are appropriate. Correct sensitive issues privately rather than embarrassing employees in front of customers or colleagues.

### 7. Document where required
Company procedures may require records of coaching, performance discussions or training. Accurate records support continuity and fair follow-up. Do not create unofficial “disciplinary” records outside approved processes.

### 8. Know when coaching is insufficient
Serious misconduct allegations, repeated deliberate breaches, formal incapacity/performance processes, harassment or other significant matters may require escalation. Current South African CCMA guidance distinguishes counselling/coaching-type correction from disciplinary action and emphasises fair process. Managers should follow current employer procedures and HR guidance rather than improvise sanctions.

## Worked Example
A new cashier forgets to explain the loyalty prompt and appears uncertain about the sequence. The supervisor first checks whether training was completed. After finding that the employee had only observed the process once, the supervisor demonstrates the sequence, lets the employee practise, observes several transactions and follows up later.

This is a capability gap suitable for coaching. The response would differ if an experienced employee knowingly bypassed a critical control after repeated instruction.

## Practical Activity
Write a six-step coaching conversation for an employee whose shelf-replenishment accuracy is poor. Include the observation, expected standard, one diagnostic question, practice action and follow-up date/point.

## Knowledge Check
1. What should feedback describe instead of using personal labels?
2. Name the six coaching stages in this lesson.
3. Why should managers reinforce good performance?
4. Name three possible causes of a performance gap.
5. What is the explain–demonstrate–practise method used for?
6. Why should sensitive correction usually be private?
7. Why might coaching be documented?
8. Give one situation where a manager should escalate rather than rely only on coaching.

## Feedback / Explanation
1. Observable behaviour or results compared with the standard.
2. Context, observation, standard, employee view, practice/action and follow-up.
3. To clarify and strengthen behaviours that should be repeated.
4. Examples: knowledge, practice, unclear expectations, resources, workload or conduct.
5. Developing practical skills through active performance.
6. To protect dignity and support a constructive conversation.
7. For continuity, evidence of development and fair follow-up where policy requires it.
8. Examples: serious misconduct, harassment, formal performance/incapacity matters or repeated deliberate breaches.

## Key Takeaways
- Coach behaviour and capability, not personality labels.
- Diagnose the cause before choosing the intervention.
- Effective coaching includes practice and follow-up.
- Recognition is part of development.
- Use formal processes when the matter goes beyond ordinary coaching.
