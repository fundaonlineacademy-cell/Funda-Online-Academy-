# Professional Certificate in Retail Management & Team Leadership
## Module 2: Store Operations & Execution Excellence
### Lesson 3: Merchandising Standards & Product Availability

**Estimated study time:** 55–65 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the management purpose of merchandising standards.
2. Distinguish physical stock from customer-facing availability.
3. Identify common causes of shelf or floor availability gaps.
4. Conduct a structured merchandising and availability check.
5. Assign corrective actions and verify completion.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A store can own stock and still lose a sale because the customer cannot find or access it. Product may be sitting in a backroom, placed in the wrong location, missing a price, displayed poorly or unavailable in the size or variant the customer needs. Retail managers therefore control both inventory and presentation.

Merchandising is not decoration. It helps customers understand the offer, locate products and make purchasing decisions while supporting brand, promotional and operational standards.

## Key Terms
- **Merchandising** — presenting and organising products to support customer shopping and commercial objectives.
- **Availability** — the extent to which the product a customer wants can actually be purchased.
- **Planogram** — approved visual or positional guide for arranging products where applicable.
- **Facing** — visible product presentation at the front of a shelf or display.
- **Out-of-stock** — product not available for sale when required.
- **Replenishment** — moving available stock into the customer-facing selling area.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Merchandising turns inventory into a customer offer
Stock only creates commercial value when customers can identify it, evaluate it and purchase it. Good merchandising combines logical placement, clear pricing, suitable presentation and reliable availability.

### 2. Physical stock is not the same as availability
Suppose the system shows twelve units of a product. If all twelve are in the backroom while the shelf is empty, the store has stock but poor customer-facing availability. Managers must investigate both records and physical execution.

### 3. Follow approved merchandising standards
Retailers may prescribe layouts, promotional displays, product adjacencies, shelf allocations, size sequencing or visual presentation. Managers should ensure the approved standard is understood and maintained rather than allowing displays to drift during trading.

### 4. Protect price and product clarity
Customers should be able to connect the product with the correct price and offer. Missing, outdated or confusing tickets can cause complaints, abandoned purchases and unnecessary till disputes. Where pricing is centrally controlled, the store still has responsibility for accurate execution and escalation of discrepancies.

### 5. Common causes of availability gaps
A gap may result from:
- genuine zero stock;
- delayed replenishment;
- inaccurate inventory records;
- stock in the wrong location;
- receiving or processing delays;
- damages or unrecorded losses;
- unusually high demand;
- poor ordering or allocation;
- system or master-data problems.

Do not assume every empty space has the same cause.

### 6. Use structured floor walks
A useful merchandising walk can examine availability, ticketing, promotional compliance, cleanliness, display condition and customer navigation. Record significant exceptions, assign owners and set realistic completion times.

### 7. Prioritise commercially
Not every gap has equal impact. Priority may be given to high-demand lines, advertised promotions, essential products, major customer complaints or categories with repeated lost-sales risk. This is management judgement supported by data and standards.

### 8. Verify the correction
A message saying “done” is not always sufficient. Managers should sample or inspect completed actions, particularly when the issue is high impact or recurring.

## Worked Example
A supermarket promotion advertises a popular household product. At 11:00, the promotional display is almost empty, yet the inventory system indicates significant stock on hand.

The manager checks the backroom and finds unopened cases awaiting replenishment. The immediate action is to replenish safely and correct the display. The management action goes further: establish why the promotional line was not monitored, assign a replenishment frequency for the rest of the day and check whether similar promotional products are at risk.

## Practical Activity
Create a merchandising walk sheet with these headings: Area/Product, Expected Standard, Observed Gap, Customer/Commercial Impact, Owner, Due Time and Verification.

Complete five fictional examples from a clothing, grocery, pharmacy, hardware or general-merchandise store.

## Key Takeaways
- Customer-facing availability is a management outcome, not merely a stock figure.
- Merchandising should follow approved standards and make the offer clear.
- Empty spaces require diagnosis, not assumptions.
- Managers prioritise high-impact exceptions and assign clear ownership.
- Verification and root-cause follow-up turn correction into sustained improvement.
