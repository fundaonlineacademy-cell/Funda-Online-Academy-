# Module 8 — Retail Management Capstone & Career Leadership
## Lesson 1 — Capstone Brief: Managing a Simulated Retail Store

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- explain the purpose and evidence requirements of the retail-management capstone;
- interpret a simulated store brief as a manager rather than as a single-task employee;
- identify operational, customer, people, commercial, stock and technology priorities;
- organise a professional evidence pack for management review; and
- distinguish facts, assumptions, risks and recommended actions.

## Why This Matters
A retail manager rarely receives one neat problem at a time. A real shift can include an absent employee, a late delivery, queue pressure, a customer complaint, a promotion discrepancy, a stock variance and a system exception at the same time. Management capability is therefore demonstrated through integration: deciding what matters most, protecting critical controls, allocating work, communicating clearly and following up.

This capstone brings together the entire course. You will manage a fictional South African store scenario and produce evidence showing how you diagnose problems, plan improvements, lead people, control commercial and stock risks, use management information and review results.

### Key Terms
**Capstone:** An integrated final learning activity demonstrating combined competence.
**Management brief:** A structured description of the business situation, expectations and constraints.
**Evidence pack:** Documents and records that demonstrate analysis, decisions and completed work.
**Priority:** An issue requiring attention according to impact, urgency and risk.
**Assumption:** Something believed to be true but not yet verified.
**Management rationale:** The reason supporting a chosen decision or action.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. The simulated store
For the capstone, imagine you have been appointed acting store manager of **Ubuntu Retail Hub**, a fictional medium-sized general merchandise store. The scenario is educational and uses no real customer or employee information.

The store has experienced mixed results. Sales are below target in several categories, customer complaints about queues have increased, selected promotional lines are frequently unavailable, a recent stock count produced unexplained variances, and some employees say shift responsibilities are unclear. Online collection orders are growing, but handover delays have been reported. Senior management wants a practical recovery plan rather than a list of excuses.

### 2. Your role
You are responsible for coordinating the store response. You are not expected to personally perform every task. You must demonstrate the manager's cycle:

**Observe → verify → prioritise → plan → allocate → monitor → correct → review.**

Your decisions should protect customer service, people fairness, stock and cash controls, safety, information, and commercial performance.

### 3. The capstone evidence pack
Across Module 8 you will build a portfolio containing:
- store-performance diagnosis;
- priority and risk register;
- operations and customer-service improvement plan;
- people leadership and shift plan;
- commercial and stock action plan;
- management meeting/reporting pack;
- professional career portfolio elements; and
- final simulation review with continuous-improvement plan.

A strong evidence pack is concise enough to use, but detailed enough for another manager to understand what was found, what was decided, who owns each action and how completion will be measured.

### 4. Separate facts from assumptions
Suppose the brief says queue complaints increased by 30%. That is a reported fact within the simulation. Saying, “The reason is that staff are lazy” is an unsupported assumption. A manager should test causes such as deployment, peak-time demand, till availability, process delays, training or system problems.

Use four labels during diagnosis:
1. **Fact** — supported by the scenario or verified evidence.
2. **Assumption** — requires checking.
3. **Risk** — something that could cause harm or loss.
4. **Action** — a controlled response with ownership and timing.

### 5. Prioritisation
Not every issue deserves the same response time. Consider:
- safety and legal/compliance risk;
- customer impact;
- financial or stock exposure;
- urgency;
- number of people affected;
- operational dependency; and
- whether the problem will worsen if delayed.

A blocked emergency exit, for example, would require immediate control before a low-impact display improvement.

### Worked Example
The capstone brief shows: queue waiting time high between 16:00 and 18:00; one till often unavailable; lunch breaks overlap with the beginning of the peak; and transaction volume rises sharply after 16:00.

A weak response is: “Tell cashiers to work faster.”

A management response is: verify till availability, review hourly demand, adjust break/deployment coverage within approved requirements, resolve the unavailable till, assign a queue monitor during peak trading and measure waiting time after the intervention.

### Feedback

2. A fact is supported/verified; an assumption still requires evidence.
3. Examples include safety/compliance, customer impact, financial/stock exposure, urgency and operational dependency.
4. So responsibility and completion can be monitored.
5. Observe, verify, prioritise, plan, allocate, monitor, correct and review.

## Key Takeaways
- Retail management competence is demonstrated by integrated decision-making.
- Diagnose before blaming or prescribing solutions.
- Separate verified facts from assumptions.
- Prioritise according to risk, impact and urgency.
- Build evidence that another manager could follow.
