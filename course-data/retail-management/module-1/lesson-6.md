# Professional Certificate in Retail Management & Team Leadership
## Module 1: South African Retail Management Foundations
### Lesson 6: Management Priorities, Daily Planning & Store Walks

**Estimated study time:** 55–60 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Build a practical daily retail management plan.
2. Prioritise tasks using customer, risk and commercial impact.
3. Conduct purposeful opening and trading store walks.
4. Allocate ownership and deadlines for corrective actions.
5. Review the plan as trading conditions change.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Retail managers rarely have enough time to do everything at once. Strong management therefore depends on choosing the right priorities, assigning work clearly and checking the store repeatedly. A store walk is not simply walking around; it is a structured observation and control activity.

## Key Terms
- **Priority** — task or issue requiring attention before lower-impact work.
- **Store walk** — structured inspection of store conditions and execution.
- **Trading readiness** — condition in which the store is prepared to serve customers safely and effectively.
- **Action owner** — person responsible for completing an agreed action.
- **Deadline** — time by which action must be completed.
- **Reprioritisation** — changing priorities when credible new information appears.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Start before the doors open
Review expected trading, staffing, deliveries, promotions, known maintenance issues, previous handover and important stock/service exceptions. The aim is to enter trading with a plan rather than immediately reacting.

### 2. Use a priority test
A useful sequence is:
1. immediate safety/security risk;
2. serious customer or legal/control risk;
3. trading blockers such as tills or essential stock availability;
4. high commercial/service impact;
5. routine operational work;
6. lower-value administrative tasks.

This does not remove judgement, but it helps managers explain why one issue was handled before another.

### 3. Opening store walk
Check areas such as entrances, customer routes, cleanliness, promotional execution, shelf/rack readiness, visible pricing, high-priority availability, tills, staff readiness and known hazards.

### 4. Observe through the customer's eyes
Walk the journey a customer experiences. Can they enter easily? Is the offer understandable? Are key products accessible? Are queues controlled? Is help available?

### 5. Observe through the control lens
Also look behind the experience: unattended stock, blocked exits, receiving congestion, unsecured sensitive areas, unexplained price labels, backroom conditions and incomplete control records.

### 6. Turn observations into actions
A useful action contains:
- issue;
- required result;
- owner;
- deadline;
- verification method.

“Fix the aisle” is weak. “Thandi: remove empty cartons, replenish top promotional lines and confirm ticket accuracy in Aisle 4 by 09:30; supervisor rechecks at 09:40” is controllable.

### 7. Repeat store walks
Conditions change after opening. Peak traffic, deliveries, staff breaks and customer returns can create new gaps. Short focused walks during the day keep management connected to reality.

### 8. Reprioritise deliberately
A new urgent issue may replace a planned task. Record or communicate the change so abandoned actions are not forgotten.

## Worked Example
At 08:30 the manager plans a stockroom review. At 08:40 a till network problem creates queues and customer frustration. The manager temporarily reprioritises: deploys trained staff to manage queues, escalates the system issue, checks alternative payment procedures and communicates with customers. Once trading stabilises, the deferred stockroom review is rescheduled rather than silently dropped.

## Practical Activity
Create a daily manager control sheet with opening priorities, first store walk, peak-trading checks, second walk, outstanding actions and closing handover. Add an owner, deadline and verification field for every corrective action.

## Key Takeaways
- Daily retail management begins with a plan but remains adaptable.
- Priority should reflect risk, customer impact and commercial importance.
- Store walks are structured management controls.
- Every important corrective action needs ownership and verification.
- Reprioritisation should be deliberate, communicated and followed through.
