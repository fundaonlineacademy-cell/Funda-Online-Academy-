# Professional Certificate in Retail Management & Team Leadership
## Module 1: South African Retail Management Foundations
### Lesson 4: Customer, Product & Channel Fundamentals

**Estimated study time:** 50–60 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain the relationship between customer need, product offer and retail channel.
2. Identify common customer shopping missions.
3. Explain assortment and availability at management level.
4. Distinguish physical, digital and connected retail journeys.
5. Diagnose basic gaps between customer expectation and store execution.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Retail performance starts with a simple question: can the business provide the right product or solution to the right customer through a convenient and trustworthy shopping experience? Managers influence this through availability, product presentation, staff knowledge, channel coordination and service execution.

## Key Terms
- **Customer proposition** — combination of product, price, convenience, service and experience offered to customers.
- **Assortment** — range of products selected for sale.
- **Availability** — whether the product the customer wants can actually be purchased when needed.
- **Shopping mission** — purpose of a customer's shopping interaction.
- **Channel** — route used to shop or transact.
- **Omnichannel** — coordinated customer experience across connected physical and digital channels.
- **Substitution** — alternative product offered when the preferred item is unavailable.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Start with the customer mission
A customer buying an emergency phone charger has a different mission from someone comparing televisions for a future purchase. A grocery top-up differs from a monthly bulk shop. Understanding the mission helps managers judge whether speed, advice, assortment, price or convenience matters most in that interaction.

### 2. The product offer
Managers may not choose the national assortment, but they influence whether approved products are correctly received, replenished, displayed, priced and explained. A strong range has little value when key items remain in the backroom or are incorrectly labelled.

### 3. Availability is both a stock and execution issue
An item can be physically present in the building but unavailable to the customer because it is not replenished, cannot be found, is blocked from sale, has incorrect system status or is reserved for another channel.

Managers should investigate the reason for the gap instead of assuming every empty shelf means the supplier failed.

### 4. Product knowledge supports trust
Employees do not need to memorise every technical detail, but they should know the information required for their role, use approved product sources and avoid inventing claims. Where specialist advice is required, refer appropriately.

### 5. Physical retail
In-store customers experience layout, cleanliness, staff interaction, queues, product condition, price communication and availability directly. Small execution failures can combine into a poor overall experience.

### 6. Digital retail
Customers may browse websites or apps, place orders, request collection, receive delivery or contact digital support. Store managers may be responsible for part of this fulfilment journey even when the sale began online.

### 7. Connected journeys
A customer may research online, visit the store to inspect the product, order through an app and return it to a branch. Policies and information need to work across these touchpoints.

### 8. Diagnose the gap
When a customer need is not met, ask:
1. Was the required product/range approved?
2. Was stock physically available?
3. Was the system information accurate?
4. Was the item customer-accessible?
5. Did the employee understand the request?
6. Was a suitable alternative or channel offered?
7. Does the problem require escalation?

## Worked Example
A customer sees a kettle online as “available” at a branch but cannot find it on the shelf. The employee simply says it is sold out. A stronger response is to check the approved inventory process. The manager discovers six units are in the receiving area but were not moved to the sales floor after delivery.

The issue is not supplier availability; it is internal replenishment execution. Corrective action should therefore address the handoff from receiving to the floor.

## Practical Activity
Choose three customer missions for a fictional retailer. For each mission, describe the expected product need, most important service factor, likely channel and one execution failure that could lose the sale.

5. Incorrect information damages trust and can create risk.

## Key Takeaways
- Retail management connects customer needs with product and channel execution.
- Availability is not only a purchasing issue; it is also an operational discipline.
- Product information must be accurate and within role competence.
- Customers increasingly move between physical and digital channels.
- Managers should diagnose the actual cause of availability and service gaps.
