# Module 7 — POS, Digital Retail & Management Information
## Lesson 3: Retail Data, Reports & Management Dashboards

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- distinguish raw retail data from useful management information;
- identify common store reports and dashboard measures;
- select measures that support operational decisions;
- recognise misleading or incomplete interpretations; and
- use dashboards as part of a management review cycle.

## Why This Matters
Retail systems can generate large amounts of data. More data does not automatically produce better management. A strong manager knows which information matters, how measures relate to one another and what action should follow.

### Key Terms
**Data:** Recorded facts or observations.  
**Management information:** Data organised and interpreted for decision-making.  
**Dashboard:** A focused display of selected measures.  
**KPI:** Key performance indicator used to monitor an important result or driver.  
**Trend:** Direction or pattern across comparable periods.  
**Exception:** A result outside expected or acceptable conditions.

## 1. Common Retail Information
Store managers may use reports covering:
- sales and revenue;
- transactions;
- conversion;
- average transaction value;
- units per transaction;
- margin;
- markdowns;
- stock availability;
- shrinkage or stock variance;
- labour productivity;
- refunds, voids and overrides; and
- customer-service indicators.

The exact measures depend on the business model and approved systems.

## 2. Data Is Not Yet a Decision
Suppose sales are 8% below target. That tells management there is a gap, but not why.

The manager may need to investigate:
- traffic;
- conversion;
- availability;
- transaction value;
- promotion execution;
- staffing;
- customer feedback; and
- external trading conditions.

A KPI should therefore trigger questions, not automatic conclusions.

## 3. Designing a Useful Dashboard
A dashboard should be focused. Too many measures can hide what matters.

A practical daily dashboard might show:
1. sales versus target;
2. conversion or transactions;
3. average transaction value;
4. availability exceptions;
5. labour or service pressure;
6. major POS exceptions; and
7. priority actions.

A weekly or monthly dashboard may add trends and broader commercial measures.

## 4. Compare Like with Like
Data becomes misleading when managers compare incompatible periods. For example:
- Saturday versus a quiet Monday;
- a promotional week versus a normal week;
- a full trading day versus a shortened day.

Good interpretation considers context.

## 5. Leading and Lagging Indicators
Some measures describe outcomes already achieved, such as completed sales. Others may provide earlier operational signals, such as shelf availability, queue pressure or staffing gaps.

Managers benefit from combining results with operational drivers.

## 6. From Dashboard to Action
A dashboard is valuable when it leads to management action:
1. review the result;
2. identify material gaps;
3. investigate likely drivers;
4. assign action;
5. monitor execution; and
6. review whether the result improves.

## Worked Example
Sales are down, but customer traffic is unchanged. The dashboard shows conversion has fallen and several high-demand products are out of stock. Management therefore prioritises availability and selling execution rather than assuming the problem is insufficient marketing.

## Practical Activity — Build a Store Dashboard
Create a one-page dashboard containing six KPIs. For each, add:
- current result;
- target or comparator;
- status;
- likely driver;
- action owner; and
- next review date.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is the difference between data and management information?  
2. Why can one KPI rarely explain a problem completely?  
3. Why should comparable periods be used?  
4. What makes a dashboard actionable?

### Feedback
Management information turns relevant data into decision support. KPIs interact with operational drivers. Comparability reduces misleading conclusions. Dashboards become actionable when gaps lead to assigned, measurable follow-up.

## Key Takeaways
- More data is not automatically better management.
- Select KPIs that connect to store priorities.
- Investigate drivers before drawing conclusions.
- Context and comparability matter.
- Dashboards should lead to action and review.

**Suggested visual:** Dashboard flow: Measure → Compare → Diagnose → Act → Review.
