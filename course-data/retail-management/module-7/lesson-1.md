# Module 7 — POS, Digital Retail & Management Information
## Lesson 1: POS Systems & Transaction Control

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- explain the operational role of a point-of-sale (POS) system;
- identify the main stages of a controlled retail transaction;
- distinguish ordinary transactions from exceptions requiring additional authority;
- explain why user access, evidence and reconciliation matter; and
- supervise POS activity without weakening customer service.

## Why This Matters
The POS is more than a till. It is a control point where products, prices, payments, customer interactions and management information meet. A transaction entered incorrectly can affect cash, inventory, sales reporting, customer trust and later management decisions.

Retail managers therefore need enough POS knowledge to supervise controls even when they are not system administrators.

### Key Terms
**POS:** The system used to record and process retail transactions.  
**Transaction:** A recorded commercial event such as a sale, refund or authorised adjustment.  
**Tender:** The payment method used by the customer.  
**User access:** Permissions assigned to an authorised system user.  
**Reconciliation:** Comparing related records to identify and explain differences.  
**Exception:** A transaction or event outside the ordinary process that needs additional review or authority.

## 1. What a POS System Does
A modern POS may connect several functions:
- product identification;
- price lookup;
- promotion application;
- tax and transaction calculation;
- payment processing;
- receipt production;
- inventory movement;
- sales reporting; and
- exception logging.

This integration creates efficiency, but it also means one incorrect action can affect several records.

## 2. The Controlled Transaction Flow
A typical sale should follow an authorised sequence:
1. identify the correct item;
2. apply the approved price and promotion rules;
3. confirm quantities;
4. process the customer's approved payment method;
5. complete the transaction;
6. provide the required receipt or transaction evidence; and
7. ensure the transaction is reflected correctly in system records.

Managers should teach teams not to create informal shortcuts simply because a queue is long.

## 3. User Accountability
Where systems support individual user accounts, staff should use their own authorised credentials. Shared passwords weaken accountability because management may no longer be able to establish who performed an action.

Access should also match job responsibility. A cashier may need ordinary sale functions, while certain overrides or refunds may require a supervisor or manager.

The principle is simple: **access should be sufficient to perform the job, but not broader than necessary.**

## 4. Ordinary Transactions and Exceptions
An ordinary sale follows the normal process. Exceptions may include:
- price overrides;
- voids;
- refunds;
- unusual discounts;
- cancelled transactions;
- tender corrections; or
- repeated transaction reversals.

Exceptions are not automatically wrongdoing. They may be legitimate. However, they carry greater control risk and therefore require the correct evidence, authority and review.

## 5. Reconciliation
At the end of a shift or defined period, management may compare:
- POS sales totals;
- tender totals;
- cash or other settlement evidence;
- refunds and voids;
- relevant inventory movements; and
- exception reports.

The purpose is not merely to find shortages. Reconciliation helps establish whether the different records tell a consistent story.

## Worked Example
A cashier reports that a customer changed payment method after a transaction had already been partially processed. Instead of creating an undocumented workaround, the cashier calls the supervisor. The supervisor follows the authorised correction procedure and ensures the resulting exception is recorded.

The transaction may take slightly longer, but the store preserves both customer service and control integrity.

## Practical Activity — POS Control Map
Draw a simple POS transaction from item scan to completed payment. Mark:
- normal control points;
- actions that require supervisor authority;
- evidence produced; and
- records that could be affected by an error.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why is a POS more than a payment device?  
2. Why should staff avoid shared user credentials?  
3. Give three examples of POS exceptions.  
4. What is the purpose of reconciliation?

### Feedback
A POS connects transactions with financial, stock and management records. Individual credentials support accountability. Exceptions include refunds, voids and overrides. Reconciliation compares related records so differences can be identified and explained.

## Key Takeaways
- POS accuracy affects sales, stock, payments and management information.
- Access should reflect authorised job responsibilities.
- Exceptions require stronger evidence and review than routine transactions.
- Reconciliation is a core management control.
- Speed should not be achieved by bypassing essential controls.
