# Module 7 — POS, Digital Retail & Management Information
## Lesson 4: Using Sales and Stock Data for Decisions

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- combine sales and inventory information;
- identify basic patterns in demand and availability;
- distinguish symptoms from possible causes;
- prioritise store actions using evidence; and
- explain the risks of making decisions from incomplete data.

## Why This Matters
Sales data tells managers what customers bought. Stock data helps explain what customers could buy. Looking at only one side can produce poor decisions.

A product with low sales may be unpopular—or it may simply have been unavailable. A product with high stock may be overstocked—or it may be preparing for a planned promotion. Context matters.

### Key Terms
**Sell-through:** The relationship between units sold and available or received stock over a defined period.  
**Availability:** Whether saleable product is available when required.  
**Stock cover:** A planning view of how long available stock may support expected demand, based on approved business methods.  
**Slow mover:** A product selling more slowly than expected.  
**Fast mover:** A product selling rapidly relative to expectations or available stock.

## 1. Connect Demand and Supply Evidence
Useful combinations include:
- units sold + stock on hand;
- sales trend + availability trend;
- promotion performance + received stock;
- lost-sales feedback + out-of-stock reports;
- stock variance + unusual POS activity.

Combining information reduces the risk of simplistic conclusions.

## 2. Diagnose Low Sales
When a product sells poorly, ask:
- Was it available?
- Was it displayed correctly?
- Was the price correct?
- Was the promotion executed?
- Did customer demand change?
- Is the system record accurate?

Only after diagnosis should management decide whether the answer is replenishment, merchandising, pricing escalation, promotion execution or another action.

## 3. Diagnose High Sales
High sales can be positive but may create risk if stock is not replenished. A fast-selling product may need:
- more frequent replenishment;
- closer availability checks;
- expedited supply escalation;
- revised display capacity; or
- careful monitoring during promotions.

## 4. Prioritisation
Managers rarely have enough time to investigate every SKU equally. Prioritise using factors such as:
- sales value;
- margin contribution;
- customer importance;
- stockout risk;
- variance risk;
- promotion status; and
- recurrence.

## 5. Avoid False Precision
Retail data can contain timing differences, recording errors and incomplete context. Managers should not treat every number as perfect simply because it appears on a screen.

Where a decision is material, verify important assumptions and source records.

## Worked Example
A store sees weak sales in a promoted beverage line. The POS report suggests demand is low, but stock records show repeated zero-on-hand periods during peak hours. The manager confirms shelf gaps and late replenishment. The correct response is operational availability improvement, not immediately cancelling the promotion.

## Practical Activity — Sales/Stock Matrix
Choose five fictional products and classify each as:
- high sales/high stock;
- high sales/low stock;
- low sales/high stock; or
- low sales/low stock.

For each, write two questions a manager should investigate before taking action.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why can low sales be caused by poor availability?  
2. Why should high-selling products receive replenishment attention?  
3. Name three factors used to prioritise product investigation.  
4. Why should managers verify material data assumptions?

### Feedback
Customers cannot buy unavailable products. Fast movers can quickly create lost-sales risk. Prioritisation may consider value, customer importance and recurrence. Important decisions should account for possible data errors or missing context.

## Key Takeaways
- Combine sales and stock evidence.
- Low sales do not automatically mean low demand.
- High sales can create availability risk.
- Prioritise material products and exceptions.
- Verify critical data before major decisions.
