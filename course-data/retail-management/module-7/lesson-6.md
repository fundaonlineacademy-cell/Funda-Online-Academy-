# Module 7 — POS, Digital Retail & Management Information
## Lesson 6: Customer and Business Information Protection

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- recognise personal and commercially sensitive information in retail operations;
- explain why access should be limited to legitimate business needs;
- apply practical information-security habits at store level;
- respond appropriately to suspected information compromise; and
- explain a retail manager's role in supporting POPIA-aware practices.

## Why This Matters
Retail stores process information through POS systems, loyalty programmes, online orders, customer queries, CCTV, employee records and management reports. Poor handling can harm customers, employees and the business.

South Africa's Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA) places responsibilities on organisations that process personal information. Retail managers do not need to act as privacy lawyers, but they must follow approved organisational controls and escalate suspected compromises promptly.

### Key Terms
**Personal information:** Information relating to an identifiable person or, where applicable under law, identifiable entities.  
**Access control:** Restricting information or system functions to authorised users.  
**Confidentiality:** Protecting information from unauthorised disclosure.  
**Security compromise:** A situation in which personal information may have been accessed, acquired, lost or disclosed contrary to required safeguards.  
**Least privilege:** Providing only the access reasonably required for a role.

## 1. Information in a Retail Store
Potentially sensitive information can include:
- customer contact details;
- loyalty information;
- online-order details;
- transaction records;
- employee records;
- CCTV footage;
- passwords and access credentials;
- internal commercial reports; and
- supplier or business information.

Not every employee needs access to every category.

## 2. Practical Security Safeguards
Store-level controls may include:
- individual user accounts;
- strong authentication practices;
- screen locking;
- secure physical storage;
- controlled printing;
- careful disposal of records;
- restricted access to reports;
- avoiding unauthorised copying or photographing; and
- following approved device and communication rules.

The Information Regulator's guidance emphasises appropriate technical and organisational measures, identifying foreseeable risks and maintaining safeguards as risks change.

## 3. Do Not Collect or Share “Just in Case”
A useful operational principle is to handle information only where there is a legitimate authorised purpose. Staff should not browse customer records out of curiosity, share screenshots casually or collect extra information without a valid process.

## 4. Password and Credential Discipline
Employees should not:
- share passwords;
- write credentials where unauthorised people can access them;
- use another person's account; or
- approve system prompts they do not understand.

Managers should escalate access problems rather than creating insecure workarounds.

## 5. Suspected Security Compromise
Examples include:
- a device containing customer data is lost;
- customer records are emailed to the wrong recipient;
- unauthorised access is suspected;
- printed personal information is found unsecured; or
- credentials may have been exposed.

The manager should follow the organisation's incident process promptly. This may include preserving evidence, limiting further exposure and escalating to designated privacy/security personnel. Staff should not independently decide legal notification requirements unless authorised to do so.

## Worked Example
An employee accidentally sends an order report containing customer details to the wrong internal distribution list. The employee wants to delete the sent message and say nothing.

The manager instead follows the incident process, records what happened and escalates promptly so authorised personnel can assess risk and required action.

## Practical Activity — Information Risk Walk
Inspect a fictional store and identify risks involving:
- unlocked screens;
- printed customer details;
- shared credentials;
- unattended order paperwork;
- CCTV access; and
- disposal of documents.

For each risk, propose one practical control.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why should system access be role-based?  
2. Give three examples of retail personal information.  
3. What should happen after a suspected information compromise?  
4. Why is password sharing risky?

### Feedback
Role-based access limits unnecessary exposure. Retail personal information can include customer contact, order and loyalty details. Suspected compromises should be escalated through the authorised incident process. Shared passwords weaken both security and accountability.

## Key Takeaways
- Retail managers handle information risk every day.
- Access should match legitimate responsibilities.
- Use approved technical and organisational safeguards.
- Do not create insecure shortcuts when access fails.
- Escalate suspected compromises promptly through organisational procedures.
