# Lesson 5 — Average Transaction Value, Units per Transaction & Conversion

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- explain average transaction value, units per transaction and conversion;
- calculate the three measures correctly from store data;
- distinguish between customer traffic, conversion and basket performance;
- diagnose common reasons for weak KPI results;
- select practical management actions without encouraging inappropriate selling behaviour.

## Why This Matters
A sales total tells a manager what happened, but it does not always explain why. Two stores can produce the same revenue through very different patterns. One may attract many visitors but convert few of them into buyers. Another may convert strongly but have small baskets. A third may have good basket value but insufficient customer traffic. Retail managers therefore need measures that break sales performance into manageable components.

## Key Terms
**Average transaction value (ATV):** sales value divided by the number of completed transactions.

**Units per transaction (UPT):** units sold divided by the number of completed transactions.

**Conversion:** the proportion of relevant customer opportunities or visitors that become purchasing transactions, where reliable traffic data exists.

**Traffic:** customer visits or another approved measure of selling opportunities.

**Basket:** the collection of items in one customer transaction.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Average Transaction Value
ATV shows the average rand value generated by each transaction.

**ATV = Total sales value ÷ Number of transactions**

If a store records R96,000 in sales from 800 transactions, ATV is R120.

ATV can rise because customers buy higher-value products, purchase more units, respond to relevant add-on recommendations, or because the product mix changes. It can also rise while customer numbers fall, so managers should never interpret ATV alone.

### 2. Units per Transaction
UPT measures the average number of units included in each transaction.

**UPT = Units sold ÷ Number of transactions**

If 1,520 units are sold in 800 transactions, UPT is 1.9 units.

UPT helps a manager understand basket depth. A low result may indicate missed complementary-product opportunities, poor availability, weak merchandising, unsuitable assortment, limited staff product knowledge or simply a customer mission that normally involves single-item purchases. The KPI must therefore be interpreted in context.

### 3. Conversion
Where a retailer has a reliable traffic-counting method, conversion helps distinguish attracting customers from successfully serving and selling to them.

**Conversion rate = Transactions ÷ Relevant customer traffic × 100**

If 1,000 relevant visits produce 300 transactions, conversion is 30%.

The denominator must be defined consistently. Staff movements, children, repeat entries or collection-only customers can distort some traffic systems. Managers should use the retailer's approved definition rather than inventing a denominator that makes performance look better.

### 4. Diagnose Before Acting
Suppose traffic rises 15%, transactions rise only 2%, ATV is stable and UPT falls. The weak sales result may not primarily be a traffic problem. The manager should investigate conversion and basket execution: queue abandonment, availability gaps, service coverage, product knowledge, merchandising, price competitiveness or customer mission.

A good manager asks:
1. What changed?
2. Which KPI explains most of the change?
3. Is the data reliable?
4. What operational evidence supports the explanation?
5. Which controllable action should we test?

### 5. Ethical Basket Building
Increasing ATV or UPT should not mean pressuring customers into unsuitable purchases. Strong retail selling begins with understanding the need. Appropriate actions include identifying genuinely complementary products, explaining useful alternatives, improving product availability, ensuring promotions are understood and making the shopping journey easier.

A manager should coach teams on relevance rather than aggressive scripts. Sustainable commercial performance comes from customer value and trust.

### 6. Use KPIs Together
ATV, UPT and conversion are diagnostic measures, not isolated targets. A strong daily review might combine sales versus target, traffic, conversion, transactions, ATV, UPT, gross margin indicators and availability exceptions. Together they create a clearer picture of the trading day.

## Worked Example
A store records R180,000 sales, 1,200 transactions, 2,040 units and 4,000 relevant customer visits.

- ATV = R180,000 ÷ 1,200 = **R150**
- UPT = 2,040 ÷ 1,200 = **1.7**
- Conversion = 1,200 ÷ 4,000 × 100 = **30%**

If conversion is below the store benchmark while ATV remains healthy, the manager should investigate why visitors are not becoming buyers before simply demanding larger baskets.

## Feedback
1. Average sales value per completed transaction.
2. Units sold divided by transactions.
3. An inconsistent or contaminated denominator produces a misleading rate.
4. Each explains a different part of sales performance and prevents simplistic conclusions.
5. Examples include relevant complementary recommendations, better availability or improved product knowledge.

## Key Takeaways
- Revenue should be broken into understandable performance drivers.
- ATV measures transaction value; UPT measures basket depth; conversion measures purchasing success from relevant opportunities.
- Data definitions must be consistent.
- Managers diagnose causes before choosing actions.
- Commercial coaching must protect customer value and trust.
