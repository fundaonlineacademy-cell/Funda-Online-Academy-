# Module 5 — Retail Finance & Commercial Performance
## Lesson 2: Gross Profit, Margin & Mark-up Fundamentals

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- distinguish selling price, cost of goods, gross profit, gross margin and mark-up;
- calculate simple gross profit, margin and mark-up examples;
- explain why margin percentage and mark-up percentage are not interchangeable;
- identify operational factors that erode achieved margin; and
- use basic margin information to support retail decisions.

## Why This Matters
Retail managers regularly make decisions about products, discounts, damages, markdowns, promotions and stock. Without understanding margin, a manager can celebrate high sales while unknowingly damaging profitability.

This lesson develops practical commercial literacy. It does not turn the manager into an accountant; it gives the manager enough understanding to interpret everyday retail decisions responsibly.

### Key Terms
**Cost price / merchandise cost:** amount used by the business as the cost of acquiring the product, according to its accounting method.  
**Selling price:** price at which the product is sold, subject to applicable pricing and tax treatment.  
**Gross profit:** selling revenue less relevant cost of goods sold.  
**Gross margin percentage:** gross profit expressed as a percentage of sales/revenue.  
**Mark-up percentage:** amount added above cost expressed as a percentage of cost, when calculated on a cost basis.  
**Markdown:** reduction from an established selling price.  
**Product mix:** combination of products/categories contributing to sales.

## 1. Gross Profit
For a simplified product example, assume a product costs the retailer R60 and sells for R100, ignoring tax/accounting complications for learning purposes.

**Gross profit = Selling price − Cost**

R100 − R60 = **R40 gross profit**

The R40 is not necessarily net profit. The retailer may still have operating expenses such as labour, occupancy, utilities, technology, security and other costs.

## 2. Gross Margin Percentage
Gross margin asks: what proportion of the selling revenue remains as gross profit after the relevant merchandise cost?

**Gross margin % = Gross profit ÷ Selling price × 100**

Using the example:
R40 ÷ R100 × 100 = **40% gross margin**

## 3. Mark-up Percentage
When mark-up is expressed on cost:

**Mark-up % = Gross profit ÷ Cost × 100**

R40 ÷ R60 × 100 = **66.67% mark-up on cost**

The same product therefore has a 40% gross margin and approximately 66.67% mark-up on cost. This is why the terms must not be treated as synonyms.

Organisations may use specific reporting conventions, so managers should follow the definitions in their approved financial reports and systems.

## 4. Why Margin Changes
A theoretical or planned margin can differ from the margin actually achieved. Common operational influences include:
- markdowns and discounts;
- promotional pricing;
- damaged or written-off stock;
- shrinkage;
- product-mix changes;
- pricing errors;
- supplier-cost changes;
- returns and adjustments, depending on accounting treatment.

Managers therefore protect margin through disciplined operations, not merely by refusing discounts.

## 5. Product Mix Matters
Imagine two departments each generate R100,000 sales. Department A operates at a 45% gross margin and Department B at 25%, using the organisation’s reporting basis.

Although their revenue is identical, their gross-profit contribution is different. If the store’s sales mix shifts heavily toward lower-margin products, total revenue can remain strong while gross-margin percentage declines.

A manager should therefore ask both:
- What did we sell?
- At what commercial quality did we sell it?

## 6. Discount Decisions
Assume an item sells for R200 with a merchandise cost of R120. Simplified gross profit is R80.

If the item is discounted to R160 while cost remains R120, gross profit falls to R40. The selling price dropped 20%, but gross profit in this simplified example dropped 50%.

This illustrates why seemingly small price reductions can have a large effect on gross profit. Managers should use authorised markdown and promotional rules rather than improvising discounts.

## 7. Margin Protection on the Shop Floor
Managers can protect commercial performance by:
- maintaining pricing accuracy;
- executing authorised promotions correctly;
- preventing avoidable damage and shrinkage;
- controlling unauthorised discounts and overrides;
- maintaining stock condition;
- investigating margin variances;
- improving sell-through before products require deep markdowns where this is within the store’s influence.

## Worked Example
A store exceeds its weekly sales target by 3%, but its gross-margin percentage is below plan. The manager finds that a large share of sales came from heavily promoted products, while several full-margin best sellers were out of stock.

The correct conclusion is not that promotions are automatically bad. The manager should examine promotion objectives, product mix, availability and the planned versus achieved margin. The action may include restoring full-margin availability and improving replenishment while continuing approved promotions correctly.

### Feedback
The central concept is the denominator: margin is generally related to sales while mark-up on cost is related to cost. Always follow the organisation’s reporting definitions.

## Key Takeaways
- Gross profit is revenue less relevant merchandise cost in a simplified retail view.
- Gross margin and mark-up are different percentages.
- Discounts can reduce gross profit disproportionately.
- Product mix influences store margin.
- Shrinkage, damage, markdowns and pricing errors can erode commercial performance.
- Retail managers protect margin through accurate, authorised and disciplined execution.
