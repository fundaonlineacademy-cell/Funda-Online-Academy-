# Module 5 — Retail Finance & Commercial Performance
## Lesson 3: Budgets, Targets & Performance Variance

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- explain the purpose of retail budgets and targets;
- calculate simple actual-versus-target variances;
- distinguish favourable-looking results from results that require deeper investigation;
- identify operational causes behind commercial variances; and
- create an action-focused performance review.

## Why This Matters
Budgets turn business plans into measurable expectations. Store managers use targets for sales, labour, expenses, stock, margin and other indicators. A budget is not merely a finance-department document. It gives management a reference point for deciding whether the store is performing as expected.

A variance is valuable only when it leads to understanding and appropriate action.

### Key Terms
**Budget:** approved financial or operational plan for a defined period.  
**Target:** expected level of a particular measure.  
**Actual:** recorded performance achieved.  
**Variance:** difference between actual and budget/target.  
**Favourable/unfavourable:** interpretation of a variance according to the nature of the measure and organisational convention.  
**Forecast:** updated expectation of future performance based on current information.  
**Controllable factor:** factor management can reasonably influence.

## 1. Calculating a Simple Variance
Suppose weekly sales target is R700,000 and actual sales are R665,000.

**Sales variance = Actual − Target**

R665,000 − R700,000 = **−R35,000**

The store is R35,000 below target.

Percentage variance can be calculated as:

**Variance % = (Actual − Target) ÷ Target × 100**

−R35,000 ÷ R700,000 × 100 = **−5%**

This tells the manager the size of the gap relative to plan.

## 2. Not Every Positive Variance Is Good
If sales exceed target, that may be favourable. But if controllable expenses exceed budget, the same positive numerical difference may be unfavourable.

Managers must understand what the measure represents rather than treating every plus sign as success.

Similarly, labour cost below budget might initially appear favourable, but if the store was severely understaffed, lost sales and damaged customer service, the commercial outcome may be poor overall.

## 3. Diagnose Before Acting
A manager should move through four questions:
1. **What happened?** Identify the variance.
2. **Why did it happen?** Examine drivers and evidence.
3. **What can we influence?** Separate controllable and external factors.
4. **What will we do?** Assign actions, owners and review dates.

For a sales shortfall, investigate traffic, conversion, ATV, availability, trading disruptions, promotions, competitor activity and staffing execution.

For an expense overspend, investigate whether it resulted from waste, emergency repair, inefficient scheduling, authorised investment or another cause.

## 4. Time Matters
A month-end result may hide daily patterns. A store could exceed target on weekends but consistently underperform Monday to Thursday. Category-level analysis may reveal that one department is carrying the store while another has a serious availability problem.

Useful variance analysis therefore looks at the appropriate level of detail without drowning the manager in unnecessary data.

## 5. Forecasting and Reforecasting
A forecast asks what is now likely to happen based on current evidence. If the store is halfway through a month, management can assess whether current trading suggests the original target remains achievable and what interventions may be required.

A forecast should not become an excuse to lower expectations casually. It is a decision-support tool that helps managers anticipate outcomes and act earlier.

## 6. Building an Action Plan
A strong action plan converts analysis into management work.

Example:
- **Issue:** Category sales 12% below target.
- **Evidence:** Traffic stable; conversion down; top three lines repeatedly unavailable on floor.
- **Action:** Increase replenishment checks at 10:00, 13:00 and 16:00; assign department owner.
- **Measure:** availability of identified lines and category conversion/sales trend.
- **Review:** end of each trading day for one week.

This is stronger than “improve sales.”

## Worked Example
A fictional store is 4% above sales target but 9% above its controllable overtime budget. The manager discovers repeated late replenishment because the morning work allocation is poor. The response should not simply be “cut overtime.” The root cause is workflow planning. The manager redesigns morning task allocation, monitors completion by midday and reviews whether overtime falls without damaging availability.

## Practical Activity — Variance Review
Create a table for a fictional store with five measures: sales, labour cost, controllable expense, gross margin and stock loss. Enter a target and actual figure for each. Calculate the variance and write:
- one possible cause;
- one piece of evidence you would inspect; and
- one management action.

### Feedback
Strong variance management connects numbers to operational causes. Managers should avoid treating a budget as a scorecard without diagnosis.

## Key Takeaways
- Budgets and targets provide planned reference points.
- Variances measure the gap between actual and planned performance.
- The meaning of a variance depends on the measure.
- Diagnosis should precede corrective action.
- Forecasting helps managers act before the period is finished.
- Strong action plans specify issue, evidence, action, owner, measure and review point.

### Visual Guidance
Use a variance-management loop: **Target → Actual → Variance → Diagnose → Action → Review → Forecast**.
