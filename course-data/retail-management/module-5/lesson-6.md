# Lesson 6 — Promotions, Markdown & Profitability Decisions

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- distinguish promotions from markdowns;
- explain how discounting can affect revenue, margin and stock movement;
- calculate simple discount and gross-profit effects;
- identify operational risks that can destroy promotional value;
- make evidence-based recommendations within delegated authority.

## Why This Matters
Promotions and markdowns can increase customer response and accelerate stock movement, but they are not automatically profitable. A busy promotional weekend can produce impressive sales while delivering disappointing gross profit if discount depth, product cost, stock availability or execution is poorly controlled. Retail managers need enough commercial understanding to ask whether an activity generated valuable sales, not merely more transactions.

## Key Terms
**Promotion:** a planned offer intended to influence customer behaviour for a defined period or purpose.

**Markdown:** a reduction from an existing selling price, often used to clear stock or respond to a commercial requirement.

**Discount depth:** the size of a price reduction relative to the original selling price.

**Gross profit:** sales revenue less cost of goods sold, before other operating expenses.

**Sell-through:** the proportion of available stock sold during a defined period, according to the retailer's approved calculation.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Promotions Have Different Objectives
A promotion may aim to generate traffic, increase basket value, introduce a product, support a seasonal event, defend market position, reward loyalty or clear inventory. The manager must understand the intended objective before evaluating success.

A promotion designed primarily to clear ageing stock should not be judged only by margin percentage. Equally, a high-sales campaign that leaves substantial stock unsold or causes unacceptable margin erosion may not have achieved its commercial purpose.

### 2. Discount Mathematics
If an item normally sells for R500 and is reduced by 20%, the discount is R100 and the promotional selling price is R400.

If the item cost is R300:
- original gross profit per unit = R500 − R300 = R200;
- promotional gross profit per unit = R400 − R300 = R100.

The selling price fell 20%, but gross profit per unit fell 50%. This demonstrates why managers should not assume that a percentage discount causes the same percentage reduction in profit.

### 3. Volume Needed to Offset Lower Unit Profit
Using the example above, 100 units at normal price would generate R20,000 gross profit. At R100 gross profit per promotional unit, 200 units would be required to generate the same gross profit amount, assuming the same unit cost and ignoring other effects.

This does not mean the promotion is wrong. The business may intentionally accept lower unit margin to clear inventory or achieve another objective. The important point is that the trade-off should be understood.

### 4. Execution Determines Results
A commercially sound promotion can fail operationally because of:
- insufficient stock;
- incorrect tickets or POS prices;
- late promotional setup;
- poor display visibility;
- staff who do not understand the offer;
- unavailable complementary items;
- excessive queues;
- weak replenishment;
- expired promotional material remaining on display.

Retail managers therefore connect commercial planning with operational readiness.

### 5. Markdown Discipline
Markdowns should follow approved authority and system processes. Managers should avoid unofficial price reductions, inconsistent treatment of customers, unsupported overrides or attempts to hide stock problems through arbitrary discounting.

Before recommending a markdown, consider stock age, quantity, condition, demand, seasonality, space pressure, likely recovery value and alternative actions such as transfer or approved return-to-supplier processes.

### 6. Review the Outcome
A post-promotion review can compare:
- sales versus plan;
- units and transactions;
- margin or gross-profit effect;
- stock availability and sell-through;
- customer response;
- operational incidents;
- residual stock;
- lessons for future events.

The goal is organisational learning, not merely declaring the event successful because sales were high.

## Worked Example
A product costs R120 and normally sells for R200. A promotion reduces it to R170.

Normal gross profit per unit = R80.
Promotional gross profit per unit = R50.

If 300 units are sold at promotional price, gross profit is R15,000. To generate R15,000 at the normal R80 unit profit, the store would need 187.5 units, practically 188 units. The promotion generated more unit movement, but management should still ask whether clearing stock or generating traffic justified the lower unit return.

## Practical Activity
A store has 240 units of a seasonal product costing R75 each and selling at R150. Management is considering a 25% markdown.
1. Calculate the new selling price.
2. Calculate gross profit per unit before and after markdown.
3. Identify three commercial or operational factors that should be reviewed before implementation.
4. Explain how you would evaluate the markdown after the event.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why are promotions not automatically profitable?
2. What is a markdown?
3. Why can gross profit fall faster than selling price when a discount is applied?
4. Name two execution failures that can damage a promotion.
5. Why should markdown authority be controlled?

## Feedback
1. Discounts, costs, volume, stock and execution determine the actual commercial result.
2. A reduction from an existing selling price, commonly for a defined commercial reason.
3. Cost remains in the transaction while the selling-price reduction comes directly from the available margin.
4. Examples include stock-outs, wrong tickets, POS errors, weak replenishment or poor staff briefing.
5. To protect margin, consistency, auditability and authorised decision-making.

## Key Takeaways
- Promotional sales must be evaluated against their intended objective.
- Discount percentage and profit impact are not the same thing.
- Operational execution can determine whether a promotion succeeds.
- Markdowns require authority, evidence and control.
- Post-event reviews turn trading activity into management learning.
