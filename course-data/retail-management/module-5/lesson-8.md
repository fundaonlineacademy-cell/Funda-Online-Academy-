# Lesson 8 — Reading a Retail Scorecard & Management Action Planning

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
- interpret a balanced retail performance scorecard;
- distinguish results from drivers and diagnostic measures;
- identify material variances and relationships between KPIs;
- prioritise management actions using evidence;
- build a concise commercial action plan with ownership and follow-up.

## Why This Matters
Retail managers are surrounded by numbers, but effective management is not the ability to repeat a dashboard. It is the ability to turn information into decisions. A scorecard should help the manager see whether the store is delivering customer, commercial, people and operational outcomes and where intervention is most valuable.

## Key Terms
**Scorecard:** a structured set of measures used to review performance against expectations.

**KPI:** key performance indicator.

**Variance:** difference between an actual result and a target, budget, benchmark or comparison period.

**Leading indicator:** a measure that may provide early information about future results.

**Lagging indicator:** a measure that largely records an outcome already produced.

**Action plan:** a documented set of actions with ownership, timing and expected result.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. A Balanced Scorecard
A retail scorecard may include measures from several categories:

**Commercial:** sales, gross profit, margin, ATV, UPT, conversion, markdown.

**Customer:** complaints, service measures, returns patterns or satisfaction indicators.

**Stock and operations:** availability, stock variance, shrinkage indicators, receiving exceptions, compliance completion.

**People:** labour productivity, attendance, training completion, staffing coverage or other approved workforce measures.

A manager should avoid allowing one attractive number to hide deterioration elsewhere.

### 2. Results and Drivers
Sales is an important outcome, but its drivers may include traffic, conversion, availability, pricing, service capacity and basket performance. Gross profit may be affected by sales mix, product cost, markdown and discount activity. Shrinkage can be influenced by receiving, stock handling, POS exceptions, damage control and other processes.

Understanding these relationships allows the manager to move from "sales are down" to a more useful diagnosis.

### 3. Prioritise Material Variances
Not every red number deserves equal management attention. Consider:
- size of the variance;
- financial or customer impact;
- risk and urgency;
- trend or recurrence;
- degree of management control;
- relationship to other KPIs.

A small repeated stock-control variance may deserve more attention than a one-day sales miss caused by severe weather if the stock issue signals an escalating control weakness.

### 4. Look for Relationships
Suppose a scorecard shows:
- sales 8% below target;
- traffic 2% below target;
- conversion significantly below benchmark;
- ATV on target;
- top-selling products frequently unavailable;
- customer complaints about queues rising.

The data suggests the manager should investigate conversion barriers, availability and service capacity rather than simply instructing employees to "sell harder."

### 5. Build a Management Action Plan
A useful action should state:
- **issue:** what evidence shows;
- **cause hypothesis:** what is likely driving it;
- **action:** what will be done;
- **owner:** who is responsible;
- **deadline:** by when;
- **measure:** how improvement will be verified;
- **follow-up:** when the result will be reviewed.

Example: "Availability on the top 20 lines averaged 88% against a 97% internal target. Department supervisor to complete twice-daily gap scans and resolve backroom replenishment exceptions for seven trading days. Store manager reviews availability at 12:00 and close; result assessed after one week."

### 6. Avoid False Precision
Data quality matters. A manager should know which reports are system-generated, which measures depend on manual capture and whether definitions changed. An impressive dashboard can still produce poor decisions if the underlying information is incomplete or inconsistent.

### 7. The Management Rhythm
Scorecards are most useful when integrated into a rhythm:
**review → diagnose → prioritise → act → verify → standardise or adjust.**

This cycle connects Module 5's financial measures with the broader management-control approach taught throughout the course.

## Integrated Store Scenario
Ubuntu Retail Hub, a fictional store, reports the following weekly results:
- sales: R920,000 against R1,000,000 target;
- gross margin: 30.5% against 31% target;
- traffic: 1% below plan;
- conversion: 7 percentage points below internal benchmark;
- ATV: 4% above target;
- UPT: on target;
- top-50-line availability: 91% against 97% internal target;
- queue complaints: doubled from the previous week.

The strongest diagnosis is not simply "customers are spending too little" because ATV is above target. Conversion, availability and queue experience require priority investigation. The manager should validate the data, observe trading conditions and build actions around those drivers.

## Practical Activity
Using the Ubuntu Retail Hub scenario:
1. Rank the three most important issues.
2. Explain the evidence for each priority.
3. Create one SMART-style management action for each.
4. Identify the KPI or observation that will verify improvement.

## Feedback
1. Sales alone does not explain customer, margin, stock, people or operational drivers and risks.
2. The difference between actual performance and a target, budget, benchmark or comparison.
3. Relationships help identify likely causes rather than treating symptoms.
4. Issue, cause hypothesis, action, owner, deadline, measure and follow-up are examples.
5. Review, diagnose, prioritise, act, verify, then standardise or adjust.

## Key Takeaways
- Managers convert scorecards into decisions, not merely reports.
- Balanced performance requires commercial, customer, stock, operational and people evidence.
- Materiality, trend, risk and controllability help set priorities.
- KPI relationships improve diagnosis.
- Every important action needs ownership and verification.
