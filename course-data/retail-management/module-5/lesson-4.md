# Module 5 — Retail Finance & Commercial Performance
## Lesson 4: Labour Productivity & Controllable Costs

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- explain labour productivity in practical retail terms;
- identify common controllable store costs;
- distinguish cost control from indiscriminate cost cutting;
- connect staffing decisions to customer demand and operational workload; and
- recommend actions that improve productivity without undermining service, safety or control.

## Why This Matters
Labour is both a cost and a capability. Too little staffing at the wrong time can cause queues, poor replenishment, weak service, safety problems and lost sales. Too much staffing without productive work can weaken commercial performance.

The manager’s job is therefore not simply to minimise hours. It is to use available labour intelligently while controlling avoidable expense.

### Key Terms
**Labour productivity:** relationship between labour input and useful output or commercial result.  
**Labour cost:** employee-related cost recognised by the organisation.  
**Controllable cost:** expense that store management can reasonably influence within approved authority.  
**Scheduling:** allocation of employee working time to meet operational requirements.  
**Workload:** volume and complexity of tasks requiring completion.  
**Productivity measure:** indicator comparing output/results with resources used.

## 1. Productivity Is Not “Work Faster”
Productivity improves when the store produces better results from its resources through good planning, capability and process design.

Examples include:
- aligning staffing with customer demand;
- completing replenishment before key trading periods;
- reducing duplicate work;
- training employees to perform tasks correctly first time;
- improving handovers;
- positioning employees where they create the most value.

Simply pressuring employees to move faster can create mistakes, injuries, poor service and turnover.

## 2. Useful Labour Measures
Retailers use different productivity measures. Examples may include:
- sales per labour hour;
- transactions per labour hour;
- units processed per hour in a relevant task;
- labour cost as a percentage of sales;
- planned versus actual hours.

These measures must be interpreted carefully. A high sales-per-hour figure may look strong but could reflect understaffing if queues and lost sales are also rising.

## 3. Demand-Based Scheduling
A manager should understand when work occurs.

Customer demand may peak at lunch, after work, weekends, month-end or promotional periods. Operational workload may peak before opening, after deliveries or during stock counts.

A strong schedule places the right capabilities at the right times. It also respects applicable employment requirements, approved contracts, breaks and organisational rules.

## 4. Controllable Costs
Depending on store structure and delegated authority, managers may influence costs such as:
- overtime;
- consumables;
- packaging;
- utilities through responsible usage;
- avoidable damage and waste;
- certain maintenance costs through preventative care;
- printing and stationery;
- cleaning consumables;
- emergency costs caused by poor planning.

Managers should use their organisation’s budget categories rather than inventing definitions.

## 5. Cost Control versus Cost Cutting
Cost control asks: “Are we using resources efficiently and for the right purpose?”

Indiscriminate cost cutting asks: “What can we remove?”

The second approach can be dangerous. Cutting cleaning resources can damage hygiene. Reducing security controls can increase shrinkage. Understaffing tills can lose customers. Delaying necessary maintenance can create larger costs later.

Commercial leadership considers total impact.

## 6. Root Causes of Overtime
Repeated overtime may result from:
- poor rosters;
- late deliveries;
- absenteeism;
- inefficient task allocation;
- insufficient skills coverage;
- unrealistic workload assumptions;
- repeated rework;
- poor closing routines.

A manager who merely bans overtime without fixing the cause may shift the problem elsewhere.

## Worked Example
A store is consistently over its labour budget on Fridays. Initial pressure is to remove two employees from the evening shift. Analysis shows that a large delivery is being processed late because receiving begins too slowly in the morning. This delays replenishment and forces employees to stay after closing.

The manager changes receiving allocation, prepares the backroom before delivery and establishes completion checkpoints. Overtime falls while evening customer coverage remains intact.

## Practical Activity — Productivity Improvement
Choose one store process such as receiving, replenishment, checkout or closing. Identify:
1. current labour inputs;
2. common delays or rework;
3. one useful productivity measure;
4. two process improvements; and
5. one risk to monitor so that productivity improvement does not damage quality or safety.

### Feedback
Productivity is a management-system issue. Better planning, capability, workflow and allocation often create more sustainable savings than pressure alone.

## Key Takeaways
- Labour is both a cost and a source of service and execution capacity.
- Productivity compares useful results with resources used.
- Scheduling should match workload and demand.
- Managers should control avoidable cost without weakening critical operations.
- Overtime and overspend should be diagnosed at root-cause level.
- Sustainable productivity protects service, quality, safety and commercial performance together.
