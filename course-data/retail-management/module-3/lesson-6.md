# Professional Certificate in Retail Management & Team Leadership
## Module 3: Customer Experience & Service Leadership
### Lesson 6: Omnichannel & Digital Customer Experience

**Estimated study time:** 45–55 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
1. Explain omnichannel retail from a store-management perspective.
2. Identify common customer journeys across physical and digital channels.
3. Manage collection, return and information hand-offs consistently.
4. Recognise operational failures that damage digital customer experience.
5. Coordinate store teams around channel-neutral customer service.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Customers increasingly experience a retailer as one business even when they move between a website, app, contact centre, delivery service and physical store. A store manager therefore needs to understand how local execution affects the wider customer journey.

## Key Terms
- **Omnichannel** — coordinated customer experience across connected shopping channels.
- **Click-and-collect** — online purchase or reservation collected from a physical location.
- **Fulfilment** — process of preparing and delivering or handing over an order.
- **Channel hand-off** — transfer of a customer request or order between service channels.
- **Order accuracy** — degree to which the fulfilled order matches the customer's confirmed order.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Customers see one brand
A customer who orders online and collects in-store usually does not care which internal department caused a problem. They experience the retailer as a single organisation. Store teams should therefore avoid statements such as “that is an online problem” when the approved process requires store support.

### 2. Typical omnichannel journeys
Journeys include researching online then buying in-store, ordering online for collection, checking local stock digitally, returning eligible online purchases through a store, or receiving support in one channel after purchasing in another.

### 3. Collection quality matters
A strong collection process confirms order identity, locates the parcel efficiently, follows verification requirements, handles exceptions correctly and completes required system steps. Long searches for supposedly ready orders damage trust.

### 4. Inventory accuracy supports digital promises
If systems display stock that is not physically available, customers may place orders the store cannot fulfil. Accurate receiving, replenishment, stock counts and exception processing therefore contribute directly to digital customer experience.

### 5. Returns require channel clarity
Employees need approved guidance on which purchases can be handled locally, required proof, system steps and escalation routes. Managers should not create unofficial rules at branch level that conflict with consumer rights or organisational policy.

### 6. Communication during exceptions
When an order is delayed, missing or incomplete, customers need clear ownership. Explain what is known, what will be checked, who owns the next step and when the customer should expect an update where the process provides a timeframe.

### 7. Protect customer information
Digital service often involves names, contact details, order numbers and payment-related information. Employees should access only what is required for their work and follow approved privacy and security procedures.

### 8. Measure the journey
Useful measures may include collection wait time, order readiness, fulfilment accuracy, cancellations caused by unavailable stock, complaints and repeat exceptions. Measures should lead to corrective action rather than becoming reports nobody uses.

## Worked Example
A customer receives a notification that an order is ready for collection. At the store, the parcel cannot be found. Instead of sending the customer away, the supervisor verifies the order, checks the approved storage and system locations, identifies whether fulfilment was completed incorrectly, communicates ownership and escalates through the correct channel. The manager later investigates why the system indicated readiness before physical handover readiness was confirmed.

## Key Takeaways
- Physical stores are part of the digital customer journey.
- Inventory and fulfilment accuracy protect customer trust.
- Channel hand-offs require ownership and clear procedures.
- Exceptions should be investigated, not repeatedly normalised.
- Customer information must be handled securely.
