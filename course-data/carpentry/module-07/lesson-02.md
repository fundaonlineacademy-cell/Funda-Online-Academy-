# Module 7 — Construction Methods & Practical Application
## Lesson 2 — Planning Work Sequence, Materials & Cut Lists

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. convert confirmed drawing information into a practical cutting list;
2. determine material requirements without confusing finished and stock dimensions;
3. arrange carpentry operations in a logical work sequence;
4. identify dependencies, inspection points and preparation requirements; and
5. plan for efficient material use without overriding specification or quality.

### Why This Lesson Matters
A carpenter can lose time and material through poor sequencing even when individual operations are performed correctly. Cutting before confirming quantities, finishing inaccessible surfaces too late, or installing hardware before checking alignment can create rework. SAQA carpentry standards emphasise accurate cutting lists from working drawings, correct setting-out, cost-effective methods and minimised wastage. Planning turns technical information into controlled production.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
**Cutting list** — A controlled list of required components, normally showing quantity, material and component dimensions derived from the authorised job information.

**Finished size** — The required final dimension of a component after the relevant preparation and machining operations.

**Stock size** — The dimensions of available material before it is converted into finished components.

**Work sequence** — The planned order in which preparation, machining, assembly, installation, inspection and finishing operations will occur.

**Dependency** — A task or condition that must be completed before another task can proceed correctly.

**Inspection point** — A planned stage where dimensions, quality, alignment or another requirement is checked before further work makes correction difficult.

**Yield** — The useful components obtained from a quantity of stock material.

**Material optimisation** — Planning component layout and processing to reduce avoidable waste while maintaining all specification and quality requirements.

### Main Lesson Content
Planning begins only after the brief and drawing have been interpreted and unclear information resolved. A cutting list is not a guess about what material might be needed. It is a translation of confirmed component requirements into a controlled production record. For each component, record a clear identifier, quantity, material or board type, required dimensions and any relevant orientation or quality note.

Keep finished dimensions separate from stock or preparation dimensions. If a component requires machining before reaching its final size, the amount of material needed for that process must follow the approved workshop method, material condition and job specification. Do not invent a universal machining allowance. Different materials, machinery, defects and processes can require different preparation decisions.

Check quantities carefully. A frame may contain repeated members that appear identical but have different joint details. A cabinet may have two sides, one top, one bottom, shelves and a back. Count from the drawing systematically and identify each part rather than relying on memory. Where handed components exist, such as left and right parts, label them clearly.

Once the component list is confirmed, compare it with available stock. Consider stock lengths or sheet sizes, grain direction, defects, visible faces, board orientation and any specification restrictions. Optimisation means arranging cuts intelligently, but it never means using a defective or non-compliant area merely to improve yield. A small reduction in waste is not a saving if the resulting component must later be remade.

A work sequence should follow technical dependencies. A common general sequence is: verify information; inspect/select material; prepare a cutting plan; mark and cut components; machine joints or features; dry fit; inspect dimensions and squareness; correct permitted defects; apply adhesive/fixings and assemble; allow required curing or setting where applicable; prepare surfaces; fit hardware; install; perform final quality checks; protect and hand over. Actual projects may require a different order, so the drawing, product instructions and workplace process govern.

Inspection points prevent errors from travelling downstream. Check a component after critical cutting or machining rather than discovering at final assembly that several parts are wrong. Dry assembly is especially useful before irreversible joining. Check diagonals, square, level or alignment at stages where correction remains practical.

Plan tools and workholding as part of the sequence. If a large sheet must be cut, identify how it will be supported before moving it to the cutting area. If an assembled unit will be too large to pass through a doorway, the installation sequence must be reconsidered before final assembly. If access to a fixing becomes blocked after another component is installed, reverse the sequence or use the specified alternative method.

Material planning also supports cost control. Record required quantities, compare them with usable stock and preserve suitable offcuts according to workplace practice. Avoid repeated small cuts from new full-length stock when an approved, correctly identified offcut is suitable. However, stock rotation and offcut reuse must never override material identity, grade, condition, grain requirement or dimensions.

Finally, communicate the plan. On team jobs, people need to understand component identifiers, sequence and hold points. A short pre-job briefing can prevent two workers from independently cutting the same parts or assembling components before an inspection is complete.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
A shelving unit requires two 1 800 mm sides, four 760 mm shelves and one back panel. The carpenter first confirms finished dimensions and materials. The cutting list identifies each component and quantity. Before cutting, available boards are inspected for defects and visible-face requirements. The work sequence places drilling and housing preparation before assembly, followed by dry fit and a squareness check. This prevents the common mistake of permanently assembling the carcass before confirming shelf positions and overall square.

### Practical Activity
Prepare a cutting list and work sequence for a small bench, shelf or cabinet. Include at least six component lines, material identity, quantity, finished dimensions, orientation notes where relevant, planned stock source, and a minimum of four inspection points. Add a short note explaining one waste-reduction decision that does not compromise specification.

### Knowledge Check
1. Why should finished size and stock size not automatically be treated as identical?
2. What is the purpose of an inspection point?
3. Give two factors other than component size that can affect material layout.
4. When is using an offcut inappropriate even if it is large enough?

### Feedback / Explanation
1. Material may require preparation or machining before reaching finished dimensions; the approved process determines stock needs.
2. It catches errors while correction is still practical and before further work increases the cost of the defect.
3. Examples include grain direction, defects, visible faces, material grade and sheet orientation.
4. When its identity, grade, condition, orientation or other specified requirement is unsuitable or uncertain.

### Key Takeaways
- Build cutting lists from confirmed job information.
- Count and identify every component systematically.
- Keep finished dimensions distinct from stock/preparation requirements.
- Sequence work according to dependencies.
- Insert inspection points before irreversible operations.
- Plan tools, support, handling and installation access early.
- Optimise material without lowering quality or compliance.
- Communicate the plan on team jobs.
