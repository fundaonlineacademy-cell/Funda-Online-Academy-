# Module 7 — Construction Methods & Practical Application
## Lesson 6 — Installing Components & Safe Fixing to Substrates

**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. explain why substrate identification is essential before fixing;
2. distinguish positioning, temporary support and permanent fixing stages;
3. select an installation approach from approved project or manufacturer information;
4. check level, plumb, alignment and stability during installation; and
5. recognise conditions that require escalation rather than improvised fixing.

### Why This Lesson Matters
A well-made component can fail if it is badly installed. Cupboards, shelves, panels, frames and fittings transfer forces into walls, floors or other supporting construction. Fixings must therefore suit the substrate, load and specified installation system. Guessing a wall type or choosing a fastener because it “looks strong” can create serious defects.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
**Substrate** — The material or construction into which a component is fixed, such as concrete, masonry, timber framing or an approved board system.

**Anchor** — A fixing component designed to transfer load into a substrate according to its specified installation method.

**Pilot hole** — A prepared hole used to guide a screw or fixing and reduce certain installation risks where the method requires it.

**Embedment** — The depth or extent to which a fixing engages the supporting material, as defined by the applicable fixing specification.

**Plumb** — Vertically aligned according to the required reference.

**Level** — Horizontally aligned according to the required reference.

**Packing / shim** — A controlled spacer used where the approved installation method permits adjustment between a component and an uneven supporting surface.

**Load path** — The route through which forces pass from a component through its fixings into supporting construction.

### Main Lesson Content
Installation begins with verification, not drilling. Confirm the component, position, drawing, finished level and fixing specification. Then identify the substrate. A painted wall surface does not tell you what lies behind it. The supporting material may be masonry, concrete, timber framing, light-gauge framing, a hollow partition system or a combination. If the substrate cannot be confirmed and the fixing is safety-critical, stop and obtain the required information.

Fixings are systems. Their performance depends on the correct anchor or screw, substrate, hole diameter, hole depth, edge distance, spacing, installation torque or tightening method and load direction where applicable. These values vary between products and applications. Therefore, a course should not teach one universal fixing size or spacing. Follow the project design, manufacturer data and competent instruction.

Before drilling, check for concealed services using the workplace’s approved process and available information. Electrical cables, water pipes and other services can be hidden in walls or floors. Never assume a convenient drilling point is safe simply because nothing is visible on the surface.

Set out the component position using the drawing and site references. Mark fixing points accurately. Where a component is large or heavy, plan temporary support and handling before lifting it into position. A worker should not hold a heavy cabinet in an unstable posture while another person tries to locate fixings. Use suitable team lifting, supports or mechanical assistance according to the risk assessment and workplace procedure.

Temporary positioning is not the same as permanent fixing. Place the component, check level/plumb and confirm clearances before final tightening. For a long cabinet run or panel system, establish a consistent datum so individual pieces do not drift out of alignment. Packing may be used where the specified method allows it, but packing should provide stable support and must not conceal a fundamentally unsuitable substrate or excessive site error.

Drilling should be controlled. Use the correct tool and bit for the substrate and fixing system. Maintain stable body position and protect nearby surfaces. Dust controls and PPE should follow the applicable task risk assessment and workplace procedure. Clean or prepare holes as required by the fixing manufacturer. Do not improvise an anchor into a damaged, oversized or incorrectly drilled hole.

During tightening, avoid pulling the component out of square or crushing the material. Tighten progressively where multiple fixings are used, monitoring alignment. Recheck level, plumb and position after fixing because tightening can move a component.

Installation quality includes the surrounding work. Fixings should not damage visible finishes, split timber edges or obstruct hardware. Gaps, sealants, trims or cover plates must follow the job specification. Clean the work area and remove drilling debris before handover.

Escalate when the substrate is unknown, damaged or different from the design assumption; when services conflict with the fixing position; when specified anchors are unavailable; when the component cannot be safely handled; or when the required fixing would be too close to an edge or opening according to the approved system. Professional judgement includes knowing when not to proceed.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
A wall cabinet drawing specifies fixing to masonry, but site inspection suggests a lightweight partition at the intended location. The carpenter does not substitute a hollow-wall anchor without approval. The discrepancy is recorded and escalated because the load path and fixing design have changed. The correct solution may require locating framing, adding support or using another engineered fixing method.

### Practical Activity
Complete an Installation Planning Record for a shelf or wall cabinet. Include substrate identification evidence, concealed-service check, component weight/handling consideration, datum, fixing specification source, drilling method, temporary support, alignment checks, final stability check and escalation criteria. Do not invent anchor sizes; cite the project or manufacturer requirement supplied in the exercise.

### Knowledge Check
1. Why is the wall surface alone insufficient for selecting a fixing?
2. Name four factors that can affect anchor performance.
3. Why should alignment be rechecked after tightening?
4. Give two situations where installation should stop for clarification.

### Feedback / Explanation
1. The visible finish may conceal a different supporting substrate.
2. Examples include substrate, fixing type, hole dimensions, embedment, edge distance, spacing, tightening method and load direction.
3. Tightening can pull or distort the component away from its intended position.
4. Examples: unknown substrate, concealed-service conflict, damaged substrate, unavailable specified fixing or unsafe handling conditions.

### Key Takeaways
- Identify the supporting substrate before selecting or installing fixings.
- Treat anchors and fasteners as specified systems.
- Check for concealed services before drilling.
- Plan safe support and handling for large components.
- Position and align before permanent tightening.
- Recheck level, plumb and stability after fixing.
- Do not improvise into damaged or unknown substrates.
- Escalation is part of competent installation practice.
