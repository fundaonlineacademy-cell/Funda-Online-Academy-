# Module 7 — Construction Methods & Practical Application
## Lesson 4 — Shelving & Basic Storage Construction

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. interpret a basic shelving or storage-unit drawing;
2. identify key construction decisions affecting shelves, carcasses and supports;
3. set out components and shelf positions accurately;
4. assemble a basic storage unit while maintaining square and alignment; and
5. recognise when loading, anchorage or installation requirements must be confirmed rather than assumed.

### Why This Lesson Matters
Shelving and cupboards are common carpentry tasks in homes, offices, shops and workshops. Their apparent simplicity can hide important decisions about material, span, fixing, edge support, squareness, hardware and wall attachment. SAQA carpentry standards specifically include cupboards and shelving among finishing components and require installation according to working drawings, with components aligned and level.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
**Carcass** — The main box-like body of a cabinet or storage unit, typically made from sides, top, bottom and sometimes a back.

**Shelf span** — The unsupported distance between shelf supports.

**Housing / dado** — A groove or recess that receives another component, often used to locate a shelf or divider.

**Adjustable shelf** — A shelf supported by hardware that permits its height to be changed between approved positions.

**Fixed shelf** — A shelf permanently joined or fixed in a defined position and often contributing to carcass rigidity.

**Back panel** — A rear component that closes a carcass and, depending on the design, may contribute to alignment or rigidity.

**Load** — The weight or force that a shelf, fixing or storage unit is expected to support.

**Anchorage** — The method by which a unit or component is securely fixed to the supporting structure.

### Main Lesson Content
Begin by identifying what the storage unit must do. A light display shelf, kitchen wall unit, workshop storage cabinet and bookcase may require different materials, spans, hardware and anchorage. Do not transfer a construction detail from one application to another without checking the job specification.

Read the drawing and identify the sides, top, bottom, shelves, dividers, back, doors or other components. Confirm overall dimensions and internal clearances. Determine which shelves are fixed and which are adjustable. Identify edge treatments, hardware and visible surfaces before machining begins. If the drawing specifies grain or decorative face direction, include that in the cutting plan.

Shelf span matters because a longer unsupported shelf is generally more susceptible to deflection under load. However, there is no single universal maximum span that applies to every material and load. Thickness, material properties, load magnitude, support conditions and intended service all affect suitability. Use the approved design, manufacturer information or competent specification rather than inventing a span rule.

Fixed shelves may be housed, dowelled, screwed, joined with suitable fittings or constructed using another specified system. Adjustable shelves require correctly aligned support holes, tracks or fittings. Accuracy is important: mismatched support positions can cause a shelf to rock. Use a jig, template or controlled marking system where appropriate rather than independently guessing each hole position.

A carcass must be assembled square. After components are prepared, perform a dry fit where practical. Check that shelves or dividers enter their joints without excessive force, that edges are flush where required and that overall dimensions are correct. During final assembly, monitor diagonals and square before adhesive cures or the back is permanently fixed.

The back panel must be installed according to the design. Some backs fit into grooves; others are fixed to the rear edges or within rebates. Do not assume a thin back is merely decorative. In some designs it helps maintain the carcass geometry. Conversely, do not rely on a back as structural bracing unless the design intends it to perform that function.

Wall-mounted or tall storage introduces anchorage considerations. The carpenter must identify the supporting substrate and specified fixing method. Masonry, timber framing, lightweight partition systems and other substrates require different approved fixings and installation approaches. Do not select a fixing only by appearance or because it was used on a previous job. Confirm substrate, load and applicable fixing instructions. Where uncertainty affects safety, escalate before installation.

Installation requires checking level, plumb, alignment and clearances. Floors and walls may not be perfectly straight. Follow the approved method for packing, scribing or adjustment rather than forcing the cabinet out of square to match an irregular surface. Adjacent doors and drawers depend on a correctly aligned carcass.

Finish with a quality inspection. Check exposed edges, surface damage, fasteners, shelf seating, door or hardware operation where present, cleanliness, stability and specified alignment. Remove temporary markings that should not remain visible and protect finished surfaces during handover.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
A 900 mm-wide wall unit has two fixed shelves and a back. During dry assembly the diagonals differ by 7 mm. If the back is fixed immediately, the distorted carcass may be locked out of square. The correct process is to adjust the assembly, recheck width, height, diagonals and joint seating, then secure the back according to the design. Wall fixing is planned separately after confirming the substrate and approved anchors.

### Practical Activity
Prepare an assembly and installation checklist for a basic four-shelf storage unit. Include component verification, shelf positions, dry fit, diagonal check, back installation, visible-edge inspection, substrate confirmation, anchorage plan, level/plumb check and final stability check. Identify at least two conditions that would require clarification before installation.

### Knowledge Check
1. Why is there no universal shelf-span rule for all shelving?
2. What problem can occur if adjustable shelf-support holes are not aligned?
3. Why should a carcass be checked before the back is permanently fixed?
4. What must be confirmed before choosing wall anchors?

### Feedback / Explanation
1. Deflection and suitability depend on material, thickness, load, support conditions and design.
2. The shelf can rock, tilt or fail to seat correctly.
3. A permanently fixed back may lock an out-of-square carcass into the wrong geometry.
4. The substrate, expected load, specified fixing system and relevant manufacturer/project requirements.

### Key Takeaways
- Match storage construction to the intended use.
- Read component, material and hardware requirements before cutting.
- Treat shelf span as a design issue, not a guess.
- Align adjustable shelf hardware accurately.
- Dry fit and check carcass geometry.
- Install backs according to the actual design function.
- Confirm substrate and anchorage before wall installation.
- Finish with alignment, stability and quality checks.
