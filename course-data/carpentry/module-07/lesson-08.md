# Module 7 — Construction Methods & Practical Application
## Lesson 8 — Practical Build Workflow: Set-out to Handover

**Estimated study time:** 50 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. organise a small carpentry job from set-out through handover;
2. integrate safety, material, sequence and quality controls into one workflow;
3. identify hold points before irreversible operations;
4. perform staged and final inspections against the job information; and
5. prepare a clear handover record for completed work.

### Why This Lesson Matters
Workplace competence is not a collection of isolated tool skills. A carpenter must connect information, material selection, preparation, machining, assembly, installation, inspection and communication. DBE practical woodworking assessment similarly evaluates planning, scale drawings, marking and cutting, accurate assembly, ironmongery and staged work. This lesson brings the module together as a complete job workflow.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
**Set-out** — Establishing component positions, dimensions and reference marks from confirmed job information.

**Hold point** — A planned stage where work pauses for inspection or approval before an irreversible or costly operation proceeds.

**In-process inspection** — A quality check performed while work is being manufactured or installed.

**Snag** — A defect or incomplete item identified for correction before final acceptance.

**Handover** — The controlled transfer of completed work to the client, supervisor or next responsible party after required checks.

**Traceability** — The ability to connect work and decisions to drawings, specifications, materials, inspections or approvals.

**Protection** — Measures that preserve completed work from damage before and during handover.

**Completion record** — Documentation showing what was completed, checked, corrected or referred at the end of a job.

### Main Lesson Content
A practical carpentry workflow starts with information control. Confirm the job brief, drawing revision, specifications, quantity, location and completion expectations. Resolve unclear information. Record critical dimensions and requirements before ordering or cutting material. This prevents the project from beginning with an assumption that later becomes expensive.

Next plan the work. Prepare the cutting list, material requirement, tool list, work sequence and handling needs. Identify safety controls and any site-specific risks. Determine which operations depend on others. Mark hold points before irreversible work such as final machining, glue-up, finishing, drilling into a substrate or permanently fixing a component.

Receive and inspect materials. Confirm identity, dimensions, grade or product designation where specified, condition and quantity. Segregate damaged or non-compliant material. Store accepted material correctly until use. Material control at this stage prevents unsuitable stock from being built into the job.

Set out components using confirmed reference faces, datums and dimensions. Label parts. Check repeated positions using templates or jigs where appropriate. Before cutting, conduct a “last reversible check”: correct component, correct orientation, correct dimension, correct waste side and correct tool/process. This short pause prevents many avoidable errors.

Machine and prepare components in the planned sequence. Inspect critical dimensions after important operations rather than waiting until final assembly. If a component becomes non-compliant, stop and decide whether authorised rework is possible. Do not adjust every remaining component to match one incorrect part unless the design is formally changed.

Dry assemble where practical. Check joint fit, overall dimensions, square, diagonals, alignment and hardware positions. This is a major hold point because permanent assembly can make correction difficult. Once the dry fit is accepted, complete final joining according to the specified adhesive, fixing or joint process.

Prepare surfaces and apply finishes in the correct stage for the product. Some parts may require finishing before installation because access becomes limited later; other systems require finishing after assembly. Follow the approved product and project sequence. Protect finished surfaces from clamps, tools, dust and transport damage.

At installation, confirm the site condition again. Establish datum, level and plumb references. Identify substrate and concealed-service risks before drilling. Support components safely. Fix according to the approved system and recheck alignment after tightening. Coordinate interfaces with adjoining components or other trades.

Perform a final quality inspection against the original job information, not merely against memory. Check dimensions, fit, level/plumb/square where relevant, joint quality, fixings, hardware operation, finish, cleanliness, stability and completeness. Record snags and correct them through authorised methods. Repeat affected checks after correction.

Handover should be professional. Remove waste and temporary protection as appropriate, clean the work, demonstrate operation if required and communicate any care instructions or outstanding authorised items. Record completion, inspections and client/supervisor acceptance according to workplace procedure. Good handover protects both the customer and the carpenter because it establishes what condition was delivered.

Finally, review the job. Note material waste, rework, delays and successful methods. Continuous improvement means using evidence from one project to improve the next without replacing the formal specification with personal habit.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
A carpenter builds and installs a small wall cabinet. The workflow is: confirm drawing → cutting list → inspect board → cut and edge components → machine shelf positions → dry assemble → check diagonals → final assemble → fit hardware → inspect wall/substrate → install to datum → test door operation → correct a minor authorised hinge adjustment → clean → final inspection → handover record. Because inspection occurs throughout, a small error is caught before it becomes a completed defect.

### Practical Activity
Create a one-page Build-to-Handover Control Sheet for a small cabinet, shelf or framed project. Include at least 15 sequential steps, five hold points, material inspection, safety checks, dimensional checks, dry fit, installation verification, snag correction and handover evidence. Mark each step as **Plan**, **Do**, **Check** or **Handover**.

### Knowledge Check
1. What is the purpose of a hold point?
2. Why should final inspection use the original job information?
3. What should happen when a component becomes non-compliant during machining?
4. Why is job review useful after handover?

### Feedback / Explanation
1. It prevents work proceeding beyond a stage where an error would become difficult, unsafe or costly to correct.
2. Memory can omit requirements; the approved brief/drawing is the acceptance reference.
3. Stop, assess and use an authorised rework/replacement decision rather than spreading the error into other components.
4. It identifies causes of waste, rework and delay and captures effective methods for continuous improvement.

### Key Takeaways
- Control information before material and tools.
- Plan sequence, safety and hold points.
- Inspect material before processing.
- Check before irreversible operations.
- Use in-process inspection throughout the build.
- Dry fit before final assembly where practical.
- Reconfirm site conditions before installation.
- Inspect against the original requirements and document handover.
