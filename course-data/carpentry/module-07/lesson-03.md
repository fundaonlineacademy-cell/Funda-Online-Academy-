# Module 7 — Construction Methods & Practical Application
## Lesson 3 — Building Frames & Simple Timber Structures

**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. explain the function of common members in a simple timber frame;
2. set out and assemble a small non-structural practice frame from a confirmed drawing;
3. check dimensions, diagonals, squareness and alignment during assembly;
4. identify safe workholding and assembly practices; and
5. recognise when structural or regulated work requires an approved design and competent supervision.

### Why This Lesson Matters
Frames appear in furniture, doors, partitions, shopfitting, cabinetry and building carpentry. The basic discipline is the same: components must be correctly identified, accurately set out, joined in the intended orientation and checked before the assembly is fixed permanently. Frame errors multiply quickly. A small error in one member can create a large alignment problem at installation.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
**Frame** — An assembly of members connected to form a supporting or enclosing structure.

**Stile** — A principal vertical member in a framed item such as a door, panel or cabinet frame.

**Rail** — A horizontal member connecting vertical frame members.

**Member** — An individual component forming part of a frame or structure.

**Diagonal check** — Comparing corner-to-corner measurements to help assess whether a rectangular assembly is square, provided the intended geometry and dimensions are correct.

**Bracing** — Components or temporary supports used to resist unwanted movement or distortion.

**Datum** — A confirmed reference line, face or point from which measurements and positions are established.

**Racking** — Distortion of a rectangular frame toward a parallelogram shape.

### Main Lesson Content
A simple timber frame is a useful way to apply measurement, jointing, workholding and quality-control skills. Before construction, identify the purpose of the frame. A furniture face frame, door frame, wall frame and structural roof component may all contain timber members, but they are not interchangeable systems. Structural building work must follow approved drawings, applicable requirements and competent supervision. This lesson focuses on transferable construction principles and controlled practice assemblies rather than authorising structural design.

Begin with the drawing. Identify overall height and width, member sizes, joint positions, openings and any fixing or bracing details. Establish a datum or reference edge so that repeated dimensions are taken consistently. Mark components with identifiers that match the cutting list. If several members are similar, identification prevents them being rotated or exchanged accidentally.

Material should be inspected before set-out. Excessive bow, twist, damage or unsuitable defects can make accurate assembly difficult or render a component unsuitable for its specified purpose. Do not expect screws, nails or clamps to correct severely unsuitable stock. The material decision should already have been controlled through earlier selection and preparation.

Set out joints from consistent reference faces and edges. Transfer matching positions together where appropriate so that rails or intermediate members align. Before machining, verify that waste sides and component orientation are correctly marked. A correctly sized joint cut on the wrong face is still wrong.

Dry assembly is a critical control. Bring the members together without irreversible final fixing where the jointing method permits. Check overall dimensions, joint closure and member orientation. For a rectangular frame, measure both diagonals. Equal diagonals are a useful indication of squareness when opposite sides and intended geometry are correct, but diagonal equality does not replace checking the specified dimensions. Also use an appropriate square and straightedge as required.

Racking occurs when corners move out of square. During assembly, clamps should apply controlled pressure in positions that close joints without pulling the frame into distortion. Check square before adhesive sets or permanent fixings prevent adjustment. Temporary bracing may be used where the approved process requires it to maintain geometry during handling or installation.

Workholding must be stable. Assemble on a reasonably flat, suitable surface. Support long members so their weight does not lever joints apart. Keep hands away from pinch points when clamps are tightened. Select clamps of appropriate capacity and inspect them before use. If powered fastening tools are used, follow their operating procedures and keep other people clear of the firing or fastener path.

Frame quality is more than “it stays together.” Inspect joint fit, flushness, surface damage, fastener position, dimensions, square, twist and overall alignment. A frame can have tight joints and still be unusable because its opening is the wrong size. Conversely, an assembly can match overall size but contain weak, damaged or poorly fitted joints.

Installation introduces another reference system: the site opening, floor, wall or adjoining component. Do not assume the surrounding construction is perfectly square or level. Confirm site dimensions and follow the approved installation method. Where the job is governed by building regulations, structural design or manufacturer instructions, those requirements control member sizes, fixing methods, spacing and anchorage.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
A practice rectangular frame is specified at 900 mm × 600 mm overall. After dry assembly, the width and height are correct but the diagonals differ. This indicates the assembly has racked. The learner loosens the temporary clamping, adjusts the corners while monitoring the diagonals and square, then rechecks overall dimensions before final joining. Simply tightening one clamp harder without measurement could worsen the distortion.

### Practical Activity
Construct or simulate a small rectangular practice frame from an approved workshop drawing. Record: component IDs, overall dimensions before assembly, diagonal measurements during dry fit, square check, final dimensions, joint observations and any correction made. If physical construction is unavailable, complete the exercise using a scaled training drawing and a facilitator-provided set of measured assembly results.

### Knowledge Check
1. What is racking?
2. Why should overall dimensions still be checked when diagonals are equal?
3. Why are component identifiers useful?
4. Why should severe timber distortion not simply be forced straight with fixings?

### Feedback / Explanation
1. Racking is distortion that moves a rectangular frame away from its intended square geometry.
2. Equal diagonals alone do not prove the frame has the specified width and height.
3. They maintain traceability between the drawing, cutting list and actual members and reduce orientation errors.
4. Forced material can create stress, poor alignment, weak fixing conditions or later movement; suitability should be assessed first.

### Key Takeaways
- Identify frame purpose before applying a construction method.
- Work from confirmed dimensions and reference faces.
- Mark component identity and orientation.
- Dry fit before irreversible assembly where practicable.
- Check dimensions, square and diagonals during assembly.
- Use stable workholding and controlled clamp pressure.
- Inspect joint quality as well as overall geometry.
- Structural or regulated work must follow approved requirements.
