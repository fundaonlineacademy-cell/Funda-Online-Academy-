# Module 7 — Construction Methods & Practical Application
## Lesson 1 — Reading a Simple Job Brief & Working Drawing

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. identify the essential requirements in a simple carpentry job brief;
2. interpret basic dimensions, views, notes and material requirements on a working drawing;
3. distinguish stated requirements from assumptions that require clarification;
4. convert job information into a practical preparation checklist; and
5. explain why specifications must be confirmed before cutting or installation begins.

### Why This Lesson Matters
Professional carpentry begins before the first cut. A carpenter who misunderstands a drawing can manufacture an accurate component that is nevertheless wrong for the job. Errors at interpretation stage cause wasted timber, rework, delays, unsafe improvisation and dissatisfied clients. South African vocational carpentry standards repeatedly require working drawings to be interpreted accurately and cutting or installation work to follow specified dimensions. This lesson develops that discipline.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
**Job brief** — A description of what the client, supervisor or project requires, including purpose, location, dimensions, finish, timing and other constraints.

**Working drawing** — A technical drawing used to communicate the information needed to manufacture, assemble or install a component.

**Dimension** — A numerical measurement showing a required length, width, height, thickness, spacing or position.

**Specification** — A stated technical requirement that may cover material, grade, dimensions, finish, hardware, quality or installation method.

**Scale** — The relationship between a size represented on a drawing and the corresponding real size. Written dimensions take priority over attempting to measure a printed drawing unless the document specifically authorises scaled measurement.

**View** — A representation of an object from a particular direction, such as front, top or side.

**Tolerance** — The permitted variation from a specified dimension or condition where a tolerance has been defined.

**Clarification** — Obtaining authorised confirmation when job information is missing, contradictory or unclear.

### Main Lesson Content
A job brief tells the carpenter what outcome is required. It may be a formal work order, a drawing package, a supervisor’s instruction or a combination of written and verbal information. Before starting work, identify the product or component, its intended use, where it will be installed, the required quantity, material, dimensions, finish, hardware, quality expectations and deadline. Never allow a familiar-looking job to replace the actual brief. Two shelves may look similar while differing in span, thickness, support method, exposure or loading.

A working drawing converts the job requirement into technical information. Start by reading the title, drawing description, revision information where provided, notes and units. Then identify the views. A front view communicates different information from a top or side view. Details may enlarge a joint, fixing point or hardware position. Read dimensions from their dimension lines and notes rather than estimating proportions from the picture. A printed or displayed drawing may have been resized, so measuring the paper or screen is not a safe substitute for a stated dimension.

Dimensions must be interpreted as a system. A component’s overall size may be shown separately from internal openings, offsets or member sizes. Before cutting, check whether the set of dimensions makes sense together. If an overall width conflicts with the sum of component dimensions, stop and clarify the conflict. Do not choose whichever number is easier. The same rule applies where the brief specifies one material but a drawing note specifies another, or where hardware positions appear inconsistent.

Specifications can include more than dimensions. A drawing or job sheet may identify timber species or board type, thickness, grade, edge treatment, joint, fastener, adhesive, finish, hardware and installation position. These requirements influence later decisions. Substituting a different board because it is available can change strength, thickness, machining, fixing and finishing behaviour. A professional carpenter therefore separates what is specified from what is merely preferred or assumed.

Use a structured interpretation sequence. First, identify the job and latest authorised information. Second, identify the component and its function. Third, read all written notes. Fourth, identify views and dimensions. Fifth, record materials, hardware and finish requirements. Sixth, identify quality or tolerance requirements. Seventh, check the information for omissions or conflicts. Eighth, obtain clarification where required. Only then should the information be transferred into material quantities, a cutting list and work sequence.

Revision control matters. If more than one drawing is available, the carpenter should not automatically use the copy that is easiest to reach. Follow the workplace’s document-control process and confirm which revision is authorised. Manufacturing from an obsolete drawing can produce perfectly made but unusable components.

A useful preparation checklist contains: job identity; drawing or brief reference; component quantity; finished dimensions; material and grade where specified; hardware; finish; critical alignment or fit requirements; site conditions; unresolved questions; and approval or clarification received. This record improves communication and provides evidence of careful preparation.

Interpretation is also a safety issue. A carpenter who begins machining before understanding component size, material or installation method may improvise unsafe holding, lifting or fixing methods. Planning allows the correct tools, workholding, handling assistance and installation sequence to be selected before work begins.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
A brief requests a wall-mounted storage unit. The drawing gives an overall width of 900 mm, specifies an 18 mm board product and shows two internal divisions. A note calls for concealed fixing, but no fixing type is identified and the wall substrate is unknown. The correct response is not to select a convenient wall plug. The carpenter records the known dimensions and material, identifies the missing substrate/fixing information and obtains clarification before installation planning. This prevents an unsupported assumption from becoming a safety or quality defect.

### Practical Activity
Choose a simple furniture or shelving drawing supplied by your facilitator, workplace or learning resource. Create a one-page Job Interpretation Record containing: job purpose, quantity, overall dimensions, component dimensions, specified material, hardware, finish, installation requirements, three critical checks and any information requiring clarification. Mark each item as **Specified**, **Derived from confirmed information**, or **Clarification required**.

### Knowledge Check
1. Why should a carpenter not measure a resized screen drawing to determine an unstated dimension?
2. What should you do if two authorised-looking dimensions contradict one another?
3. Name four items, other than dimensions, that a specification may control.
4. Why is drawing revision information important?

### Feedback / Explanation
1. Display or print scaling may have changed, so graphical measurement may not represent the intended real dimension.
2. Stop the affected work and obtain authorised clarification rather than guessing.
3. Examples include material, grade, hardware, joint, finish, fastener or installation method.
4. An obsolete revision can contain superseded requirements and cause incorrect manufacture or installation.

### Key Takeaways
- Read the complete brief before starting work.
- Use stated dimensions and specifications, not visual guesswork.
- Check dimensions and notes as a connected system.
- Distinguish confirmed requirements from assumptions.
- Resolve conflicts before cutting, assembly or installation.
- Confirm the authorised drawing revision.
- Convert the interpreted information into a preparation record.
- Good interpretation reduces waste, rework, delays and unsafe improvisation.

### Visual Learning Guidance
**Recommended visual 1:** A simple cabinet working drawing showing front, top and side views. **Caption:** “Follow written dimensions and notes across all views; do not estimate from picture proportions.”

**Recommended visual 2:** Job-information flow diagram: Brief → Drawing → Requirements → Clarification → Cutting list/work plan. **Caption:** “Technical work starts only after the information has been checked.”
