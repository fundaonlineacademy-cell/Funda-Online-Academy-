## Lesson 1 — Measurement Systems & Reading Dimensions

### Q1
A cutting list states that a rail must be 0.85 m long. What is the same dimension in millimetres?

A. 85 mm  
B. 850 mm  
C. 8 500 mm  
D. 0.085 mm

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** One metre equals 1 000 millimetres, so 0.85 m equals 850 mm. The learner should confirm the unit before transferring a dimension to a workpiece.

**Traceability:** Lesson 1 — metric units and conversion.

### Q2
Why is a common datum useful when several positions must be marked on one component?

A. It makes every pencil line thicker.  
B. It allows each new position to be referenced from one reliable starting point.  
C. It removes the need to check dimensions.  
D. It automatically compensates for a damaged measuring tool.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Referencing several dimensions from one reliable datum reduces the risk of cumulative error caused by repeatedly measuring from previous marks.

**Traceability:** Lesson 1 — datum and cumulative error control.

### Q3
A drawing shows a dimension of 72 cm. Which value should be used if the workshop cutting list records dimensions in millimetres?

A. 7.2 mm  
B. 72 mm  
C. 720 mm  
D. 7 200 mm

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** One centimetre equals 10 millimetres; therefore 72 cm equals 720 mm.

**Traceability:** Lesson 1 — reading and converting metric dimensions.

### Q4
A learner finds two conflicting dimensions for the same component on the approved job information. What is the most professional response?

A. Choose the larger dimension without asking.  
B. Average the two dimensions.  
C. Stop and obtain clarification before cutting the component.  
D. Cut both versions and decide later.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A carpenter should not invent or guess a required dimension. A discrepancy must be clarified against the approved drawing, specification or responsible supervisor before irreversible work begins.

**Traceability:** Lesson 1 — dimensions as controlled job information.

## Lesson 2 — Tape Rules, Steel Rules & Measuring Accuracy

### Q5
Which tool is generally more suitable for measuring the overall length of a long timber board?

A. Tape rule  
B. Marking knife  
C. Sliding bevel  
D. Chisel

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A tape rule is designed for longer measurements. Tool choice must still consider the required accuracy, condition of the tool and the workpiece.

**Traceability:** Lesson 2 — measuring-tool selection.

### Q6
A tape blade is twisted and running diagonally between the two points being measured. What is the main concern?

A. The tape becomes easier to read.  
B. The reading may not represent the true straight-line distance.  
C. The timber automatically becomes square.  
D. The hook becomes a marking gauge.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A tape should be aligned with the intended measurement path. Twist, sag or diagonal placement can introduce measurement error.

**Traceability:** Lesson 2 — alignment and measuring accuracy.

### Q7
Before using a steel rule for precision marking, the learner notices that it is visibly bent. What should the learner do?

A. Continue because metal rules cannot affect accuracy.  
B. Bend the workpiece to match the rule.  
C. Use a suitable serviceable measuring tool and report/remove the damaged rule according to workplace procedure.  
D. Estimate the measurement by eye.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Measuring equipment should be in serviceable condition. A visibly damaged rule can undermine straightness and measurement reliability.

**Traceability:** Lesson 2 — inspection and care of measuring tools.

### Q8
Why should a carpenter repeat or independently verify a critical measurement before cutting?

A. To make the component heavier.  
B. To detect reading, reference or transfer errors before material is removed.  
C. To eliminate the need for a cutting list.  
D. To change the specified dimension.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verification is a low-cost control that can identify mistakes before an irreversible cutting or machining operation.

**Traceability:** Lesson 2 — verification and repeatability.

## Lesson 3 — Squares, Straightedges & Checking Geometry

### Q9
What is the primary purpose of a try square in basic carpentry layout?

A. Measuring moisture content  
B. Marking and checking a 90-degree relationship  
C. Cutting curved profiles  
D. Applying finish

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A try square is used to establish or check square relationships, commonly between a reference edge and a line or adjoining surface.

**Traceability:** Lesson 3 — try-square use.

### Q10
A rectangular frame has equal opposite sides but its two diagonal measurements are different. What does this most strongly indicate?

A. The frame may be racked and not square.  
B. The timber is automatically level.  
C. The frame has no dimensions.  
D. The finish is too thick.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** For a true rectangle, equal diagonals are an important geometric check. Different diagonals indicate that the frame may be racked, assuming the measurement points are consistent.

**Traceability:** Lesson 3 — checking rectangular geometry.

### Q11
What is a straightedge mainly used to check?

A. Whether a surface or edge follows a straight reference.  
B. Whether timber is electrically live.  
C. Whether paint is dry.  
D. Whether a screw is hardened.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A suitable straightedge provides a reference for identifying bowing, gaps or deviation from straightness.

**Traceability:** Lesson 3 — straightedge inspection.

### Q12
A learner repeatedly marks square lines from an edge that is itself damaged and irregular. What is the best corrective action?

A. Make the pencil darker.  
B. Establish or select a reliable reference edge before continuing layout.  
C. Increase the number of random measurements.  
D. Ignore the edge because the square will correct it automatically.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A square can only transfer geometry reliably when it is seated against an appropriate reference. A poor reference can reproduce error across the component.

**Traceability:** Lesson 3 — reference quality and geometry.

## Lesson 4 — Marking Gauges, Knives, Pencils & Reference Faces

### Q13
What is the main advantage of using a marking gauge for a line that must remain parallel to a prepared reference edge?

A. Its fence follows the reference while the point marks a consistent offset.  
B. It measures electrical resistance.  
C. It automatically cuts the joint to depth.  
D. It eliminates the need for a reference surface.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The gauge fence bears against the reference edge or face while the marking point maintains the set distance.

**Traceability:** Lesson 4 — marking-gauge principles.

### Q14
Why are reference-face and reference-edge marks useful on prepared timber?

A. They identify the surfaces from which important layout measurements and checks should be taken.  
B. They show where paint must always be applied.  
C. They indicate that all other faces are unusable.  
D. They replace the job drawing.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Consistent references help the carpenter transfer dimensions and geometry from known prepared surfaces instead of changing the basis of measurement unpredictably.

**Traceability:** Lesson 4 — reference faces and edges.

### Q15
When is a fine marking knife line especially useful?

A. When a precise layout boundary is required for controlled fitting or cutting.  
B. When measuring a room length.  
C. When checking a spirit level vial.  
D. When sweeping the workshop.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A marking knife can create a fine, precise reference line. It must be used safely and only where its use is appropriate for the material and task.

**Traceability:** Lesson 4 — pencils versus marking knives.

### Q16
After marking a component, why should the waste side be identified where appropriate?

A. To help prevent removing material from the required finished side of the line.  
B. To make the timber longer.  
C. To remove the need to understand kerf.  
D. To guarantee that every saw cuts identically.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Clear waste-side identification supports correct positioning of the cut relative to the finished dimension and reduces avoidable mistakes.

**Traceability:** Lesson 4 — marking and waste-side control.

## Lesson 5 — Angles, Bevels & Accurate Layout

### Q17
What is a sliding bevel primarily used for in this module?

A. Transferring or setting an angle.  
B. Measuring timber moisture.  
C. Sanding a surface.  
D. Tightening screws.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A sliding bevel is useful for transferring an existing angle or setting a required angle when used with an appropriate reference or angle-setting method.

**Traceability:** Lesson 5 — sliding-bevel use.

### Q18
Two components are intended to be mirror images. What is an important layout control?

A. Mark both components without identifying their orientation.  
B. Confirm left/right orientation and reference faces before transferring the angle.  
C. Use different units on each component.  
D. Remove all reference marks before checking.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Mirrored parts are a common source of layout mistakes. Clear orientation, reference faces and component identification should be confirmed before marking or cutting.

**Traceability:** Lesson 5 — mirrored components.

### Q19
A bevel setting moves after it has been transferred to the first component. What should happen before the next component is marked?

A. Continue because the change will be too small to matter.  
B. Re-establish and verify the required angle setting.  
C. Change the drawing to match the bevel.  
D. Estimate the remaining angles by eye.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A movable setting must be checked if there is any reason to believe it has shifted. Repeated layout should use a verified reference.

**Traceability:** Lesson 5 — repeated-angle accuracy.

### Q20
Why should the long point, short point and waste side of an angled component be understood before cutting?

A. Because angled cuts can otherwise be made in the wrong orientation or on the wrong side of the finished dimension.  
B. Because they determine timber species.  
C. Because they automatically set the saw guard.  
D. Because every angle must be 45 degrees.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Angled layout changes component orientation and finished endpoints. Clear interpretation prevents reversed or shortened components.

**Traceability:** Lesson 5 — angle layout and orientation.

## Lesson 6 — Levels, Plumb & Alignment

### Q21
In carpentry installation work, what does **plumb** describe?

A. A vertical condition relative to the chosen gravity reference.  
B. A rough-sawn surface.  
C. A timber species.  
D. A horizontal cutting list.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Plumb refers to vertical alignment. It is different from level, which refers to a horizontal condition.

**Traceability:** Lesson 6 — level and plumb terminology.

### Q22
A spirit level gives a suspicious reading. What is the best response before altering the workpiece?

A. Immediately plane the workpiece until the reading changes.  
B. Verify the tool, contact surfaces and reference, and repeat the check.  
C. Ignore the reading and use eyesight only.  
D. Assume the floor is always the correct datum.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Before modifying work, the carpenter should verify the measuring/checking method, tool condition, seating and reference. Incorrect diagnosis can create unnecessary rework.

**Traceability:** Lesson 6 — reliable level checks.

### Q23
Why is it risky to align each new installed component only to the immediately previous component?

A. Small errors can accumulate along the installation.  
B. Components become too clean.  
C. The timber changes species.  
D. It prevents the use of fasteners.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Progressive work should be checked against reliable overall references so that small local errors do not accumulate into a significant final misalignment.

**Traceability:** Lesson 6 — cumulative alignment error.

### Q24
A shelf is level but projects farther forward on one side than the other. Which statement is most accurate?

A. Level alone proves every aspect of alignment is correct.  
B. Different geometric conditions must be checked separately; level does not automatically prove front-to-back or side alignment.  
C. The shelf must be plumb because it is level.  
D. No further inspection is required.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Level, plumb, square and alignment describe different relationships. A professional inspection checks the conditions relevant to the job specification.

**Traceability:** Lesson 6 — distinguishing geometric checks.

## Lesson 7 — Tolerances, Allowances & Fit

### Q25
What is a tolerance?

A. An uncontrolled mistake that must always be accepted.  
B. The permitted variation from a specified requirement where such variation is defined.  
C. The width of every saw blade.  
D. A decorative pencil mark.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A tolerance defines an acceptable range or permitted variation. Learners must use the tolerance stated by the drawing, specification, manufacturer or authorised workplace requirement rather than inventing one.

**Traceability:** Lesson 7 — tolerance concept.

### Q26
A specification gives a target dimension but no numerical tolerance. What should the learner do if acceptance depends on knowing the permitted variation?

A. Invent a tolerance that seems reasonable.  
B. Use the tolerance from an unrelated project.  
C. Obtain clarification from the approved specification or responsible authorised person.  
D. Assume plus or minus 10 mm for all carpentry.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Tolerances are job-specific. A learner should not create an unsupported acceptance limit.

**Traceability:** Lesson 7 — specification control.

### Q27
Which statement best describes an allowance?

A. A deliberate dimensional provision made for a required purpose such as fit, movement, machining or assembly, when specified or technically required.  
B. Any accidental measuring error.  
C. A reason to ignore the drawing.  
D. A fixed value that is identical for every timber product.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** An allowance is intentional. Its value and purpose depend on the design, material, process and specification; learners should not assume a universal allowance.

**Traceability:** Lesson 7 — allowances and fit.

### Q28
Several components are each slightly outside their intended dimensions, and the assembled unit no longer fits its opening. What principle does this illustrate?

A. Cumulative dimensional error can affect final fit.  
B. Individual component dimensions never affect assembly.  
C. A finish will always correct dimensional errors.  
D. Measuring is unnecessary after assembly.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Small dimensional deviations can combine across several components and create a significant final fit or alignment problem.

**Traceability:** Lesson 7 — cumulative error and fit.

## Lesson 8 — Setting Out a Simple Carpentry Project & Cut List

### Q29
What is the best starting point for preparing a cutting list for a simple project?

A. Guess the component sizes from a photograph.  
B. Interpret the approved drawing or job specification and identify the required components.  
C. Cut available timber first and design around it later.  
D. Copy a cutting list from an unrelated project.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A cutting list should be derived from controlled job information. SAQA vocational carpentry material similarly links cutting lists and setting out to working drawings and specified dimensions.

**Traceability:** Lesson 8 — drawing-to-cutting-list workflow.

### Q30
Which cutting-list entry is most useful for controlling production?

A. “Some timber — make it fit.”  
B. Component identification, quantity, required dimensions and relevant material/specification information.  
C. Only the learner's name.  
D. Only the colour of the workshop wall.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A useful cutting list clearly identifies what must be produced and in what quantity and size, together with the material or other job information needed to avoid ambiguity.

**Traceability:** Lesson 8 — cutting-list content.

### Q31
Before cutting a marked component, which verification sequence is strongest?

A. Check component identity, reference, dimension, orientation, mark and waste side against the approved job information.  
B. Check only whether the pencil is sharp.  
C. Cut first and measure afterward.  
D. Ask another learner to guess whether it looks correct.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A structured pre-cut check catches errors in component identity, dimension, reference and orientation before material is irreversibly removed.

**Traceability:** Lesson 8 — final pre-cut verification.

### Q32
A cutting plan can produce all required components in two different ways. One method meets the specification while using stock more efficiently and producing less unnecessary waste. Which method is preferable?

A. The wasteful method because more offcuts show more work was done.  
B. The efficient method, provided quality, safety and specification requirements are maintained.  
C. Either method must be rejected because cutting plans are not part of carpentry.  
D. The method that ignores component orientation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Efficient material planning should minimise unnecessary waste without compromising dimensions, quality, safety or other specification requirements. This is consistent with vocational carpentry criteria that emphasise cost-effective methods and reduced material wastage.

**Traceability:** Lesson 8 — material optimisation and waste control.
