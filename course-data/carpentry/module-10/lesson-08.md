# Carpentry — Module 10: Applied Carpentry Practice & Workplace Readiness
## Lesson 8 — Employability, Portfolio, Entrepreneurship & Continuous Development
**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
Learners should be able to present evidence of carpentry capability; identify workplace behaviours employers value; outline basic self-employment controls; distinguish capability from unsupported claims; and create a continuous-development plan.

### Why This Lesson Matters
Technical learning creates opportunity only when a learner can demonstrate competence, work professionally and continue improving. Some graduates will seek employment; others may undertake small jobs or build businesses. Both routes require evidence, reliability, safety, quality and honest communication.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Portfolio** — organised evidence demonstrating learning, work processes and completed outcomes.
- **Competence** — demonstrated ability to apply knowledge and skill to required standards in context.
- **Employability** — combination of technical and workplace behaviours that support obtaining and sustaining work.
- **Entrepreneurship** — creating and managing economic activity by providing products or services while controlling risk and resources.
- **Quotation** — commercial offer describing proposed work and price under stated terms.
- **Invoice** — document requesting/payment-recording for supplied goods/services under the applicable business process.
- **Continuous development** — ongoing improvement of knowledge, skill and professional capability.
- **Referral** — directing work to an appropriately competent/authorised person when it exceeds one's scope or capability.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Build evidence, not just claims
A strong portfolio shows process as well as finished photographs. Include selected drawings, cutting lists, material planning, safety/risk planning, first-off checks, dry assembly, quality records, before/after images and completed products. Remove confidential customer information unless permission exists.

### 2. Present work accurately
Describe what you personally did, which tools/materials were used, the challenge and how quality was verified. Do not claim to be licensed, registered, trade-tested, accredited or qualified beyond the credentials actually held. Funda Online Academy course completion should be represented exactly as issued, without inflating it into a different statutory or occupational status.

### 3. Develop employability behaviours
Employers value punctuality, attendance, safe practice, care of tools, housekeeping, ability to read instructions, measurement accuracy, willingness to learn, communication and responsibility for mistakes. Reliability is a practical skill: arriving prepared and completing controlled work consistently reduces supervision burden.

### 4. Prepare for job applications and interviews
Create a concise CV that identifies training, relevant skills, practical projects and work experience truthfully. Prepare portfolio examples that demonstrate measurement, tool use, assembly, finishing and problem solving. In an interview, explain safe methods and decision-making rather than only naming tools.

### 5. Understand the basics of small-job entrepreneurship
Before accepting work, define scope, inspect conditions, calculate material quantities, estimate labour, obtain supplier prices, identify risks and document the commercial offer appropriately. Keep records of purchases, customer approvals, variations, payments and completed work. Applicable tax, consumer, municipal, registration and other legal obligations depend on the business and work context; obtain appropriate professional/official guidance rather than assuming one rule fits every carpenter.

### 6. Price responsibly
A price should reflect the business's actual costing method, including materials, consumables, labour and other relevant costs. Extremely low pricing can create pressure to substitute materials or rush safety/quality. Never solve a costing error by silently reducing the agreed specification.

### 7. Know the boundary of your competence
Do not accept work that requires capability, authorisation or specialist knowledge you do not possess. Electrical, structural, fire-safety or other regulated interfaces may require appropriately competent persons. Referral is professional risk management, not failure.

### 8. Protect reputation through quality
Repeat work and referrals depend on consistent delivery. Confirm requirements, communicate changes, protect property, clean the site and correct legitimate defects professionally. Keep photographs and records with appropriate permission.

### 9. Create a development plan
Review gaps in tool competence, drawing interpretation, installation, finishing, costing, digital skills and communication. Set specific goals such as supervised practice on a particular joint, learning a drawing package, improving costing accuracy or obtaining recognised further training. Record evidence of progress.

### 10. Treat learning as continuous
Materials, products, tools, standards and regulations change. Read manufacturer instructions, workplace procedures and current official requirements. Ask experienced practitioners for feedback and reflect after every project: What worked? What failed? What caused waste? What would I change next time?

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A learner applies for a junior carpentry role. Instead of writing “expert carpenter,” the CV lists the completed course and practical capabilities accurately. The portfolio contains a cabinet project with drawing, cutting plan, first-off measurement, dry-fit photo, finishing record and final inspection. In the interview the learner explains a measurement error found during first-off inspection and how it was prevented from affecting the batch. This demonstrates judgement and accountability.

## Practical Activity
Create a **Work Readiness Pack** containing: one-page CV outline; five portfolio evidence items; a sample project description; a basic quotation/costing checklist; three boundaries of work you would refer/escalate; and a six-month skills-development plan.

## Knowledge Check
1. A portfolio should show only perfect final photographs. **Answer: False.** Process evidence demonstrates capability.
2. Should a learner claim a credential they have not earned? **Answer: No.**
3. Why define scope before pricing? **Answer:** Price, materials, time and risk depend on what work is actually included.
4. Referring work outside your competence is: A) unprofessional B) professional risk control C) always illegal D) unnecessary. **Answer: B.**

### Key Takeaways
Build evidence; represent credentials accurately; practise reliable workplace behaviour; cost and document small jobs; know your competence boundaries; protect reputation; and keep developing after the course.
