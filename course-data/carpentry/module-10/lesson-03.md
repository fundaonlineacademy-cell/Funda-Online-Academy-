# Carpentry — Module 10: Applied Carpentry Practice & Workplace Readiness
## Lesson 3 — Basic Costing, Material Quantities & Waste Estimation
**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
Learners should be able to derive material quantities from approved job information; distinguish net quantity from purchasing quantity; prepare a basic job cost; account for controlled waste and consumables; and explain why price, estimate and quotation are not automatically the same thing.

### Why This Lesson Matters
A job that is technically excellent but badly costed can lose money. A job that is deliberately under-ordered can cause delays, while excessive ordering ties up cash and creates waste. Carpentry costing must connect drawings and cutting lists to actual purchasing, production and business decisions.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Quantity take-off** — calculating required material quantities from approved job information.
- **Net quantity** — material theoretically required by the finished components before purchasing/waste considerations.
- **Purchasing quantity** — actual amount to buy after considering stock sizes, cutting layout and approved allowance.
- **Yield** — useful output obtained from a quantity of material.
- **Waste** — material consumed without becoming acceptable finished work.
- **Consumable** — item used during work, such as abrasive, adhesive or certain fasteners.
- **Labour cost** — cost associated with time spent performing work.
- **Overhead** — business cost not conveniently assigned to one physical component, such as some administration or premises costs.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Start with drawings and cutting lists
Quantities must come from controlled dimensions and component counts. South African woodworking curriculum material explicitly links cutting lists with measuring, reading drawings, quantities, consumables and calculation of material cost. The cutting list should identify components, dimensions, material and quantity clearly enough to plan procurement.

### 2. Net requirement is not always purchasing requirement
Suppose a project requires four components of a given size. Their combined area or length is the net requirement, but material is purchased in available sheet or stock sizes. The carpenter must develop a cutting plan, consider actual saw kerf/process requirements, defects, grain/face orientation and reusable offcuts. Do not apply an invented universal “10% waste” rule. Appropriate allowance depends on material, stock format, layout, quality and job conditions.

### 3. Count all required categories
A basic material estimate can include solid timber/boards, hardware, fasteners, adhesives, abrasives, finishing products, edging and other specified consumables. Installation may require approved anchors or brackets. Quantities should be checked against packaging sizes and supplier units.

### 4. Calculate material cost
For each item: purchasing quantity × unit price = line cost. Add the lines for total direct material cost. Confirm whether quoted supplier prices include applicable taxes, delivery or cutting services before comparing alternatives. Record the date/source because prices can change.

### 5. Add labour and appropriate job costs
Estimate the labour time required for preparation, manufacture, finishing, transport/installation and handover as applicable. A business may also allocate overhead, transport, equipment, subcontracting or contingency according to its costing method. Learners should understand the categories rather than assume one universal pricing formula.

### 6. Cost waste caused by poor process
Waste is not only offcut. Wrong measurement, damaged surfaces, uncontrolled adhesive, failed finish, rework and unnecessary travel all have cost. Efficient planning reduces these losses without compromising specification, safety or quality.

### 7. Estimate versus quotation
An estimate is an informed approximation based on available information. A quotation may be a formal commercial offer subject to its stated scope, terms and validity. The exact legal/commercial treatment depends on context, so the carpenter or business should use approved organisational documents and applicable consumer/business requirements. Never promise a fixed amount while known scope items remain unresolved unless the commercial document clearly deals with them.

### 8. Keep records
Maintain the drawing revision, cutting list, quantity calculations, supplier prices, assumptions, labour estimate and approved changes. After completion, compare estimated quantities/costs with actuals. This improves future pricing.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A cabinet needs components cut from 18 mm board. Instead of adding a guessed percentage, the carpenter plots all parts onto the supplier's actual sheet size, includes verified process allowances, considers face/grain orientation and checks whether remaining offcuts are genuinely reusable. Hardware and edging are counted separately. Labour is estimated by operation. The resulting cost is traceable rather than based on intuition.

## Practical Activity
Using a simple shelf/cabinet drawing, create a costing sheet with component quantities, purchasing units, unit prices, direct material subtotal, consumables, estimated labour and other approved cost categories. Record all assumptions.

## Knowledge Check
1. Is net quantity always equal to purchasing quantity? **Answer: No.** Stock sizes, cutting plan and legitimate allowances affect purchasing quantity.
2. Why avoid a universal waste percentage? **Answer:** Waste depends on actual material, layout, process and job conditions.
3. Quantity take-off should primarily be based on: A) memory B) approved drawings/cutting information C) competitor prices D) appearance. **Answer: B.**
4. Why compare estimated and actual cost after the job? **Answer:** To improve future planning, pricing and waste control.

### Key Takeaways
Base quantities on controlled information; distinguish net from purchase quantity; plan waste rather than guessing it; include relevant materials, consumables and labour; record assumptions; and review actual performance.

Recommended visual: drawing → cutting list → cutting plan → supplier units → costing sheet. Caption: **“Traceable costing begins with traceable quantities.”**
