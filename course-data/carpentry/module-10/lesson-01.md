# Carpentry — Module 10: Applied Carpentry Practice & Workplace Readiness
## Lesson 1 — Interpreting a Client or Workplace Brief
**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. identify the essential requirements in a client or workplace brief;
2. distinguish stated requirements from assumptions that require clarification;
3. connect a brief to drawings, specifications, dimensions, materials, quality and time requirements;
4. record clarifications and changes before work begins; and
5. explain how accurate interpretation reduces defects, waste and disputes.

### Why This Lesson Matters
A carpenter may have excellent tool skills and still produce unacceptable work if the job itself has been misunderstood. Workplace readiness begins with translating a request into controlled work information. The carpenter must know what is being made or installed, where it will be used, what dimensions and materials apply, what finish and quality are expected, what safety or site restrictions exist, and when the work is required. Where information conflicts or is missing, guessing is not professional practice.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Brief** — the description of the work, need or result required by a client, supervisor or organisation.
- **Specification** — controlled information describing required materials, dimensions, performance, finish or quality.
- **Scope** — the work that is included in the job and the boundaries of what is not included.
- **Constraint** — a condition that limits how the work may be performed, such as time, access, budget or site restrictions.
- **Clarification** — confirmation obtained when information is incomplete, ambiguous or conflicting.
- **Revision** — an authorised change to previously issued job information.
- **Acceptance criteria** — conditions used to decide whether completed work is acceptable.
- **Handover** — the controlled transfer of completed work to the client, supervisor or next responsible person.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Begin with the required result
A brief should first be read for the required outcome rather than for isolated dimensions. Ask: What must exist when the job is complete? It may be a fitted shelf, repaired door, timber frame, storage unit, counter, panel or another component. Identify its intended use and location because these affect material selection, fixing, finish and durability.

### 2. Separate requirements from assumptions
A stated requirement is supported by approved information. An assumption is something the carpenter believes may be true but has not verified. For example, a drawing may show a cabinet width but not confirm whether the dimension is overall or between adjacent surfaces. The correct action is to clarify the ambiguity, not choose the most convenient interpretation. Never invent a missing dimension, material grade, fixing method or finish where it affects conformity.

### 3. Read the complete information set
A workplace brief may be supported by drawings, cutting lists, schedules, manufacturer information, site measurements and written instructions. These documents must be read together. Working drawings establish geometry and dimensions; schedules may identify timber sizes or hardware; specifications may control material or finish; site conditions may affect installation. South African vocational carpentry standards repeatedly require accurate interpretation of working drawings and preparation of cutting lists from those drawings. This reinforces an important professional principle: manufacture and installation should be based on controlled job information, not memory or guesswork.

### 4. Identify scope and constraints
List what the job includes: manufacture, delivery, removal of an existing item, installation, finishing, hardware, adjustment, cleaning or handover. Then identify constraints such as restricted access, occupied premises, permitted working hours, available power, dust controls, protection of nearby finishes, completion date and interfaces with other trades. A technically correct component can still create a failed job if it cannot be safely delivered or installed in the actual environment.

### 5. Establish acceptance criteria before production
Quality should not be invented during final inspection. Determine how the work will be judged before cutting material. Criteria can include approved dimensions, squareness, alignment, fit, operation, surface condition, hardware position and specified finish. Avoid creating universal tolerances: use the drawing, specification, manufacturer requirement or authorised workplace standard applicable to the job.

### 6. Control changes
Clients and supervisors may change requirements. A verbal request should not silently replace approved information where the change affects cost, material, dimensions, safety, programme or quality. Record the requested change, obtain the required approval, update the relevant information and ensure that everyone is working from the current revision.

### 7. Convert the brief into an action record
Before production, prepare a short job interpretation record containing the required product, location, dimensions, material, finish, hardware, installation requirements, quality criteria, deadline, constraints and outstanding questions. This creates traceability and makes planning easier in later lessons.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A client requests a wall-mounted timber storage unit. The sketch gives overall width and height but does not show depth. A message says “same finish as existing unit,” but no finish product is identified. The wall type is also unknown. A poor response is to assume a common depth, buy a similar-looking coating and select fixings from habit. A professional response is to record the known dimensions, confirm the required depth and finish system, inspect or verify the substrate before specifying installation fixings, confirm load expectations, and obtain approval for any revised information before production.

## Practical Activity
Create a **Job Brief Interpretation Record** for a small shelf or cabinet project. Include required result, location, known dimensions, material, finish, hardware, installation conditions, acceptance criteria, deadline, constraints, and at least three clarification questions. Mark each item as confirmed, pending clarification or revised.

## Knowledge Check
1. What should a carpenter do when two controlled job documents conflict? A) Choose the easier one B) Average the values C) Stop the affected work and clarify D) Use memory. **Answer: C.**
2. Why should acceptance criteria be identified early? A) To avoid measuring B) So production and inspection use the same requirements C) To eliminate drawings D) To permit substitutions. **Answer: B.**
3. A client verbally changes the material after approval. What is the best response? A) Change it silently B) Record and obtain required authorisation before affected work proceeds C) Ignore the client D) Use both materials. **Answer: B.**
4. Is a missing dimension permission to select a convenient value? **Answer: No.** Material job information must be clarified where the missing value affects conformity.

### Feedback / Explanation
Professional interpretation is evidence-based. Drawings, specifications, schedules and approved instructions control the work. Clarification prevents avoidable rework, while revision control ensures that production follows the latest authorised requirement.

### Key Takeaways
- Understand the required result before planning production.
- Separate confirmed requirements from assumptions.
- Read drawings, schedules, specifications and site information together.
- Identify scope, constraints and acceptance criteria early.
- Never invent missing critical information.
- Record and authorise significant changes.
- Accurate interpretation reduces waste, defects, delay and disputes.
