# Carpentry — Module 10: Applied Carpentry Practice & Workplace Readiness
## Lesson 4 — Safe Work Method & Risk Planning
**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
Learners should be able to identify hazards for a planned job; connect controls to the work sequence; include safe tool/material/site arrangements; recognise when work must stop; and prepare a basic task risk/work-method record without pretending it replaces workplace legal procedures.

### Why This Lesson Matters
Safety planning is not a paragraph added after production planning. Hazards change with each stage: moving boards, cutting, routing, sanding, coating, transport, drilling into a substrate and installation all create different risks. Professional carpenters anticipate these risks before exposing people or property to them.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Hazard** — a source or situation with potential to cause harm.
- **Risk** — the combination of likelihood and consequence associated with exposure to a hazard.
- **Control measure** — action used to eliminate or reduce risk.
- **Isolation** — separating energy, equipment or a hazardous area from exposure according to approved procedure.
- **PPE** — personal protective equipment used where appropriate as part of risk control.
- **SDS** — safety data sheet providing hazard and handling information for a chemical product.
- **Stop-work condition** — a situation in which affected work should not continue until risk or uncertainty is controlled.
- **Method statement** — documented description of how work will be carried out under applicable organisational/project requirements.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Plan safety around the actual task
Begin with the approved work sequence and ask what can cause injury, ill health, damage or uncontrolled exposure at each stage. Consider sharp tools, rotating cutters, kickback/ejection, electricity, dust, noise, manual handling, unstable workpieces, chemicals, fire, slips/trips and site interfaces.

### 2. Prefer higher-order controls
Where reasonably practicable, eliminate a hazard or reduce exposure at source before relying only on PPE. Use correct guards, extraction/dust controls, stable workholding, safe layouts and approved equipment. PPE remains important where specified, but it is not a substitute for defective guarding or unsafe methods.

### 3. Verify tools before use
Select the correct tool and accessory, inspect condition, ensure guards and safety features are present and functional, and follow manufacturer/workplace procedures. Remove defective equipment from use according to reporting/isolation arrangements. Never bypass a guard to save time.

### 4. Control materials and workpieces
Large sheets and long timber can cause handling and stability problems. Plan storage, lifting/team handling or mechanical assistance where appropriate, clear routes and adequate support. During machining, use correct workholding and support so the workpiece does not become an uncontrolled projectile or trap.

### 5. Plan dust, noise and finishing exposure
Cutting and sanding can create wood dust; finishing products may have specific ventilation, ignition, PPE, storage and disposal requirements. Use product labels/SDS and workplace procedures. Do not assume all coatings have identical hazards or drying requirements.

### 6. Plan site installation
Before drilling or fixing, verify the substrate and consider concealed services using approved site methods. Control the work area, access equipment, falling objects, public/occupant interaction and housekeeping. Use only suitable, specified fixing systems and do not guess load capacity.

### 7. Define stop-work triggers
Examples include conflicting job information, damaged guards, unknown substrate where fixing safety depends on it, uncontrolled electrical hazard, unsafe access, significant material defect, unverified chemical requirement or an incident that makes the area unsafe. Stopping affected work to obtain control is professional judgement.

### 8. Record and communicate
A basic risk/work-method record can show task, hazard, persons exposed, control, required PPE, equipment, inspection and responsible person. Actual workplaces may require formal risk assessments, permits, supervision or method statements; learners must follow those systems.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A wall cabinet installation includes transport of heavy components, drilling, elevated work and public access. The carpenter plans a clear delivery route, suitable handling, protected work zone, approved access equipment, verification of wall construction/services, specified anchors, dust control and final housekeeping. If the substrate differs from the approved installation information, drilling stops until the fixing method is confirmed.

## Practical Activity
Prepare a task-based hazard/control table for manufacturing and installing a small shelf. Include at least eight hazards and identify a stop-work condition for each major phase.

## Knowledge Check
1. Is PPE alone an adequate response to a missing machine guard? **Answer: No.**
2. Before fixing into an unknown wall, the carpenter should: A) drill immediately B) verify substrate/services and approved fixing method C) use longest screw D) use adhesive only. **Answer: B.**
3. Why use an SDS? **Answer:** To obtain product-specific hazard, handling, storage and emergency information.
4. A stop-work decision can be professional when essential controls are absent. **Answer: True.**

### Key Takeaways
Plan risk by task; use appropriate controls at source; inspect tools; control workpieces, dust, chemicals and installation hazards; define stop-work triggers; and follow workplace/legal systems.
