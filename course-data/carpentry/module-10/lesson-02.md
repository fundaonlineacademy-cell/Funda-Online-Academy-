# Carpentry — Module 10: Applied Carpentry Practice & Workplace Readiness
## Lesson 2 — Planning an End-to-End Carpentry Job
**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
Learners should be able to sequence a carpentry job from approved information to handover; identify resources and dependencies; prepare a practical work plan; include inspection points; and revise the plan when authorised conditions change.

### Why This Lesson Matters
Planning connects technical knowledge to reliable delivery. A carpenter who starts cutting before confirming information, materials, tools, site conditions and sequence can create expensive errors. Professional planning reduces waiting, repeated handling, damage and rework while protecting quality and safety.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Work sequence** — the planned order in which activities are performed.
- **Dependency** — an activity or condition that must be completed before another task can proceed.
- **Milestone** — an important completion or review point in the job.
- **Resource** — labour, material, tool, equipment, information, space or time needed for work.
- **Inspection point** — a planned stage at which conformity is checked before work continues.
- **Lead time** — time required to obtain a material, component or service.
- **Contingency** — a controlled response prepared for a foreseeable disruption.
- **Job pack** — the organised information needed to execute and record the work.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Plan from approved information
Planning begins only after the brief, drawings, dimensions and relevant specifications are understood. A plan based on an unresolved dimension is not complete. Mark outstanding clarifications and prevent affected work from proceeding until they are resolved.

### 2. Break the job into stages
A useful sequence for many workshop projects is: review information; verify measurements; prepare cutting/material lists; inspect materials; prepare tools/work area; mark out; cut/machine; prepare joints; dry assemble; correct fit; glue/fix; cure where required; prepare surfaces; finish; inspect; protect; deliver/install; adjust; clean; hand over. The exact sequence must suit the job and product instructions rather than being treated as a universal formula.

### 3. Identify dependencies
Some tasks cannot safely or logically start early. Finishing normally depends on adequate surface preparation. Final assembly may depend on successful dry fitting. Installation depends on site access and suitable substrate information. A fixing decision may depend on verified wall construction and expected load. Recognising dependencies prevents rushed improvisation.

### 4. Plan resources
For every stage, identify materials, consumables, tools, equipment, PPE, people, workspace and information required. Check that tools are serviceable and appropriate. Confirm material quantities and suitability before machining. Where special hardware or coatings have lead times, order them early enough to avoid unnecessary delay.

### 5. Build quality checks into the sequence
Do not leave quality control until the end. Inspect material before cutting, verify first-off dimensions, check joint fit before adhesive, check assembly square before final fixing, inspect surfaces before finishing, and confirm operation before handover. A defect found early is usually easier and cheaper to correct.

### 6. Coordinate workshop and site work
Installation projects require site readiness. Confirm access, working space, interfaces with other trades, substrate condition, finished floor/wall references where relevant, protection requirements and permitted working times. Protect completed work during transport and installation.

### 7. Plan for change without planning to guess
A contingency is not permission to improvise outside specifications. If a specified material is unavailable, the contingency may be to request an approved substitute, not silently install a different product. If site measurements differ materially from drawings, stop the affected activity and escalate the discrepancy.

### 8. Keep the plan usable
A professional work plan can be a table with activity, required information, resources, predecessor, inspection point, responsible person and status. Update it when authorised changes occur. This provides a simple production record and supports communication.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
For a fitted storage unit, the carpenter plans: confirm opening dimensions; approve final drawing; produce cutting list; inspect boards; cut and edge components; drill hardware; dry assemble; verify dimensions and square; final assemble; prepare surfaces; install hardware; inspect; protect for transport; verify wall/substrate and services; install using specified fixing system; adjust doors; clean; conduct final inspection and handover. The carpenter deliberately schedules inspection before irreversible steps.

## Practical Activity
Prepare an end-to-end plan for a small cabinet or timber frame. Include at least 12 activities, dependencies, resources and four inspection points. Highlight any task that must not start while information is unresolved.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why place inspection points before irreversible work? **Answer:** To detect nonconformity while correction is still controlled and less wasteful.
2. What is a dependency? A) Decoration B) A prerequisite for another activity C) A price D) A defect. **Answer: B.**
3. A specified hinge is unavailable. Best response? A) Fit any hinge B) Omit hinges C) obtain approval for an appropriate substitution D) change the door. **Answer: C.**
4. Should the same sequence be forced onto every carpentry job? **Answer: No.** Sequence must suit approved requirements, product instructions and conditions.

### Key Takeaways
Plan before production; identify dependencies and resources; include quality checks throughout; coordinate workshop and site requirements; protect completed work; and control changes rather than improvising.
