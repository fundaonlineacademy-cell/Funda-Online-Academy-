# Carpentry — Professional Skills Short Course

## Module 2: Hand Tools: Selection, Use & Maintenance
### Lesson 1: Hand Tool Categories & Correct Selection

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. Identify the main categories of carpentry hand tools and explain their purposes.
2. Select an appropriate hand tool by considering the task, material, required accuracy and condition of the tool.
3. Explain why using the wrong tool can reduce quality and increase the risk of injury or damage.
4. Carry out a basic pre-use inspection of common non-powered carpentry tools.
5. Recognise when a tool is unsuitable, damaged or beyond the learner’s competence and should not be used.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A carpenter’s effectiveness does not depend only on owning many tools. It depends on choosing the correct tool, using it for its intended purpose and keeping it in serviceable condition. A poor tool choice can damage timber, produce inaccurate work, waste material and require unnecessary force. It can also expose the worker or people nearby to avoidable hazards.

Hand tools remain important even in workshops that use power tools. A carpenter may use a rule and square to set out a component, a saw to make a controlled manual cut, a chisel to refine a joint, a plane to prepare an edge, a screwdriver to install a fitting, and clamps to hold an assembly. Correct selection therefore supports safety, accuracy, productivity and professional workmanship throughout the job.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary

**Hand tool** — A tool operated primarily by the user’s physical effort rather than by an electric, pneumatic or other powered drive. Examples include chisels, hand saws, planes and screwdrivers.

**Cutting tool** — A tool designed to remove or separate material using a sharpened cutting edge or teeth. Carpentry examples include saws and chisels.

**Measuring tool** — A tool used to determine dimensions, distances or sizes, such as a tape measure or steel rule.

**Marking tool** — A tool used to transfer a measurement, reference line or position onto a workpiece. Examples include pencils, marking knives and marking gauges.

**Workholding** — The safe and controlled securing of a workpiece so that it remains stable while an operation is performed. Vices and clamps are common workholding devices.

**Serviceable** — In a condition suitable for safe and effective use. A serviceable tool is not merely present; its important parts must function as intended.

**Cutting edge** — The sharpened working part of a blade, chisel or similar tool that removes or separates material.

**Intended use** — The type of work for which a tool was designed and specified. Using a tool outside its intended purpose can damage the tool, workpiece or user.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Think about the operation before choosing the tool
Professional tool selection begins with the work, not with whichever tool is closest. Before reaching for a hand tool, ask: What operation must be performed? What material am I working on? How accurate must the result be? What finish is required? How is the workpiece going to be supported? Is the tool suitable and serviceable?

For example, “I need to remove wood” is not specific enough to select a tool. The carpenter may need to crosscut a board to length, pare a small amount from a joint, flatten an edge or remove a rough projection. These operations remove material in different ways and therefore call for different tools and techniques.

A useful professional habit is to separate four decisions: **measure**, **mark**, **secure**, then **work**. Many avoidable errors occur because a learner begins cutting before checking the dimension, marking an accurate reference or securing the workpiece.

### 2. Main categories of carpentry hand tools
Carpentry hand tools can be grouped by function. The categories sometimes overlap, but classification helps the learner understand what each tool is meant to achieve.

**Measuring and checking tools** include tape measures, steel rules, squares, levels and other checking devices. Their purpose is not to cut timber but to establish or verify dimensions and geometry. An inaccurate measurement at the beginning of a job can affect every operation that follows.

**Marking and setting-out tools** include pencils, marking knives, gauges and bevels. They transfer the planned dimensions or geometry to the workpiece. A clear, correctly positioned reference line helps the carpenter know where material must remain and where it may be removed.

**Cutting and shaping tools** include hand saws, chisels and planes. These tools remove material, but they do not perform the same operation. A saw separates material along a cut; a chisel can chop or pare localised material; a plane removes controlled shavings to refine a surface or edge.

**Striking and driving tools** include hammers and mallets. They deliver controlled impact. Selection matters: a mallet used with an appropriate chisel is different from a hammer chosen to drive a nail. Striking a tool with an unsuitable implement can damage handles, working ends or the workpiece.

**Fastening and adjustment tools** include screwdrivers, spanners and other tools suited to particular fasteners. The working end should match the fastener. A poorly fitting screwdriver can slip, damage the screw head and make later removal difficult.

**Workholding tools** include clamps and vices. They are not optional accessories when a task requires the workpiece to remain stable. Effective workholding can improve both control and accuracy and can keep hands away from the path of a cutting tool.

### 3. Match the tool to the task and material
Correct selection is more detailed than choosing the right category. Tools within the same category can have different designs and purposes. A carpenter therefore considers the operation, dimensions, access, material and required quality before selecting a specific tool.

The material also matters. Solid timber, plywood, MDF and other boards can respond differently to cutting and shaping. Grain direction affects many operations on solid wood. Some sheet materials can chip or damage easily at an edge. The tool, its condition and the technique must therefore suit the material rather than assuming every wood-based product behaves identically.

Required accuracy is another factor. Roughly reducing an oversized component and making a final fitting adjustment are not the same task. The closer the operation is to the finished dimension, the more carefully the carpenter must control material removal and check progress.

### 4. Tool condition is part of tool selection
A tool is not correctly selected if it is the right type but unsafe or ineffective because of its condition. Before use, visually and functionally inspect the features relevant to that tool.

Look for cracked, split or loose handles; damaged or mushroomed striking surfaces; corrosion or damage that affects function; missing parts; bent components; damaged saw teeth; chipped or badly damaged cutting edges; loose adjustments; and contamination that prevents a secure grip or proper operation. A cutting tool that is excessively dull can encourage the user to apply unnecessary force, reducing control and the quality of the work.

Do not improvise repairs to safety-critical defects or continue using a tool simply because the job is almost finished. Remove defective tools from use according to workplace procedure and report them to the responsible person where applicable.

### 5. The wrong tool can create a chain of problems
Misuse often begins with convenience: using a chisel as a general pry bar, a screwdriver as a chisel, pliers where the correct spanner is required, or an unsuitable hammer on a tool that should be struck with a mallet. The immediate result may be a damaged tool or fastener, but the consequences can extend further.

A damaged edge may slip. A split handle may fail under load. A rounded fastener may become difficult to remove. A damaged workpiece may require rework or replacement. Excessive force may reduce balance and control. Professional practice therefore treats correct tool selection as both a quality-control decision and a safety decision.

### 6. Workholding must be planned with the tool
The correct cutting tool is only part of a safe setup. The workpiece must also be supported and secured appropriately. Holding a small workpiece in one hand while cutting toward that hand is not a substitute for proper workholding.

Before starting, consider where the tool will travel if it slips or completes the cut. Keep hands and other body parts out of that path. Position clamps so that they secure the work without obstructing the intended operation. Ensure the bench, vice or support is stable and suitable for the load.

This principle becomes especially important with chisels and saws because the cutting edge or teeth can continue moving after passing through the material. Good workholding gives the learner a predictable workpiece and allows attention to remain on technique and accuracy.

### 7. Sharpness, maintenance and storage support safe performance
Many hand tools depend on a correctly maintained cutting edge. A sharp, properly prepared tool can make a controlled cut with less unnecessary force than a neglected one. However, sharpening is itself a skill. Learners should use the sharpening method, equipment and supervision appropriate to the tool rather than experimenting with unfamiliar grinding or sharpening equipment.

After use, tools should be cleaned as appropriate, inspected and stored so that cutting edges are protected and tools do not create hazards for the next person. Do not leave sharp tools hidden beneath waste, loose on unstable surfaces or with exposed edges where someone can reach into them unknowingly.

Tool care also supports the academy’s wider professional standard: the next user should receive equipment in a predictable, serviceable condition.

### 8. Know when not to use a tool
Professional judgement includes stopping. Do not use a tool if you cannot identify it, do not understand its intended use, have not been shown the required technique, cannot secure the workpiece, find a significant defect, or cannot complete the operation without improvising an unsafe method.

Ask a competent supervisor or instructor for guidance. This is not a sign of poor performance. It is evidence that the learner understands the boundary between responsible work and unsafe guessing.

Hand tools can cause serious injuries even though they have no motor. The absence of electrical power does not remove the need for training, appropriate selection, sound condition, controlled technique and safe storage.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
**Scenario:** Lwazi must shorten a timber component accurately and then make a small fitting adjustment at one end.

A poor approach would be to pick up a chisel and attempt to remove all the excess timber because the chisel is already on the bench. This would require unnecessary effort and make it difficult to produce a controlled, square end.

A professional approach is:
1. Confirm the required finished length from the job information.
2. Measure from the correct reference point.
3. Mark the cut accurately and check the line.
4. Secure the timber appropriately.
5. Select a suitable hand saw for the required cut and confirm that it is serviceable.
6. Make the main cut using controlled technique.
7. Check the result before removing additional material.
8. If a small local adjustment is genuinely required, select an appropriate sharp, serviceable tool and use a controlled method rather than continuing to remove material blindly.

The important lesson is that the job may require more than one hand tool. Professional selection follows each operation in the workflow.

## Knowledge Check

**1. What is the best first question when selecting a carpentry hand tool?**
A. Which tool is closest to the bench?
B. Which tool looks most professional?
C. What operation must be performed and what result is required?
D. Which tool is heaviest?

**Correct answer: C.**

**Feedback:** Tool selection starts with understanding the task, material, accuracy and required result. Convenience is not an adequate selection method.

**2. A chisel has the correct width for a task, but its handle is badly split. What should the learner do?**
A. Use it carefully because the width is correct.
B. Wrap the handle with any available tape and continue.
C. Remove it from use and follow the workplace defect-reporting or replacement procedure.
D. Strike it more lightly and finish quickly.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Feedback:** Correct type and size do not make a defective tool serviceable. Significant defects must be dealt with before the tool is used.

**3. Why is workholding part of hand-tool selection and setup?**
A. It makes every tool sharper.
B. It helps keep the workpiece stable and supports controlled, accurate operation.
C. It removes the need to measure.
D. It guarantees that no PPE is required.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Feedback:** Appropriate workholding improves stability and control. It does not replace measuring, training or any other required control.

**4. Which statement best describes professional hand-tool use?**
A. A skilled carpenter can safely use almost any tool for any purpose.
B. Correct selection includes the task, material, condition, technique and workholding requirements.
C. Hand tools do not require inspection because they have no motor.
D. A blunt cutting tool is safer because it cannot cut easily.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Feedback:** Professional selection is a complete decision, not simply identifying a tool by name. Poorly maintained tools can require excessive force and reduce control.

## Key Takeaways
- Select the tool for the operation, material, required accuracy and intended use.
- Carpentry hand tools include measuring, marking, cutting, shaping, striking, fastening and workholding tools.
- A tool of the correct type must also be serviceable and suitable for the specific task.
- Correct workholding is part of the setup, especially when using cutting tools.
- Do not improvise by using tools for purposes for which they were not designed.
- Sharp, correctly maintained cutting tools support controlled work; sharpening must be carried out using appropriate methods and competence.
- Stop and seek guidance when a tool is defective, unfamiliar or cannot be used safely with the available setup.
