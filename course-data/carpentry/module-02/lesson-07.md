# Carpentry — Professional Skills Short Course
## Module 2: Hand Tools: Selection, Use & Maintenance
### Lesson 7: Clamps, Vices & Safe Workholding

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. Explain why secure workholding is essential for safe, accurate carpentry.
2. Identify common clamps, vices and supporting devices and select them for appropriate tasks.
3. Position clamps and workpieces so that pressure is controlled and the work is not unnecessarily damaged or distorted.
4. Inspect workholding equipment before use and recognise defects that require removal from service or reporting.
5. Apply a systematic workholding check before sawing, chiselling, planing, drilling, fastening or assembly.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A carpenter often needs both hands to control a tool. If the workpiece moves while it is being cut, chiselled, planed, drilled, fastened or assembled, the result may be inaccurate and the tool may move in an unintended direction. Secure workholding therefore supports safety and workmanship at the same time.

Good workholding is not simply a matter of tightening a clamp as hard as possible. The carpenter must select a suitable device, support the material, position pressure correctly, protect finished surfaces where necessary and confirm that the workpiece is stable before starting the operation. Excessive or poorly positioned pressure can bow a component, mark a surface, pull an assembly out of square or damage an edge.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary

**Workholding** — The methods and devices used to keep a workpiece stable and correctly positioned while work is carried out.

**Clamp** — A device that applies controlled pressure to hold one or more components in position temporarily.

**Vice** — A fixed or portable holding device, normally using jaws that close around a workpiece.

**Jaw** — The part of a vice or clamp that contacts and grips the workpiece.

**Clamping pressure** — The force applied by a clamp or vice to hold components. It should be sufficient for stability without unnecessarily crushing, marking or distorting the material.

**Caul** — A protective or pressure-spreading piece placed between a clamp and a workpiece, or across an assembly, to distribute force and help protect the surface.

**Support** — A bench, trestle, block or other suitable surface that carries the workpiece so that it remains stable during an operation.

**Racking** — Distortion in which a rectangular frame or assembly shifts away from square, often because forces are uneven or the assembly is not properly controlled.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Workholding is part of the operation, not an afterthought
Before a carpenter starts an operation, the workpiece should be positioned so the intended tool can be used without the material moving unexpectedly. Trying to hold a component with one hand while controlling a saw, chisel or other tool with the other can reduce control and may place the holding hand close to the cutting path.

The correct question is therefore not only, “Which tool will I use?” It is also, “How will I support and secure the work while I use that tool?” This decision should be made during task preparation.

A good workholding arrangement should keep the work stable, allow the tool to travel through its intended path, keep clamps out of that path, provide enough access to see reference marks, and avoid creating unnecessary strain or awkward body position.

### 2. Common clamps and their uses
Carpentry workshops may contain several types of clamps. Names and designs vary between manufacturers, so the learner should identify the actual equipment available in the workplace and follow the relevant instructions.

A **G-clamp or C-clamp** has a rigid frame and screw mechanism. It is useful for holding a workpiece to a bench, securing components during some assembly operations, or applying pressure over a relatively local area. Because the frame has a limited throat depth, it cannot reach far into a wide panel.

A **bar clamp or sash clamp** provides a greater clamping span and is commonly useful when assembling frames, panels and wider components. Pressure can act over a larger assembly, so alignment and squareness should be checked as clamps are tightened.

A **quick-action clamp** can be positioned rapidly and is useful for many temporary holding operations. Speed, however, does not remove the need to confirm that the clamp has sufficient capacity and is properly seated.

A **spring clamp** provides relatively light, convenient pressure. It may be useful for temporary positioning or light components, but it should not be assumed to provide the same holding force or control as a screw or bar clamp.

Specialised clamps may also be available for corners, edges or particular assembly tasks. Select a clamp because its design suits the operation, not simply because it is the nearest one on the bench.

### 3. Vices provide stable bench-based workholding
A bench vice uses fixed and movable jaws to grip a workpiece. South African Department of Basic Education workshop material describes a bench vice as a device used to hold a workpiece firmly while it is being worked on. That basic principle applies directly to carpentry workholding as well.

A woodworking vice is particularly useful for operations such as planing edges, controlled sawing, fitting and some chisel work. The workpiece should sit securely without unnecessary projection. Excessive projection above or beyond the jaws can allow vibration or movement.

Jaw faces should be appropriate for the material. Where a vice or clamp could mark a finished or delicate timber surface, suitable protective jaw liners, pads or cauls may be used where permitted by the workplace procedure. Protection must not make the grip unstable.

Never use a vice as though it were an anvil unless it is specifically designed and authorised for that purpose. Do not extend the vice handle with a pipe or other improvised lever to create extra force. Excessive force can damage the vice, the workpiece or both.

### 4. Inspect workholding equipment before use
Clamps and vices are mechanical devices and can deteriorate. Before use, check the device for obvious damage and correct operation.

For a clamp, look for a bent or cracked frame or bar, damaged screw threads, missing or damaged pads, a loose handle, a slipping movable head, contamination that prevents secure seating, or any other condition that prevents controlled pressure.

For a vice, check that it is securely mounted where applicable, that the jaws and screw mechanism operate correctly, that the handle is serviceable and that there is no obvious damage affecting safe holding.

If a device cannot hold reliably, do not compensate by improvising or applying excessive force. Remove it from use or report it according to the workshop procedure.

### 5. Support the workpiece before applying pressure
A clamp cannot correct poor support. Long boards, wide panels and assemblies may need a bench, trestles or additional supports so their weight does not twist the work or overload the clamped area.

The support arrangement must also account for the operation. When sawing, the cut line and offcut need appropriate support without trapping the saw. During planing, the workpiece must resist the horizontal force of the plane. During chiselling, it must resist both impact and cutting forces. During assembly, the components need to remain aligned while pressure is applied.

Clamps should not be positioned where the tool will strike them. A saw, drill bit, plane or chisel contacting a metal clamp can damage the cutting edge and create an unsafe event.

### 6. Apply pressure deliberately
More pressure is not automatically better. The purpose of clamping is to create sufficient controlled holding force for the task.

When joining components, apply pressure gradually and observe what happens. Check that mating surfaces remain aligned, components have not slipped, the assembly remains square where required, and the clamp faces are seated properly. Where several clamps are used, pressure may need to be increased progressively rather than fully tightening one clamp before the others are positioned.

A rectangular frame can rack if pressure is uneven. The carpenter should compare the assembly with the drawing or job specification and use suitable checks, such as a square or relevant dimensional comparison, rather than assuming that clamping automatically produces correct geometry.

When adhesive is involved, clamping requirements depend on the adhesive manufacturer’s instructions, joint design and materials. Do not invent a universal pressure or clamping time. Follow the product instructions and workplace procedure.

### 7. Protect the work without compromising the hold
Clamp faces can leave dents or marks, particularly on softer timber or finished surfaces. A suitable scrap block, pad or caul can spread pressure and protect the workpiece. Cauls can also help distribute force across an assembly.

Protection should be clean and appropriately shaped. A dirty pad containing grit can mark the work. A badly positioned block can cause uneven pressure. A soft layer that compresses too easily may reduce stability. The learner should therefore treat protective pieces as part of the workholding setup, not as random scraps placed under a clamp.

### 8. Perform a final stability check before starting the tool
Before beginning the operation, deliberately test the setup. Without placing hands in a dangerous position, confirm that the workpiece is stable, the clamp or vice is seated correctly, supports are stable, the tool path is clear, reference lines remain visible and the intended operation will not release an unsupported section unexpectedly.

During the task, stop if the workpiece begins to move, a clamp loosens, a support shifts or the setup no longer feels controlled. Re-secure the work rather than trying to finish the operation quickly.

Professional carpentry depends on repeatable control. Good workholding makes accurate measurement, cutting, shaping, drilling, fastening and assembly more achievable and reduces avoidable rework.

## Worked Example / Demonstration

A learner must hand-saw the end of a timber component accurately to a marked line.

A poor setup would be to place the timber loosely on the corner of a bench and hold it with one hand while sawing with the other. The work can move, the holding hand may be too close to the saw path, and the cut may wander.

A better process is:
1. Inspect the timber and confirm the correct cut line and waste side.
2. Select a suitable bench position and workholding method.
3. Position the timber so the cut is accessible and the work is adequately supported.
4. Secure it using a suitable vice or clamp without covering the cut line.
5. Confirm the clamp is not in the saw path and the offcut will not create an uncontrolled situation.
6. Test the stability of the workpiece.
7. Make the cut using the correct sawing technique.
8. Stop and re-secure the work if movement develops.
9. Inspect the completed cut against the required line and geometry.

The workholding step is therefore part of cutting accuracy, not merely a safety precaution.

## Practical Activity — Workholding Selection & Setup Record

Under appropriate supervision and using the equipment available in the training environment, select **three different carpentry tasks**, for example:
- hand-sawing a short component;
- planing an edge;
- assembling two practice pieces;
- controlled chiselling on a practice piece;
- marking or drilling a supported component.

For each task, record:

| Task | Workholding device selected | Why it suits the task | Support required | Tool path clear? | Surface protection needed? | Final stability check |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example: Hand-sawing timber | Bench vice | Holds component while both hands control saw | Bench/vice support | Yes | If required | Stable before cutting |

Then answer:
1. What could happen if the workpiece moved during the operation?
2. Could excessive clamping pressure damage or distort this workpiece?
3. Is any clamp positioned in the path of the tool?
4. What would make you stop and reset the workholding arrangement?

Keep the completed record as portfolio evidence where required by the academy.

## Knowledge Check

### Question 1
Why is secure workholding important when using a hand tool?

A. It makes measuring unnecessary.  
B. It keeps the work stable and improves control and accuracy.  
C. It allows any tool to be used for any task.  
D. It removes the need for pre-use checks.

**Correct answer: B**

**Feedback:** Secure workholding supports both safety and workmanship. It does not replace correct measurement, tool selection or inspection.

### Question 2
A learner is clamping a rectangular frame and notices it moving out of square. What is the best response?

A. Tighten one clamp as hard as possible.  
B. Ignore it because clamps automatically make assemblies square.  
C. Stop, correct the alignment and apply controlled, balanced pressure while rechecking the geometry.  
D. Hit the clamp with a hammer.

**Correct answer: C**

**Feedback:** Clamping pressure can move components. Alignment and squareness must be checked during assembly rather than assumed.

### Question 3
A G-clamp has damaged threads and slips when pressure is applied. What should the learner do?

A. Use a longer handle to tighten it harder.  
B. Remove it from use or report it according to workshop procedure.  
C. Hold the clamp closed by hand.  
D. Continue if the job is almost finished.

**Correct answer: B**

**Feedback:** A clamp that cannot maintain controlled holding force is not reliable workholding equipment. Improvised extra force does not correct the defect.

### Question 4
Why might a clean caul or protective pad be placed between a clamp and timber?

A. To make the clamp unnecessary.  
B. To spread pressure and help protect the work surface.  
C. To hide inaccurate marking.  
D. To increase the cutting speed of a saw.

**Correct answer: B**

**Feedback:** A suitable caul or pad can distribute pressure and reduce marking, provided it does not make the setup unstable.

## Feedback / Explanation for the Knowledge Check
The four questions follow the same professional workholding logic: **select → inspect → support → position → protect → apply controlled pressure → verify stability → work → recheck**. If any part of that sequence fails, the learner should correct the setup before continuing.

## Key Takeaways
1. Workholding is part of planning every hand-tool operation.
2. Different clamps and vices suit different spans, materials and tasks.
3. Workholding equipment must be inspected and must hold reliably before use.
4. The workpiece needs suitable support as well as clamping pressure.
5. Excessive or uneven pressure can mark timber, distort components or rack an assembly.
6. Clamps must be positioned outside the intended tool path.
7. Protective pads and cauls can distribute pressure and protect surfaces when correctly used.
8. If the work moves or the setup becomes unstable, stop and reset it before continuing.
