## Lesson 1 — Hand Tool Categories & Correct Selection

### Q1
A learner needs to mark a square line across a timber face before making a cut. Which selection is most appropriate?

A. Claw hammer  
B. Try square and suitable marking tool  
C. G-clamp only  
D. Jack plane

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** The required result is an accurate square line, so a try square used from a reliable reference edge with an appropriate marking tool is suitable.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 1 — selecting tools according to operation and required result.

### Q2
What is the best first question when selecting a hand tool?

A. Which tool is closest to me?  
B. Which tool is newest?  
C. What operation and result are required?  
D. Which tool is heaviest?

**Correct answer:** C  
**Explanation:** Professional selection begins with the task, material and required result, not convenience or appearance.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 1 — task-based tool selection.

### Q3
A screwdriver does not correctly fit the screw head. What should the learner do?

A. Apply more force  
B. Strike the screwdriver with a hammer  
C. Select a correctly fitting driver before continuing  
D. Hold the screw with fingers and force the driver

**Correct answer:** C  
**Explanation:** Incorrect driver fit increases slipping, damage and poor fastening. The correct type and size should be selected.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 1 — compatibility and correct selection.

### Q4
Why is improvised substitution of one hand tool for another generally poor practice?

A. It always makes the job slower but has no safety effect  
B. It can create uncontrolled forces, damage tools or workpieces and increase injury risk  
C. It is only a problem when using expensive timber  
D. It is acceptable whenever a supervisor is not present

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** Tools are designed for particular operations and loads. Improvised use can reduce control, quality and safety.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 1 — misuse and substitution.

## Lesson 2 — Measuring & Marking Hand Tools

### Q5
Why should measurements and markings be taken from an established reference face or edge where practical?

A. To make pencil lines darker  
B. To reduce accumulated error and keep layout relationships consistent  
C. To eliminate the need to inspect the work  
D. To make every workpiece the same colour

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** Consistent references reduce error transfer and improve dimensional relationships between marked features.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 2 — reference faces, edges and datums.

### Q6
A try square rocks against the timber instead of seating firmly. What should the learner do first?

A. Mark the line anyway  
B. Check the reference surface/edge and the tool for contamination or damage  
C. Press harder until the square bends  
D. Replace the pencil only

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** A square must register reliably against the reference. Dirt, damage or an unsuitable reference can make the marking inaccurate.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 2 — accurate use and condition checks.

### Q7
What is a marking gauge primarily useful for in woodworking?

A. Driving nails  
B. Marking a line parallel to a reference edge or face  
C. Checking whether a wall is plumb  
D. Removing saw marks

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** A marking gauge transfers a set distance from its stock to create a controlled parallel line.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 2 — marking gauges.

### Q8
A learner measures a component several times from different, unverified edges and obtains conflicting dimensions. What principle would best prevent this problem?

A. Increase pencil pressure  
B. Use a consistent verified datum/reference  
C. Use a hammer to align the rule  
D. Round all measurements upward

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** Establishing and maintaining a known reference helps prevent cumulative and contradictory layout errors.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 2 — datum-based measurement and marking.

## Lesson 3 — Saws & Manual Cutting Techniques

### Q9
Before sawing a marked component to final size, why should the learner identify the waste side of the line?

A. To determine where the saw kerf should be placed  
B. To make the saw heavier  
C. To avoid supporting the workpiece  
D. To eliminate the need for a square

**Correct answer:** A  
**Explanation:** The saw removes material as a kerf. Planning the cut relative to the line helps preserve the required finished dimension.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 3 — kerf and waste-side control.

### Q10
Which statement best describes a controlled start to a hand-saw cut?

A. Begin with maximum force immediately  
B. Establish the cut carefully with controlled strokes before developing a steady rhythm  
C. Twist the blade to widen the kerf  
D. Start without securing or supporting the workpiece

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** A controlled start helps the saw track accurately and reduces jumping or damage to the layout.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 3 — starting and controlling a cut.

### Q11
A long offcut is unsupported and begins to sag as a cut nears completion. What is the main concern?

A. It may bind, split or tear material and destabilise the operation  
B. It automatically sharpens the saw  
C. It improves the finished dimension  
D. It has no effect on the cut

**Correct answer:** A  
**Explanation:** Work and offcuts should be supported so their weight does not create uncontrolled movement, binding or breakout.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 3 — workpiece and offcut support.

### Q12
A learner notices the saw drifting away from the required line. What is the best response?

A. Increase force and continue  
B. Stop or reduce the cut, assess the cause and correct technique while sufficient material remains  
C. Bend the blade sideways during the stroke  
D. Ignore the error until assembly

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** Early correction is preferable to forcing a drifting blade and creating an oversized or unusable component.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 3 — cutting faults and corrective practice.

## Lesson 4 — Chisels: Chopping, Paring & Controlled Cutting

### Q13
Which statement best distinguishes paring from chopping with a chisel?

A. Paring normally uses controlled hand pressure for fine removal; chopping commonly uses controlled mallet blows where the tool and task permit  
B. Paring always uses a steel hammer  
C. Chopping is only for metal  
D. There is no practical difference

**Correct answer:** A  
**Explanation:** Paring and chopping use different force and control strategies. The chisel must be suitable for the operation.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 4 — chopping versus paring.

### Q14
Where should the learner's free hand be positioned during a controlled chisel operation?

A. Directly in front of the cutting edge  
B. In the expected path of the chisel  
C. Clear of the cutting path and potential slip direction  
D. Under the workpiece beside the cutting edge

**Correct answer:** C  
**Explanation:** Hands and body parts must be kept out of the path a cutting tool could follow if it slips or breaks through.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 4 — hand and body positioning.

### Q15
Why is removing waste progressively often preferable when chiselling a recess?

A. It gives better control and reduces the chance of splitting or removing material beyond the required boundary  
B. It makes measurement unnecessary  
C. It guarantees the grain cannot change direction  
D. It allows the workpiece to remain unsecured

**Correct answer:** A  
**Explanation:** Controlled incremental removal supports accuracy and reduces the consequences of excessive force.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 4 — progressive waste removal.

### Q16
A chisel handle is badly cracked. What should the learner do?

A. Wrap it loosely with paper and continue  
B. Remove the tool from use and report or arrange appropriate repair/replacement under workplace procedure  
C. Strike it harder so the crack closes  
D. Use it only for heavy chopping

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** A seriously defective handle can fail under load and should not remain in service.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 4 — pre-use inspection and defect response.

## Lesson 5 — Planes: Surface & Edge Preparation

### Q17
What is a principal reason for planing with attention to grain direction?

A. To reduce the risk of tear-out and improve surface control  
B. To make the plane heavier  
C. To avoid checking dimensions  
D. To eliminate the need for a sharp cutting edge

**Correct answer:** A  
**Explanation:** Grain direction influences how fibres are cut. Poor approach can lift fibres and produce tear-out.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 5 — grain direction and tear-out.

### Q18
When preparing a straight, square edge, what should the learner do during the process?

A. Plane continuously without checking until the edge looks smooth  
B. Check straightness and squareness repeatedly and correct high areas deliberately  
C. Judge only by colour  
D. Measure only after assembly

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** Surface appearance alone does not prove geometry. Regular checking prevents over-removal and guides correction.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 5 — edge preparation and dimensional checks.

### Q19
Why are light, controlled finishing passes useful when approaching the required dimension?

A. They reduce the risk of removing excessive material and help improve surface quality  
B. They make reference surfaces unnecessary  
C. They allow the blade to be blunt  
D. They guarantee all timber has identical grain

**Correct answer:** A  
**Explanation:** As the target is approached, smaller controlled removals give the worker greater dimensional control.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 5 — controlled planing and final passes.

### Q20
A plane suddenly begins producing severe tear-out on a changing-grain area. What is the best response?

A. Apply maximum pressure and continue unchanged  
B. Reassess grain direction, blade condition, setup and cutting approach before continuing  
C. Strike the plane with a hammer  
D. Wet the workpiece without checking material requirements

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** Tear-out is a signal to reassess the cutting conditions rather than simply applying more force.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 5 — diagnosing planing problems.

## Lesson 6 — Hammers, Mallets, Screwdrivers & Fastening Tools

### Q21
Why is a wooden or suitable non-marring mallet often selected when tapping a timber joint together?

A. It can apply controlled impact while reducing surface damage compared with an inappropriate hard-faced striking tool  
B. It cuts the joint automatically  
C. It replaces clamps in every assembly  
D. It measures joint depth

**Correct answer:** A  
**Explanation:** Tool selection should match both the required force and the need to protect the workpiece.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 6 — hammer and mallet selection.

### Q22
What is the most appropriate response when a screw is becoming damaged because the driver repeatedly slips?

A. Continue with greater force  
B. Stop and check driver type/size, screw condition, alignment and preparation  
C. Lubricate every screw regardless of material or specification  
D. Strike the screwdriver sideways

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** Repeated slipping indicates a setup or compatibility problem that should be corrected before damage increases.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 6 — screw/driver matching and fastening faults.

### Q23
Why may a pilot hole be useful before driving a screw in some timber applications?

A. It can guide the screw and reduce splitting or excessive driving resistance where appropriate  
B. It always replaces the screw  
C. It makes tool inspection unnecessary  
D. It is used only to change timber colour

**Correct answer:** A  
**Explanation:** Correct preparation can improve alignment and reduce material damage. Exact pilot requirements depend on the screw, material and application.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 6 — fastening preparation.

### Q24
After fastening two components, what should a quality check include?

A. Only whether the fastener is visible  
B. Alignment, seating, material damage, joint security and compliance with the required result  
C. Only the worker's speed  
D. Whether every screw was driven with maximum torque

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** Fastening quality includes the finished relationship and condition of the components, not merely the presence of a fastener.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 6 — fastening quality inspection.

## Lesson 7 — Clamps, Vices & Safe Workholding

### Q25
What is the primary purpose of workholding during many hand-tool operations?

A. To stabilise the workpiece so the operation can be controlled safely and accurately  
B. To make the timber heavier permanently  
C. To replace measurement  
D. To hide defects

**Correct answer:** A  
**Explanation:** Secure workholding reduces unwanted movement and supports controlled tool use and accuracy.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 7 — purpose of workholding.

### Q26
A clamp is tightened so aggressively that it dents a finished timber face. What does this demonstrate?

A. More pressure always improves quality  
B. Clamping pressure and contact surfaces must be controlled; protective pads may be appropriate  
C. Clamps should never touch timber  
D. Dents prove the joint is square

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** Excessive or poorly distributed pressure can damage workpieces. Pressure should be sufficient for the task, not simply maximum.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 7 — clamping pressure and surface protection.

### Q27
Before beginning a cutting operation on a clamped workpiece, what should the learner verify?

A. That the clamp obstructs the intended tool path  
B. Stability, clamp security, support and a clear tool path  
C. Only the colour of the clamp  
D. That the workpiece can still move freely

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** The workpiece must be stable and the holding arrangement must not create a collision or access hazard.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 7 — stability and clear tool paths.

### Q28
During glue-up, an assembly begins to rack out of square as clamps are tightened. What should the learner do?

A. Continue tightening without checking  
B. Adjust the clamping arrangement and pressure while checking alignment and squareness  
C. Add random clamps until movement stops  
D. Plane the assembly while the glue is wet

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** Clamp placement and pressure can alter alignment. The assembly should be checked and corrected during tightening.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 7 — racking, alignment and controlled clamping.

## Lesson 8 — Hand Tool Care, Sharpening, Storage & Inspection

### Q29
Why can a properly maintained sharp cutting tool improve control compared with a neglected blunt one?

A. It can perform the intended cut with less unnecessary force when used correctly  
B. A sharp tool cannot cause injury  
C. Sharp tools do not require workholding  
D. Sharpness eliminates the need for training

**Correct answer:** A  
**Explanation:** Proper cutting condition can reduce excessive force and improve quality, but a sharp edge remains hazardous and requires correct handling.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 8 — maintenance, sharpness and control.

### Q30
Which is the best end-of-task hand-tool routine?

A. Leave tools on the bench for the next learner  
B. Clean, inspect, maintain as authorised, protect vulnerable surfaces/edges and return tools to suitable storage  
C. Put wet or dirty tools into a closed box immediately  
D. Store sharp tools loose beneath other equipment

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** A consistent end-of-task routine protects tools, helps identify defects and prepares equipment for safe future use.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 8 — cleaning, inspection and storage.

### Q31
A learner finds a tool with a loose head during inspection. What is the professional response?

A. Hide it at the back of the tool cabinet  
B. Continue using it for light work  
C. Remove it from normal use and report it for appropriate repair or replacement  
D. Lend it to another learner

**Correct answer:** C  
**Explanation:** A defect that can affect safe operation should be reported and controlled rather than passed to another user.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 8 — defect reporting and unsafe tools.

### Q32
What is the best principle for sharpening a hand cutting tool?

A. Grind away as much metal as possible every time  
B. Follow the appropriate tool geometry, approved method and safe procedure, removing only what is necessary to restore a serviceable edge  
C. Change the bevel angle randomly  
D. Sharpen only after the tool has completely failed

**Correct answer:** B  
**Explanation:** Sharpening is controlled maintenance. Tool geometry, safe technique and manufacturer/workplace requirements should be respected.  
**Traceability:** Lesson 8 — sharpening and honing principles.
