# Carpentry — Professional Skills Short Course

## Module 2: Hand Tools: Selection, Use & Maintenance
### Lesson 5: Planes: Surface & Edge Preparation

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. Explain the purpose of common hand planes used for surface and edge preparation.
2. Select a suitable plane for rough preparation, general truing, smoothing, edge work and end-grain trimming.
3. Inspect and prepare a hand plane for controlled, safe use.
4. Apply correct planing technique while considering grain direction, workholding and reference surfaces.
5. Check a planed face or edge for flatness, straightness and squareness and respond appropriately to common defects.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A carpenter rarely receives every timber component in perfect condition. A board may have saw marks, small high spots, an edge that is not square, or a surface that needs refinement before marking, joining, assembly or finishing. Hand planes allow controlled removal of thin layers of timber and can produce accurate reference faces and edges when they are selected, adjusted and used correctly.

Good planing is not simply about making timber feel smooth. In professional work, a surface may need to be flat, an edge may need to be straight and square to a reference face, and a component may need to remain within the dimensions required by the job brief or drawing. Removing material without checking can make a component undersize and create problems that cannot easily be corrected. The professional carpenter therefore combines tool control with repeated measurement and inspection.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary

**Hand plane** — A hand-operated cutting tool that uses a sharpened iron or blade projecting through the sole to remove controlled shavings from timber.

**Sole** — The bottom reference surface of a plane that travels over the workpiece and helps guide the cutting action.

**Plane iron** — The sharpened cutting blade of a hand plane. Its condition, projection and alignment strongly influence the quality of the cut.

**Jack plane** — A versatile bench plane commonly used for general stock preparation, reducing high areas and preparing surfaces before finer work.

**Smoothing plane** — A shorter bench plane normally set for fine cuts to refine a surface after major flattening or preparation has been completed.

**Reference face** — A verified face of a workpiece used as the primary datum from which other measurements and squareness checks are made.

**Tear-out** — Damage that occurs when wood fibres are lifted and broken below the intended surface rather than being cut cleanly.

**Arris** — The line or sharp edge formed where two surfaces of a piece of timber meet.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. A plane is an accuracy tool, not only a smoothing tool

The basic action of a hand plane is simple: a sharpened iron removes a thin shaving as the sole travels across the timber. Professional planing, however, depends on understanding what the surface is supposed to become. Before beginning, identify whether the task is to remove roughness, flatten a face, straighten an edge, bring an edge square to a reference face, reduce a component toward a marked dimension, trim end grain, or prepare a fine surface for later finishing.

These purposes are different. A surface can feel smooth while still being bowed or twisted. An edge can look straight but be out of square to the reference face. For that reason, the carpenter must inspect with appropriate tools such as a straightedge and square rather than relying only on touch or appearance.

### 2. Selecting an appropriate plane

Different planes are suited to different stages of work. A **jack plane** is a versatile general-purpose bench plane. It can remove high areas and prepare stock before finer truing or smoothing. A **smoothing plane** is normally used later, with a fine cut, to improve the final surface. A longer truing or jointing plane can be useful where a long, straight reference surface is required because its longer sole tends to bridge small hollows while cutting high areas. A **block plane** is compact and is often useful for controlled trimming, chamfering and suitable end-grain work.

The names of planes do not remove the need for judgement. Tool condition, workpiece size, grain, required accuracy and the amount of material to be removed all affect selection. Learners should use the tools supplied and approved by their training centre or workplace and follow the manufacturer's and workplace instructions for those tools.

### 3. Inspect the plane before use

Before planing, check that the tool is suitable and serviceable. Inspect the body and sole for obvious damage. Confirm that handles, knobs, wedges, levers and adjustment parts are secure. The plane iron should be sharp enough for the intended operation and should not have obvious chips or damage that will spoil the cut. The iron must be securely retained.

A plane set too aggressively can be difficult to control and may cause excessive tear-out or remove more material than intended. For controlled preparation, begin conservatively and test the cut on suitable material where appropriate. Observe the shaving and the surface rather than assuming that the adjustment is correct.

A dull iron should not be compensated for by excessive force. Excessive force reduces control and can damage the work. Sharpening and adjustment should be carried out using the method taught and approved for the particular tool.

### 4. Secure and inspect the workpiece

The workpiece must be supported so that it does not slide, tip or rock during planing. Depending on the task, this may involve a vice, bench stop, dogs, clamps or another approved workholding arrangement. The holding method should not place hardware where the plane iron can strike it.

Before cutting, inspect the timber for embedded metal, loose material or other conditions that may damage the cutting edge or create a hazard. Identify the reference face and edge if these have already been established. Also identify the target line or dimension. Planing without a target encourages unnecessary material removal.

### 5. Read the grain and control the cut

Wood fibres have direction. On many straight-grained boards, planing in the favourable direction allows the iron to cut fibres cleanly. Working against rising grain can cause fibres to lift ahead of the cutting edge and produce tear-out. Examine the face and edges of the timber for clues to grain direction and begin with a controlled test pass.

Grain is not always simple. Knots, figured timber and reversing grain may change direction within one component. If the surface begins tearing rather than cutting cleanly, stop and reassess. Possible responses include changing planing direction, taking a finer cut, ensuring the iron is sharp, or using another appropriate surface-preparation method. Do not continue damaging the work merely because one direction worked elsewhere on the board.

### 6. Use balanced planing technique

Stand in a stable position that allows a controlled stroke without overreaching. Keep hands on the intended handles or safe gripping surfaces and away from the cutting edge. At the beginning of a full-length pass, control the front of the plane so the sole registers securely on the work. Through the middle of the stroke, maintain balanced control. Near the end, avoid tipping the front downward beyond the workpiece. Poor pressure control can create low areas near the ends.

Use deliberate overlapping strokes where appropriate. The objective is not to create the largest shaving possible. The objective is to remove only the material required while progressively improving the geometry and surface.

### 7. Flattening a face

Begin by checking the face with a straightedge in several directions. Look for high areas, hollow areas, cup, bow or twist. Marking high areas lightly with pencil can help show where material needs to be removed.

When significant high areas need correction, controlled diagonal or cross-grain passes may be appropriate during early flattening, followed by finer passes that refine the surface. Recheck frequently. A plane cannot replace measurement: every pass changes the workpiece.

Once the face approaches the required flatness, take finer finishing passes suited to the timber and grain. A smoothing plane can then refine remaining tool marks where appropriate. The finished reference face should be checked again before it is used as the datum for later marking.

### 8. Straightening and squaring an edge

To prepare an edge, secure the board so the edge is accessible and stable. Use the verified reference face when checking squareness. Take controlled strokes along the edge, then use a square at several positions rather than checking only one point. Also use a straightedge or other suitable check for straightness along the length.

If one side of the edge is high, correction should be deliberate and small. Repeatedly taking full-width cuts without diagnosing the error may preserve the same out-of-square condition while simply making the board narrower.

When working toward a marked finished width, stop and check as the line is approached. A carpenter can remove more timber later, but cannot put an accidentally planed-away edge back onto the board.

### 9. End grain and edge protection

End grain requires particular control because fibres at the far corner can break away if they are unsupported. A common controlled approach is to work inward from the ends rather than blindly planing through an unsupported far corner. A backing piece may also be appropriate for some tasks. The exact method depends on the workpiece and plane being used.

A plane may also be used to remove or soften a sharp arris where the specification calls for it. This must be intentional. Do not round or chamfer edges that are required to remain sharp for joinery, alignment or appearance.

### 10. Quality control during planing

Inspection should happen throughout the operation, not only after it. Check:
- flatness of faces;
- straightness of edges;
- squareness to the reference face;
- dimensions and marked limits;
- surface condition and tear-out;
- whether the required arrises remain intact;
- whether the workpiece is still suitable for the next operation.

If a serious defect, hidden fastener, unusual timber condition or tool fault is discovered, stop and obtain appropriate guidance rather than improvising a risky correction.

## Worked Example / Demonstration

A learner must prepare one edge of a timber rail so it is straight and square to an already established reference face.

1. The learner confirms which face is the reference face and identifies the finished-width requirement.
2. The rail is secured firmly in the vice with the working edge accessible.
3. The learner inspects the plane, confirms the iron is secure and checks that the cut is appropriately fine.
4. Grain direction is observed and a controlled test pass is made.
5. The learner takes deliberate strokes along the edge rather than repeatedly attacking one small area without checking.
6. After several passes, the learner checks straightness along the edge and uses a square registered against the reference face at the front, middle and rear of the rail.
7. The square reveals that one side remains slightly high. The learner makes a small controlled correction rather than continuing with indiscriminate full-width passes.
8. The edge is rechecked. Once straight, square and within the required dimension, planing stops.

The important professional behaviour is the cycle **plane → check → diagnose → correct → recheck**, not the number of shavings produced.

## Practical Activity — Face and Edge Inspection Plan

Using a training workpiece supplied or approved by your facilitator, prepare an inspection and planing record. If practical tool use is authorised, complete the operation under the required supervision. If not, perform the planning and inspection portion only.

Record:
1. The intended purpose of the planing operation.
2. The plane selected and why it is suitable.
3. The identified reference face or edge.
4. The observed grain direction.
5. The workholding method.
6. Initial defects or high areas found.
7. Checks used for flatness, straightness and squareness.
8. At least two inspection points during the work.
9. The final result and any corrective action still required.

Keep the completed record as portfolio evidence.

## Knowledge Check

### Question 1
A board feels smooth but a straightedge shows a hollow along its face. What is the best conclusion?

A. The face is acceptable because smoothness proves flatness.  
B. The plane iron must always be replaced.  
C. Smoothness and flatness are different quality requirements.  
D. The board should automatically be discarded.

**Correct answer: C**

**Feedback:** A surface can be smooth but geometrically inaccurate. Flatness must be checked using an appropriate reference rather than touch alone.

### Question 2
Why should a carpenter frequently check dimensions while planing toward a finished size?

A. Timber removed by mistake cannot simply be restored.  
B. Checking makes the plane iron sharper.  
C. Dimensions matter only after finishing.  
D. It guarantees that grain direction never changes.

**Correct answer: A**

**Feedback:** Planing is a material-removal process. Frequent checks help prevent the component from becoming undersize.

### Question 3
A plane begins producing severe tear-out in an area of changing grain. What is the best response?

A. Push harder until the fibres disappear.  
B. Stop, reassess grain direction and tool setup, and adjust the method.  
C. Increase the depth of cut immediately.  
D. Ignore the damage because it will always disappear during assembly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Feedback:** Tear-out is a signal to reassess. Grain direction, sharpness, depth of cut and the chosen preparation method may all need attention.

### Question 4
When checking whether a prepared edge is square, what should the square register against?

A. Any convenient rough surface.  
B. The floor.  
C. The verified reference face.  
D. The plane handle.

**Correct answer: C**

**Feedback:** Accurate marking and inspection depend on established references. The square should register against the verified reference face.

## Knowledge Check Feedback / Explanation

The four questions reinforce a professional planing sequence. First establish the required geometry, then select and inspect the tool, secure the work, read the grain, remove material conservatively and inspect repeatedly. Surface appearance alone is not enough. A professional carpenter works from verified references and stops removing material once the required condition is achieved.

## Key Takeaways

1. Hand planes remove controlled shavings and can prepare accurate faces and edges.
2. Plane selection depends on the operation, workpiece and required quality.
3. Inspect the plane, cutting iron, workpiece and workholding before use.
4. Grain direction strongly influences surface quality and tear-out.
5. Use controlled strokes and avoid excessive depth of cut or force.
6. Check flatness, straightness, squareness and dimensions throughout the operation.
7. Work from verified reference faces and edges.
8. Stop removing material when the specification is achieved; unnecessary planing can make a component undersize.
