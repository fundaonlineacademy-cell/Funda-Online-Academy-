# Carpentry — Module 9: Quality Control, Defects & Corrective Practice
## Lesson 3 — Checking Square, Level, Plumb & Alignment

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
1. Explain square, level, plumb and alignment as separate quality conditions.
2. Select suitable checking methods for assemblies and installations.
3. Diagnose common causes of geometric error.
4. Correct setup-related problems without concealing underlying defects.
5. Document geometric inspection results against requirements.

### Why This Lesson Matters
An item can have correct individual component dimensions and still be assembled badly. A cabinet may rack out of square, a frame may lean out of plumb, a shelf may be out of level or several doors may be visibly misaligned. Geometric inspection converts these conditions into controlled checks rather than visual guesswork.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Square** — Condition in which specified adjoining lines or faces form a right angle.
- **Level** — Horizontal condition relative to gravity where required.
- **Plumb** — Vertical condition relative to gravity where required.
- **Alignment** — Correct positional relationship between components, edges, centres or surfaces.
- **Diagonal check** — Comparison of corner-to-corner measurements used as one method of assessing rectangular geometry.
- **Racking** — Distortion of a frame or rectangular assembly away from its intended geometry.
- **Reference line** — Controlled line used to position or compare components.
- **Shim** — Thin packing material used in an approved installation method to adjust position, level, plumb or support.

### Main Lesson Content
Square, level, plumb and alignment are related but not interchangeable. A cabinet carcass can be square yet sit out of level on an uneven floor. A door frame can have a level head but one jamb may be out of plumb. Two shelves can individually be level but misaligned with each other. Quality inspection must therefore identify which condition the specification requires.

To check square on a small component, a reliable square may be used at corners or reference faces. For larger rectangular assemblies, diagonal comparison can provide useful evidence: equal diagonals are consistent with a rectangle when opposite sides and other relevant geometry are controlled. However, a diagonal check should not be treated as magical proof of every dimension. The inspector should combine it with appropriate dimensional and joint checks.

Level and plumb checks depend on gravity and require a suitable level, laser or other approved instrument. Before trusting a level, confirm that it is serviceable. A simple reversal check may reveal a suspicious spirit level: place it, note the indication, reverse its orientation on the same reference surface and compare. Where formal calibration or verification procedures apply, follow them.

Alignment is often assessed against a straightedge, string line, laser, reference mark, adjacent component or specified centreline. Typical examples include cabinet fronts lining up, hinges positioned consistently, rails sharing a common plane, wall units following a controlled datum, and hardware centres matching the drawing.

When an assembly is out of square, investigate the cause before forcing it into position. Possible causes include unequal component lengths, inaccurate joints, debris trapped at joints, incorrect clamp pressure, a distorted work surface, premature fixing, warped material or measurement error. Simply pulling the diagonals equal may not solve a component that was cut incorrectly.

Installation introduces additional conditions. Floors and walls are not always perfectly level, plumb, straight or square. The carpenter must distinguish the building condition from the manufactured item's condition. Approved shimming, scribing or adjustment may be required, but structural support, fixing requirements, manufacturer instructions and project specifications must be respected. Never weaken a fixing or remove excessive material merely to make a visual line appear correct.

Sequence matters. During assembly, check geometry before adhesive cures or permanent fixings prevent adjustment. During installation, establish reference lines and critical levels before fixing multiple components. Intermediate quality checks reduce expensive rework.

Documentation should state what was checked and against what reference. “Cabinet crooked” is ambiguous. “Right jamb out of plumb relative to verified vertical reference; exceeds project acceptance criterion” is much more useful. If exact permitted deviation is not known, record the observation and seek the applicable requirement rather than inventing a limit.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
A base cabinet has correct width and height but its doors show a widening gap toward the top. The carpenter does not immediately adjust the hinges. First, the carcass diagonals and corner squareness are checked. The carcass is square. A level check then shows the installed cabinet is twisted because one corner is unsupported on the floor. Following the approved installation method, support is corrected and the cabinet is rechecked before final door adjustment. This addresses the cause rather than using hinge adjustment to disguise an installation problem.

### Practical Activity
Inspect a rectangular frame, cabinet or training mock-up. Record: corner/square check, two diagonals, level condition, plumb condition where applicable, alignment of at least two components and any corrective action required. Identify the reference used for each check.

### Knowledge Check
1. A cabinet can be square but not level. A. True B. False C. Only outdoors D. Only if painted
2. Unequal diagonals in a rectangular frame should lead to: A. Immediate painting B. Investigation of geometry and component/joint causes C. Ignoring dimensions D. Larger screws only
3. A widening door gap may be caused by: A. Only hinges B. Geometry, installation or hinge adjustment, so the cause must be diagnosed C. Paint colour D. Timber name
4. If a wall is out of plumb, the carpenter should: A. Assume any installation is acceptable B. Follow approved adjustment/fixing methods and applicable requirements C. Remove structural fixings D. Invent a tolerance

### Feedback / Explanation
1. **A.** Square concerns angle/geometry; level concerns horizontal relationship to gravity.
2. **B.** Diagnose before correction.
3. **B.** Visible symptoms can have several causes.
4. **B.** Installation adjustments must remain controlled and safe.

### Key Takeaways
Check square, level, plumb and alignment separately. Establish reliable references, verify instruments, inspect before permanent fixing, distinguish product geometry from site conditions and diagnose causes before adjustment.
