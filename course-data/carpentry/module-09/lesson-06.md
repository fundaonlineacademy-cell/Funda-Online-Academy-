# Carpentry — Module 9: Quality Control, Defects & Corrective Practice
## Lesson 6 — Diagnosing Common Carpentry Defects

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
1. Distinguish a defect symptom from its underlying cause.
2. Diagnose common dimensional, material, joint, installation and finish defects.
3. Use evidence to narrow possible causes.
4. Avoid destructive or unsafe trial-and-error correction.
5. Escalate defects when the cause or authorised remedy is uncertain.

### Why This Lesson Matters
Fixing the wrong cause wastes time and can make a problem worse. A sticking door may result from hinge position, frame geometry, swelling, loose fixings or installation distortion. A gap in a joint may result from inaccurate machining, debris, warp or clamping. Diagnosis is therefore a core professional skill. Current SAQA occupational criteria require workers to identify component and process faults, explain causes and take corrective action.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Symptom** — Observable sign of a problem.
- **Root cause** — Fundamental cause that created or allowed a defect.
- **Contributing factor** — Condition that increases the likelihood or severity of a defect without necessarily being the sole cause.
- **Evidence** — Measurement, observation, record or test result supporting a conclusion.
- **Isolation** — Controlled separation of a suspected variable or non-conforming item for investigation.
- **Recurrence** — Reappearance of a defect after correction or in repeated production.
- **Diagnostic sequence** — Ordered process used to identify likely causes systematically.
- **Verification** — Check confirming whether corrective action achieved the required result.

### Main Lesson Content
Diagnosis begins by describing the symptom precisely. “Door wrong” provides almost no useful information. “Top latch-side clearance is smaller than bottom clearance and door contacts the frame during closing” gives a starting point for investigation. Precise symptoms guide the next check.

Next identify the requirement and establish when the defect appeared. Was the component correct after machining but wrong after assembly? Did the finish defect appear only after the second coat? Did an installation become misaligned after fixing to the wall? The stage at which the problem emerged narrows likely causes.

Collect evidence before altering the work. Measure relevant dimensions, check square/level/plumb, inspect material condition, examine joints and fixings, confirm drawings and review process records. If you immediately plane a sticking door, you may remove correct material and hide an out-of-square frame. If you sand a finish defect before documenting it, you lose evidence about its original pattern.

Use cause categories. **Material causes** include warp, moisture-related movement, defects, damaged board edges or unsuitable material. **Measurement/setup causes** include wrong datum, inaccurate stop, jig movement or incorrect dimensions. **Tool/process causes** include dull cutters, excessive feed, wrong abrasive sequence or poor machine setup. **Assembly causes** include debris, clamp distortion, wrong component orientation or fixing sequence. **Installation causes** include uneven substrates, inadequate support or incorrect reference lines. **Finish causes** include contamination, poor preparation, incompatible products or unsuitable application conditions.

Look for patterns. A single chipped corner may be handling damage. Identical breakout on twenty components points toward machining direction, tool condition, support or process setup. A defect appearing only on one timber batch may indicate material variation or storage conditions. Patterns make diagnosis more efficient.

Test hypotheses safely. If a jig is suspected, verify it against a known reference before changing production parts. If a measuring tool is suspect, compare it through the workplace verification process. If moisture movement is suspected, inspect storage and material condition and use an appropriate moisture assessment where required. Do not perform unsafe machine adjustments or unauthorised destructive tests.

Separate cause from responsibility. Quality diagnosis should focus on what happened and how to prevent recurrence, not on blaming a person without evidence. A worker may have followed an outdated drawing issued through a document-control failure. Corrective action then needs both product correction and process correction.

After corrective work, verify the result against the original acceptance criteria. A repair is not complete merely because work was performed. The item must be re-inspected. Where the defect has broader implications, inspect related items or the process to ensure the same fault has not affected additional work.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
Several shelf panels show burn marks on one machined edge. Instead of sanding every edge and continuing production, the carpenter isolates affected parts and examines the pattern. Marks occur on the same operation and edge. Tool condition, feed, setup and material presentation are checked according to safe procedures. The process fault is corrected by the authorised person, a test component is produced and inspected, and affected panels are dispositioned according to quality requirements.

### Practical Activity
Choose one simulated defect: sticking door, open joint, misaligned shelf, chipped board edge or patchy finish. Prepare a diagnostic sheet with symptom, requirement, when detected, five possible causes, evidence needed for each, safest check sequence, likely corrective route and final verification method.

### Knowledge Check
1. Why describe the symptom before repairing? A. To delay work B. To preserve evidence and guide diagnosis C. To avoid specifications D. To increase waste
2. The same defect on many components often suggests: A. A process or setup issue worth investigating B. Pure coincidence only C. Better quality D. No need to inspect
3. After rework, the item should be: A. Automatically accepted B. Re-inspected against the applicable criteria C. Hidden D. Sold immediately
4. Root-cause work should focus on: A. Blame first B. Evidence and prevention of recurrence C. Appearance only D. Removing records

### Feedback / Explanation
1. **B.** Accurate symptoms direct efficient investigation.
2. **A.** Repetition is valuable evidence of a systematic cause.
3. **B.** Corrective work requires verification.
4. **B.** Effective quality improvement addresses causes and recurrence.

### Key Takeaways
Describe symptoms precisely, preserve evidence, investigate by process stage and cause category, use patterns, test hypotheses safely, avoid blame-based assumptions and always verify corrective action against the original requirement.
