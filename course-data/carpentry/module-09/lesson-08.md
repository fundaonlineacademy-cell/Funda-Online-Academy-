# Carpentry — Module 9: Quality Control, Defects & Corrective Practice
## Lesson 8 — Final Inspection, Documentation & Handover

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
1. Conduct a structured final inspection of carpentry work.
2. Confirm completeness, conformity, function, finish and cleanliness.
3. Compile clear quality and handover records.
4. Identify unresolved items before customer or workplace acceptance.
5. Explain why final inspection does not replace in-process quality control.

### Why This Lesson Matters
Handover is the point at which workmanship becomes a delivered product or completed installation. Missing hardware, damage, poor adjustment, unfinished edges or incomplete documentation discovered after handover can create callbacks and loss of trust. A professional final inspection confirms that work is complete and supported by evidence.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Final inspection** — Last planned conformity check before controlled release or handover.
- **Punch list / snag list** — Recorded list of outstanding defects or incomplete items requiring action before final acceptance where that process is used.
- **Handover** — Formal transfer of completed work, information or responsibility to the authorised recipient.
- **Traceability** — Ability to connect work, materials, inspections, actions or records to the relevant job or item.
- **Release** — Authorised decision permitting work to proceed to delivery, installation, use or customer acceptance.
- **Functional check** — Test confirming that a completed item operates as required.
- **Close-out** — Completion of outstanding work and required records before the job is considered finished.
- **Care information** — Approved instructions given to the recipient concerning use, cleaning, maintenance or protection where applicable.

### Main Lesson Content
Final inspection should be planned rather than performed as a quick glance while loading the vehicle. Begin with the latest approved job information and confirm the scope. Are all required components present? Have approved changes been incorporated? Are outstanding corrective actions closed?

Use a logical inspection route. For a cabinet installation this might include overall position, level/plumb/alignment, dimensions, fixings, joints, doors, drawers, hardware, shelves, surface finish, edges, internal cleanliness and surrounding work area. A repeatable sequence reduces omissions.

Function must be checked. Open and close doors through their intended range. Operate drawers, catches, locks or adjustable hardware as applicable. Confirm that moving components do not bind and that clearances meet the applicable requirement. A product that looks complete but does not operate correctly is not ready for handover.

Inspect finish and protection. Look for scratches, chips, glue residue, pencil marks, coating damage, exposed raw edges where finishing is required and damage caused during installation. Remove temporary protection only according to the project process; sometimes protection must remain until another trade or the customer takes control.

Check completeness of fixings and accessories. Missing shelf pins, caps, handles, screws or brackets are common close-out problems. Confirm quantity and correct installation against the specification or schedule.

Cleanliness is part of professional handover. Remove appropriate debris, dust and packaging, while respecting site waste procedures and any required protective materials. Do not use cleaning chemicals that may damage the finish. Follow product care instructions.

Documentation should match the scale of the job. It may include inspection checklist, measurement record, defect/rework record, approved deviations, photographs, material or hardware information, care instructions and recipient acknowledgement. Records should be accurate and not claim checks that were not performed.

If defects remain, record them clearly. A snag list should identify the item, location, issue, responsible action and status. Do not pressure the recipient to accept incomplete work as complete. Where partial or conditional handover is permitted, follow the authorised process and document outstanding items.

Handover communication matters. Explain relevant operation, adjustment limits, care and maintenance without making unsupported guarantees. If timber movement is possible under changing environmental conditions, provide approved care information rather than promising that natural material will never move.

Final inspection is a safety net, not the entire quality system. Defects are cheaper to correct before adhesive cures, finish is applied or installation is complete. Strong carpentry uses incoming checks, in-process checks and final checks. The final stage confirms that those controls have produced an acceptable result.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
A built-in wardrobe is ready for handover. The carpenter uses the approved checklist: verifies installation geometry, checks every door and drawer, confirms handles and shelf supports, inspects surfaces under good light, checks internal cleanliness and reviews previous snag items. One handle is loose and one protective cap is missing. Both are corrected, the affected areas are rechecked, photographs are taken according to workplace procedure, care instructions are prepared and the completed handover record is signed through the authorised process.

### Practical Activity
Develop a final inspection checklist for a small cabinet or shelving installation with at least 20 checkpoints grouped under: scope/completeness, dimensions/geometry, joints/fixings, function, finish, cleanliness/protection and documentation. Apply it to a sample and create a short snag list for any findings.

### Knowledge Check
1. Final inspection should use: A. Memory only B. Current approved requirements and a structured checklist C. Customer mood D. Production speed
2. A visually attractive drawer that binds is: A. Ready for handover B. Not conforming to required function C. Automatically repaired D. Acceptable without testing
3. A snag list records: A. Outstanding defects or incomplete items B. Only material prices C. Staff holidays D. Tool brands
4. Final inspection replaces in-process checks. A. True B. False C. Only on small jobs D. Only for cabinets

### Feedback / Explanation
1. **B.** Controlled requirements and sequence make inspection reliable.
2. **B.** Function is part of quality.
3. **A.** Snagging supports controlled close-out.
4. **B.** Quality should be controlled throughout production and installation.

### Key Takeaways
Final inspection confirms scope, geometry, joints, function, finish, completeness and cleanliness. Use accurate records, close outstanding defects, communicate approved care information and never treat final inspection as a substitute for earlier quality control.
