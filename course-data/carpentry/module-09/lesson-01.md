# Carpentry — Module 9: Quality Control, Defects & Corrective Practice
## Lesson 1 — Specifications & Acceptance Criteria

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. Explain the purpose of specifications and acceptance criteria in carpentry quality control.
2. Identify the controlled information against which completed work must be inspected.
3. Distinguish conformity, non-conformity, tolerance and workmanship preference.
4. Apply a systematic acceptance check to a simple carpentry component.
5. Record and report quality findings accurately without changing requirements without authority.

### Why This Lesson Matters
A carpenter cannot judge quality only by asking whether an item “looks good.” Professional work is accepted because it satisfies defined requirements. Those requirements may come from drawings, dimensions, schedules, approved samples, manufacturer instructions, project specifications, workplace procedures or client-approved details. Quality control protects the customer, the employer and the carpenter because it replaces guesswork with evidence. SAQA occupational material similarly requires inspection of wood products against specifications, tolerances and quality requirements, including identification of faults and corrective action.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Specification** — Controlled information stating what a product, component, material or process is required to achieve.
- **Acceptance criterion** — A measurable or observable condition used to decide whether work may be accepted.
- **Conformity** — A condition in which the inspected work meets the applicable requirement.
- **Non-conformity** — A condition in which work fails to meet a specified requirement.
- **Tolerance** — The permitted variation from a specified target dimension or condition.
- **Inspection** — A deliberate examination, measurement or check used to determine whether requirements have been met.
- **Reference document** — An approved drawing, cutting list, schedule, instruction or other controlled source used during inspection.
- **Disposition** — The authorised decision about non-conforming work, such as rework, repair, rejection or approved acceptance under a formal process.

### Main Lesson Content
#### 1. Quality begins with a defined requirement
Inspection has little value if the inspector does not know what the work was supposed to be. Before checking a component, identify the applicable job information. For a cabinet side, for example, the relevant information may include material type and thickness, finished length and width, edge treatment, hole positions, face orientation and finish requirement. A door installation may additionally require clearances, alignment, hardware position, operating condition and an approved appearance.

The carpenter should work from the current approved information. An old sketch, verbal memory or assumption is not a reliable substitute where controlled job information exists. If two documents conflict, the correct action is to stop the affected decision and obtain clarification from the authorised person rather than choosing the requirement that is easiest to meet.

#### 2. Acceptance criteria turn requirements into inspection decisions
A specification can contain many requirements. Acceptance criteria identify what must be observed or measured to determine whether those requirements have been achieved. Some criteria are numerical: length, width, diagonal difference, hole position or permitted tolerance. Others are observable: correct material, specified face orientation, absence of unacceptable surface damage, complete fixing, correct hardware or acceptable finish.

Not every visual difference is automatically a defect. Natural timber varies in colour and grain, while some projects intentionally allow such variation. The inspector must therefore distinguish personal preference from an actual requirement. “I would have made it darker” is not a valid rejection reason if colour satisfies the approved finish requirement. Conversely, a component cannot be accepted merely because it looks attractive if its dimensions or material are wrong.

#### 3. Understand tolerances correctly
A target dimension and a tolerance are not the same thing. If a controlled specification states a nominal dimension and a permitted variation, the finished component must fall inside that permitted range. The carpenter must never invent a universal tolerance for every job. Appropriate tolerances depend on the drawing, product, function, joint, manufacturing process, applicable standard and workplace or project requirements.

Tolerance also does not mean careless workmanship. A component near one permitted limit may still be conforming, but repeated drift toward a limit can warn that a machine setup, measuring method or production process should be checked before non-conforming work is produced.

#### 4. Use the right inspection method
Inspection should match the characteristic being checked. A tape or steel rule may verify dimensions; a square may check right angles; a straightedge may reveal bowing or surface irregularity; a level or plumb instrument may be appropriate during installation; visual and tactile inspection may identify scratches, tear-out, chipped edges or finish defects. Measuring equipment must itself be suitable and serviceable.

Do not use an imprecise method for a requirement demanding greater accuracy. Equally, avoid pretending that a measurement is more precise than the tool and method can support.

#### 5. Inspect in a repeatable sequence
A useful sequence is:
1. Confirm the item and current specification.
2. Identify critical acceptance criteria.
3. Check material and component identity.
4. Measure critical dimensions and geometry.
5. Inspect joints, fixings, surfaces and finish as applicable.
6. Check function where the item moves or interfaces with another component.
7. Compare findings with the specified requirement and tolerance.
8. Record the result.
9. Separate or identify non-conforming work where workplace controls require it.
10. Report the problem and follow the authorised corrective process.

This sequence prevents the common mistake of correcting a visible problem before determining its cause or recording the original condition.

#### 6. Conforming work, rework and unauthorised concessions
When work conforms, it can proceed according to the workplace process. When it does not, the carpenter should not quietly alter the specification, hide the fault or decide that the deviation is “close enough.” Depending on authority and procedure, the item may require rework, repair, replacement, formal concession or rejection. Any acceptance of a deviation must come through the authorised process, not from the learner inventing a new standard.

#### 7. Documentation protects quality
A useful inspection record identifies the job or component, requirement checked, measurement or observation, result, defect location where relevant, action taken, date and responsible person. Clear records allow recurring faults to be traced. If five components are repeatedly undersize, the problem may be a setup, stop, measurement or process issue rather than five unrelated mistakes.

Quality records should be factual. Write “left stile 2 mm below specified minimum” where that is the verified finding, rather than vague language such as “door bad.” Good records support corrective action and professional handover.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
A learner inspects a small wall cabinet before finishing. The current drawing specifies the material, overall dimensions, shelf positions and door arrangement. The learner first confirms the drawing revision, then checks component identity and material. Overall width and height are measured, diagonals are compared to assess squareness, shelf positions are checked against the drawing, joints are inspected for gaps and damage, and the door is temporarily fitted to check operation. One shelf is found outside its specified position. The learner records the measured position, identifies the item as non-conforming and reports it for authorised corrective action instead of changing another component to disguise the error.

### Practical Activity
Create a **Carpentry Acceptance Inspection Record** for a simple item available in your training environment. Include at least six criteria: material, one overall dimension, one internal or component dimension, geometry, joint/fixing condition and surface/finish condition. For each criterion record the source requirement, inspection method, finding and result as Conforming, Non-conforming or Clarification Required. Do not invent missing tolerances; mark them for clarification.

### Knowledge Check
1. What should determine whether carpentry work is accepted?
   A. The carpenter's personal preference
   B. The applicable specification and acceptance criteria
   C. The amount of material already used
   D. How quickly the item was completed

2. Two current-looking job documents give different dimensions. What is the best response?
   A. Use the larger dimension
   B. Choose whichever saves material
   C. Clarify which controlled requirement applies before proceeding
   D. Average the two dimensions

3. A component looks attractive but is outside the specified dimensional tolerance. It is:
   A. Automatically acceptable
   B. Non-conforming unless handled through an authorised disposition process
   C. Acceptable if the customer has not seen it
   D. Conforming because appearance is more important

4. Why are inspection records useful?
   A. They replace measurements
   B. They provide evidence and help trace recurring quality problems
   C. They allow specifications to be changed informally
   D. They remove the need for corrective action

### Feedback / Explanation
1. **B.** Acceptance must be based on applicable requirements, not preference.
2. **C.** Conflicting controlled information must be resolved by an authorised source.
3. **B.** Attractive appearance does not cancel a dimensional non-conformity.
4. **B.** Records provide traceability and support corrective action and process improvement.

### Key Takeaways
- Quality inspection starts with the current approved requirement.
- Acceptance criteria may be dimensional, functional, material-related or visual.
- Tolerances must come from applicable requirements; do not invent universal values.
- Personal preference is not the same as a specification.
- Use an inspection method suitable for the characteristic being checked.
- Record non-conformities factually and traceably.
- Do not hide faults or alter requirements without authority.
- Good inspection data helps prevent repeated defects.
