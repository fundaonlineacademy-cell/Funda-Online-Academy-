# Carpentry — Module 9: Quality Control, Defects & Corrective Practice
## Lesson 5 — Surface & Finish Inspection

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
1. Inspect prepared and finished timber surfaces under suitable conditions.
2. Identify common surface and coating defects.
3. Relate visible defects to likely preparation, application or environmental causes.
4. Separate acceptable natural variation from non-conforming workmanship.
5. Record finish findings and select an authorised corrective route.

### Why This Lesson Matters
Surface quality strongly influences customer perception, but professional inspection is more than looking for shine. Scratches, sanding marks, contamination, runs, uneven colour, exposed filler, damaged edges and incomplete coverage can indicate failures in preparation or application. SAQA occupational criteria specifically recognise smoothness, scratches, burn marks, paint contamination, overspray, runs, wrong mixing and viscosity as quality faults requiring identification and corrective action.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Surface preparation** — Processes used to bring a substrate to the condition required before finishing.
- **Defect** — Feature that fails an applicable quality requirement.
- **Contamination** — Unwanted material such as dust, oil, silicone, moisture or debris that interferes with finishing.
- **Run** — Downward flow of excessive wet coating producing a raised trail or curtain.
- **Orange peel** — Textured coating appearance resembling citrus peel, associated with application and flow conditions.
- **Blushing** — Cloudy or whitish appearance that can occur in certain finish systems under unsuitable conditions.
- **Adhesion** — Ability of a coating to remain bonded to the prepared substrate or previous coat.
- **Cure** — Development of the finish toward its specified final properties; not necessarily the same as being touch-dry.

### Main Lesson Content
Finish inspection should be performed under suitable lighting and after the coating has reached the inspection stage specified by its manufacturer or job process. Raking light—light directed across a surface at a shallow angle—can reveal scratches, dents, ripples and sanding marks that are difficult to see under flat illumination. The inspector should also view the item from normal use positions because appearance requirements can depend on how the finished product is seen.

Before blaming the coating, inspect the substrate. A finish can magnify preparation defects. Cross-grain scratches, torn grain, glue residue, poorly feathered filler and sharp damaged edges may remain visible after coating. Corrective work applied only to the topcoat may fail if the real problem lies underneath.

Colour and sheen must be judged against the approved requirement or sample where one exists. Timber is naturally variable, and stains can respond differently to grain, density, end grain and previous preparation. The question is not whether every piece is biologically identical but whether the completed appearance satisfies the agreed standard. Where a controlled sample is used, ensure it represents the approved system and viewing conditions.

Common application defects include runs and sags from excessive wet film or unsuitable application; dry or rough spray from poor atomisation or application conditions; dust nibs from contamination; patchy colour from inconsistent preparation or application; visible lap marks; pinholes; missed edges; overspray; and poor adhesion. Causes vary by product, equipment, substrate and environment, so diagnosis should not be reduced to one universal explanation.

Environmental conditions matter. Temperature, humidity, ventilation, dust and airflow can influence application and curing. The carpenter must follow the product technical information and workplace controls rather than inventing universal drying times. “Touch dry” does not always mean ready for sanding, recoating, assembly, packing or service.

Inspect edges, corners and hidden transitions. Finish can become thin on sharp edges, accumulate in corners or be missed underneath shelves and rails. Hardware interfaces may also be contaminated by finish, affecting movement or future maintenance.

When a defect is found, classify its significance. Is it purely visual within or outside the agreed appearance standard? Does it expose timber to service conditions? Does it interfere with fit or movement? Does it indicate coating adhesion failure? The answer determines urgency and corrective route.

Corrective action may involve cleaning, de-nibbing, sanding, recoating, stripping, local repair or complete refinishing, depending on the system and defect. Follow manufacturer and workplace procedures. Never apply an incompatible coating simply because it hides the defect temporarily.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
A stained cabinet door appears patchy. The learner checks under consistent light and compares it with the approved sample. The patchiness follows areas where glue squeeze-out had previously been wiped across the surface. Rather than adding progressively darker stain, the learner records the defect and identifies inadequate glue removal/surface preparation as the likely cause. Corrective refinishing is then carried out according to the approved finish system.

### Practical Activity
Inspect a finished sample under normal and raking light. Record at least eight inspection zones: centre field, two edges, two corners, end grain, joint area and underside/return. Note colour, sheen, scratches, contamination, coverage and damage. Classify each finding and identify likely process stage responsible.

### Knowledge Check
1. Why use raking light? A. To dry paint B. To reveal surface irregularities C. To measure length D. To change colour
2. Patchiness that follows old glue residue most likely requires investigation of: A. Surface preparation B. Door hinges C. Shelf spacing D. Packaging only
3. Touch-dry always means fully cured. A. True B. False C. Only for varnish D. Only in summer
4. A finish defect should be corrected using: A. Any available coating B. The approved compatible system and procedure C. More solvent without checking D. A universal drying time

### Feedback / Explanation
1. **B.** Shallow-angle light makes many surface defects easier to see.
2. **A.** Coatings often reveal underlying preparation problems.
3. **B.** Drying and curing stages differ by product.
4. **B.** Compatibility and manufacturer/workplace instructions matter.

### Key Takeaways
Inspect substrate and coating, use suitable lighting, compare with controlled requirements, investigate causes before repair, respect product-specific drying/curing instructions and inspect edges and hidden areas as well as obvious faces.
