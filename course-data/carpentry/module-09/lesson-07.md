# Carpentry — Module 9: Quality Control, Defects & Corrective Practice
## Lesson 7 — Corrective Action, Rework & Waste Prevention

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
1. Distinguish correction, rework, repair, rejection and preventive process action.
2. Select a controlled response to non-conforming carpentry work.
3. Prevent corrective work from creating new safety or quality problems.
4. Explain how root-cause action reduces waste and recurrence.
5. Verify and document completed corrective action.

### Why This Lesson Matters
Not every defect should be repaired in the same way, and not every defective component should be saved. Professional judgement balances specification, safety, function, appearance, time, material value and authority. Quality systems require non-conforming work to be identified, controlled, corrected where appropriate and re-inspected. SAQA quality-control material similarly requires defects/non-conformances to be contained and documented and repaired components to be re-inspected before acceptance or rejection.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Correction** — Action taken to remove a detected non-conformity or its immediate effect.
- **Rework** — Controlled processing intended to bring non-conforming work fully into conformity with original requirements.
- **Repair** — Controlled action restoring acceptable function or condition, which may require specific approval where it does not simply reproduce the original requirement.
- **Reject** — Decision that an item is not acceptable for the intended controlled use.
- **Containment** — Immediate action preventing known or suspected non-conforming work from moving forward unnoticed.
- **Corrective action** — Action addressing the cause of a non-conformity to reduce recurrence.
- **Verification** — Confirmation that the action produced the required result.
- **Waste prevention** — Reduction of avoidable material, labour, energy and time losses without lowering required quality or safety.

### Main Lesson Content
When a defect is detected, first contain it. Mark, segregate or otherwise control the affected item according to workplace procedure so it is not accidentally installed, packed or delivered. If the defect may affect a batch, identify the potentially affected range rather than treating only the first item found.

Next determine the authorised disposition. Rework is appropriate where the item can be brought fully back to the original requirement without unacceptable consequences. For example, an oversized component may sometimes be re-machined to its specified size if sufficient material remains and all other requirements can still be met. An undersize component generally cannot be restored simply by further cutting. Any repair method must be compatible with the job specification and approval process.

Avoid “saving” material at the expense of safety or performance. A cracked structural or load-relevant component cannot be patched casually. A fixing hole enlarged beyond the hardware requirement should not automatically receive a larger screw. A board edge damaged at a critical connector position may require replacement. The applicable specification and authorised technical decision determine the outcome.

Corrective work can create secondary defects. Re-sanding a panel may change thickness or flatten an edge. Re-drilling can weaken surrounding material. Removing a fastener can damage the substrate. Stripping a finish can affect veneer thickness or adhesive. Before rework, plan the sequence and identify what must be rechecked afterwards.

Corrective action goes beyond repairing the item. If repeated shelves are short because a stop moved, fixing each shelf does not prevent recurrence. The stop, setup method, inspection frequency or work instruction must be addressed through the appropriate workplace process. This is where quality control becomes waste prevention.

Waste has several forms: scrapped timber or board, wasted adhesive and finish, replacement hardware, machine time, labour, delayed installation, transport, customer complaints and reputational damage. Preventing the first defect is usually more economical than reworking finished work. Early in-process checks therefore have high value.

However, waste reduction must never become a reason to accept non-conforming work. “We have already spent too much on it” is not an acceptance criterion. Quality decisions should remain independent of pressure to meet production numbers where requirements are not satisfied.

Document the original defect, authorised action, person responsible where required, completed rework/repair and re-inspection result. If a concession or deviation is formally approved, retain the required approval evidence. Never erase the history simply because the item later passed.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
A set of cabinet doors has hinge cups drilled 3 mm from the specified position because a jig stop moved. The affected doors are contained. The team verifies which serial/batch range was produced after the last good check. The authorised person determines which doors can be safely reworked without violating hardware or appearance requirements and which must be remade. The jig is corrected and verified before production resumes. Reworked doors are re-inspected for hinge position, operation and surface condition. The event is recorded so the process control can be improved.

### Practical Activity
For four defects—oversize rail, undersize rail, chipped decorative edge and mispositioned hinge hole—prepare a disposition worksheet. For each state what additional information is needed, whether rework might be possible, risks introduced by correction, who may need to authorise the decision and what must be re-inspected.

### Knowledge Check
1. Containment is intended to: A. Hide defects B. Prevent affected work moving forward unnoticed C. Increase production D. Replace inspection
2. Rework is complete when: A. Labour stops B. The item is verified against requirements C. Filler is applied D. A supervisor leaves
3. Waste reduction allows specifications to be ignored. A. True B. False C. Only for timber D. Only on Fridays
4. Repeated defects require attention to: A. Only individual parts B. Underlying process cause as well as affected items C. Paint colour D. Customer age

### Feedback / Explanation
1. **B.** Containment protects downstream work and customers.
2. **B.** Verification demonstrates that the result conforms.
3. **B.** Efficiency never overrides required quality or safety.
4. **B.** Process correction prevents recurrence.

### Key Takeaways
Contain non-conforming work, use authorised disposition, understand the difference between rework and repair, plan for secondary risks, re-inspect corrected work and address process causes to reduce future waste.
