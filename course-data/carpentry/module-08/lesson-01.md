# Carpentry Fundamentals — Module 8, Lesson 1
## Surface Assessment & Preparation

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to assess a timber surface before finishing; identify common contamination, damage and preparation defects; select an appropriate preparation sequence; distinguish preparation from coating application; and decide when a surface should be repaired, cleaned, sanded or referred before finishing.

### Why This Lesson Matters
A finish cannot reliably correct poor preparation. Professional carpentry requires the worker to inspect the substrate first, determine what prevents a sound finish, and prepare it without damaging dimensions, edges or important features. Good preparation improves appearance, coating performance, productivity and customer confidence while reducing rework and wasted material.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
**Substrate** — The underlying timber or board surface that will receive a finishing system.

**Contamination** — Dust, grease, adhesive residue, moisture, wax or another unwanted substance that can interfere with preparation or coating adhesion.

**Surface defect** — An unwanted condition such as a dent, scratch, torn grain, glue residue, hole or damaged edge that may affect the finished result.

**Preparation** — The controlled work performed before final finishing, such as inspection, cleaning, repairing and sanding.

**Adhesion** — The ability of a coating or finishing material to remain bonded to the prepared surface.

**Compatibility** — The suitability of preparation materials, fillers and coatings for use together on the specified substrate.

**Reference surface** — A face or edge whose accuracy must be protected because later dimensions, fitting or alignment depend on it.

### Main Lesson Content
#### 1. Inspect before touching the surface
Surface preparation begins with observation, not sanding. Confirm the job specification, required finish, material type and condition. Examine faces, edges, corners, joints and repaired areas under useful lighting. Look for machining marks, scratches, torn grain, dents, raised fibres, glue squeeze-out, nail or screw holes, filler, dust, oil, wax, moisture damage and board-edge damage. A worker who immediately sands may hide evidence, round an edge or remove material that should have been preserved.

#### 2. Decide what the final finish requires
Preparation depends on the intended finish. An opaque paint system can visually cover some colour variation, while a transparent stain or clear finish may reveal scratches, filler mismatch, glue contamination and uneven sanding. This does not mean paint allows careless preparation: defects can still telegraph through coatings and poor adhesion can still occur. The product specification and required quality standard therefore guide the preparation sequence.

#### 3. Remove contamination appropriately
Loose dust should be removed using a method appropriate to the workplace and finishing system. Adhesive residue should be addressed before it becomes an obvious finishing defect. Grease, wax or incompatible residues require an approved cleaning method rather than simply sanding contamination deeper into the surface. If the correct cleaning method is uncertain, consult the product instructions or supervisor. Do not improvise with an unknown solvent.

#### 4. Repair defects without damaging geometry
Holes, small depressions or local defects may require a compatible filler or repair process. The repair should be allowed to reach the condition required by its manufacturer before further preparation. Avoid overfilling excessively and then removing unnecessary surrounding material. On components that must fit accurately, preserve reference edges, rebates, joint shoulders and dimensions.

#### 5. Sand only after the surface is understood
Sanding is a controlled cutting process using abrasive particles. It should remove preparation marks progressively rather than reshape the work unintentionally. Sand with appropriate control, normally respecting grain direction on solid timber where the process requires it. Do not assume that the finest possible abrasive automatically gives the best coating result; the finishing-product instructions may specify the appropriate final preparation.

#### 6. Clean and inspect again
After preparation, remove sanding dust and inspect the surface again. Check it visually and, where safe, by touch. Use raking or side lighting to reveal scratches and unevenness. Check edges and corners for accidental rounding. Confirm that repairs are flush, glue contamination has been removed and the surface is dry and suitable for the next stage.

#### 7. Stop when the defect exceeds your authority
A severe split, unstable joint, delamination, significant moisture damage, incorrect material or dimensional defect is not automatically a finishing problem. Finishing over a structural or manufacturing defect can conceal rather than solve it. Isolate or report the item according to workplace procedure and obtain a decision before continuing.

South African vocational references treat surface preparation as a distinct competence and include sanding, scraping and preparing timber before treatments and final finishes. The principle is consistent: identify the surface condition, remove or repair imperfections appropriately, use suitable tools and materials, and maintain safe work practices.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
A cabinet door is scheduled for a clear finish. Inspection under side lighting shows glue residue beside one joint, a shallow pencil line, two cross-grain scratches and a small dent. The worker does not immediately apply stain. The glue is removed using the approved process, the dent is assessed and repaired appropriately, and the scratches are progressively prepared without rounding the door edge. Dust is removed and the surface is reinspected before finishing. This prevents the transparent finish from making the defects more visible.

### Practical Activity
Create a **Surface Assessment Record** for one timber or board sample. Record: material; intended finish; visible defects; contamination; repairs required; areas whose dimensions must be protected; proposed preparation sequence; final inspection result; and any issue that must be referred. Photograph or sketch the sample before and after preparation if permitted.

### Knowledge Check
1. Why should inspection happen before sanding? A) Sanding always damages wood B) Inspection determines what preparation is actually required C) All surfaces require the same sanding D) Inspection replaces finishing. **Answer: B.**
2. Which issue should normally be resolved before a transparent finish? A) Glue contamination B) Correct grain appearance C) Accurate dimensions D) A clean surface. **Answer: A.**
3. A serious delamination is found in a board component. What is the best action? A) Hide it with filler B) Apply extra varnish C) Report/isolate it for an appropriate decision D) Sand until it disappears. **Answer: C.**
4. Why is a final inspection after sanding important? A) To confirm defects and dust have been controlled without damaging the component B) To change the drawing C) To increase timber moisture D) To avoid all cleaning. **Answer: A.**

### Feedback / Explanation
Preparation is diagnostic and controlled. The worker must understand the defect before choosing the process. Transparent finishes often reveal contamination and scratches clearly. Structural or material failures require appropriate referral rather than cosmetic concealment. Final inspection confirms readiness for the next finishing stage.

### Key Takeaways
- Inspect before preparing.
- Match preparation to the substrate, defect and specified finish.
- Remove contamination using an approved compatible method.
- Protect dimensions, reference edges and joint details.
- Repair defects rather than merely hiding them.
- Sand as a controlled process, not an automatic first step.
- Clean and reinspect before coating.
- Refer defects that exceed finishing scope or authority.
