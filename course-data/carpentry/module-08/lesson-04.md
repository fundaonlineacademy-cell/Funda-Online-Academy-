# Carpentry Fundamentals — Module 8, Lesson 4
## Stains, Dyes & Colour Preparation

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
Learners should be able to explain the purpose of colour preparation on timber; distinguish colouring from protective finishing; identify factors that cause uneven colour; prepare samples before committing to a final colour; and follow specification and manufacturer requirements when applying a colouring system.

### Why This Lesson Matters
Colour can dramatically change a timber product, but it can also expose preparation errors. A stain is not automatically a protective topcoat, and the same product can appear different on different timber, grain, sanding and repair areas. Professional work uses samples, controlled preparation and approved product systems.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
**Stain** — A colouring product used to alter or enhance the appearance of timber; its protective properties depend on the specific product system.

**Dye** — A colouring material that colours the substrate through dissolved colourants; behaviour varies by product chemistry.

**Colour sample** — A representative test area or sample piece used to evaluate the appearance before finishing the final work.

**Absorption** — The uptake of liquid into a substrate, which can vary with grain, density, sanding and repairs.

**Blotching** — Uneven colour caused by non-uniform absorption or application.

**End grain** — Timber surface cut across fibres, often absorbing colouring products differently from long grain.

**Compatibility** — Suitability of the colour product, substrate and subsequent finish for use together.

### Main Lesson Content
#### 1. Colouring and protection are different decisions
Stains and dyes primarily modify colour and appearance. Some commercial products combine colouring with sealing or protective functions, but this must be verified from the product information. Do not assume every stain is a complete protective coating. The job specification should identify the required appearance and protective system.

#### 2. Timber is not visually uniform
Natural timber varies in grain, density, colour and absorption. End grain can absorb more strongly than adjacent long grain. Sapwood and heartwood can differ. Repairs and filler can react differently. Sanding differences can change uptake. This is why a colour chosen from a small printed chart may not exactly match the finished component.

#### 3. Preparation controls colour quality
Glue residue can block stain penetration and produce pale patches. Deep scratches may become darker and more obvious. Uneven sanding can produce uneven colour. Dust can contaminate the application. Complete the approved surface preparation before colour work begins.

#### 4. Use representative samples
Whenever practical and required, prepare a sample from the same or representative material using the intended sanding and finishing sequence. Apply the product according to instructions and view the result after the required drying stage. If a topcoat will change the appearance, the approval sample should include the relevant system stages. Obtain client/supervisor approval where required before proceeding.

#### 5. Mix and apply consistently
Some products require stirring, shaking, dilution or a specific application tool; follow the manufacturer instructions. Maintain consistent application and wiping technique where specified. Work in manageable areas to avoid lap marks. Keep records of product name, batch or colour code where workplace procedures require traceability.

#### 6. Control end grain and repaired areas
End grain and porous areas may darken strongly. Product-specific preparation techniques may be required. Do not invent a universal pre-treatment. Test the system. Filled areas may not accept colour like timber; if the finish is transparent, repair colour compatibility must be evaluated before the final application.

#### 7. Allow the colouring stage to become ready
A coloured surface must reach the condition required before the next coat. Applying an incompatible or premature topcoat can disturb colour or affect adhesion. Follow stated drying/recoat conditions, including environmental limits.

#### 8. Work safely
Colour products can contain different solvents, binders and hazardous ingredients. Read labels and safety information, provide specified ventilation and controls, avoid ignition sources where applicable, and use required PPE. Never identify a product as safe merely because it has little odour.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
A client requests a medium-brown appearance on pine. The carpenter prepares three representative offcuts using the same sanding method planned for the job. A small amount of glue is discovered on one sample and removed before testing. The specified stain is applied according to instructions, followed by the intended clear system on the approval samples. The client-approved sample is recorded before the full project is coloured, reducing disagreement and rework.

### Practical Activity
Create a **Colour Preparation Test Board** using an approved timber sample. Mark zones for long grain, end grain and a compatible repaired area. Prepare all zones consistently, apply the approved colour product according to its instructions, and record differences in absorption and appearance. Do not generalise the result to all timber species or products.

### Knowledge Check
1. Is every timber stain a complete protective finish? A) Yes B) No; verify the specific product/system C) Only on softwood D) Only indoors. **Answer: B.**
2. Why is a representative colour sample useful? A) Timber and preparation can alter the final appearance B) It replaces specifications C) It prevents drying D) It changes species. **Answer: A.**
3. What can glue residue cause during staining? A) Guaranteed darker colour B) Uneven or blocked colour uptake C) Stronger timber D) Faster curing. **Answer: B.**
4. When should a subsequent coating be applied? A) Immediately in every case B) When the colouring product/system instructions permit C) Before surface preparation D) While dust remains. **Answer: B.**

### Feedback / Explanation
Colour finishing is sensitive to material variation and preparation. Sampling helps verify appearance. The colouring product and protective coating must be treated as a compatible system, with manufacturer requirements controlling application and readiness.

### Key Takeaways
- Colouring and protection are not automatically the same function.
- Timber absorption varies naturally.
- Preparation defects can become more visible after staining.
- Use representative samples where appropriate.
- Apply consistently and follow product instructions.
- Treat end grain and repaired areas cautiously.
- Respect drying/recoat requirements.
- Use appropriate chemical-safety controls.
