# Carpentry Fundamentals — Module 8, Lesson 2
## Abrasives & Correct Sanding Sequences

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
Learners should be able to explain abrasive grit and cutting action; select abrasives for the task; plan a progressive sanding sequence; sand solid timber and board surfaces without avoidable damage; and inspect a surface before progressing to finishing.

### Why This Lesson Matters
Sanding strongly affects the appearance and quality of finished carpentry. Poor technique can create deep scratches, rounded edges, uneven faces, swirl marks and unnecessary dust. Correct abrasive selection and progression reduce rework and preserve component accuracy.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
**Abrasive** — A material containing hard particles that cut or wear a surface in a controlled way.

**Grit** — A designation indicating abrasive particle size; lower grit numbers are generally coarser and higher numbers finer within a given abrasive system.

**Scratch pattern** — The marks left by abrasive particles on the prepared surface.

**Progression** — Moving through suitable abrasive stages so deeper marks are replaced by progressively finer preparation marks.

**Loading** — Build-up of dust or finish residue on an abrasive, reducing cutting effectiveness and potentially marking the surface.

**Swirl mark** — A curved or circular sanding mark commonly associated with poor powered-sander technique, contamination or an unsuitable abrasive stage.

**Grain direction** — The predominant orientation of wood fibres, important to many hand-sanding operations and final appearance.

### Main Lesson Content
#### 1. Sanding is material removal
Abrasive particles act like many small cutting edges. Coarse abrasives remove material quickly but leave deeper scratches. Finer abrasives remove less material and create a finer scratch pattern. Therefore sanding must be planned according to the starting condition and required finish rather than by choosing one grit for every job.

#### 2. Begin only as coarse as necessary
If machining marks are deep, a relatively coarse starting abrasive may be justified. If a surface is already smooth, beginning too coarse creates scratches that must later be removed. The correct starting point is the least aggressive abrasive that efficiently corrects the existing condition while preserving geometry. Manufacturer and workplace recommendations take priority where specified.

#### 3. Use a logical progression
Each stage should remove the significant scratches from the previous stage. Skipping too far between abrasive grades can leave deeper marks that remain visible after finishing. There is no universal grit sequence for every timber, board and coating system. The sequence depends on material, previous machining, abrasive type, sanding method and finish specification. Follow product/process requirements rather than memorising a single sequence.

#### 4. Protect geometry
A flat component should remain flat. Edges that must remain square should not be casually rounded. Use an appropriate sanding block or controlled machine technique where needed. Apply even pressure and avoid concentrating force at corners. Do not use sanding to compensate for major dimensional errors that should be corrected by the appropriate machining or fitting process.

#### 5. Work with timber characteristics
On solid timber, final hand sanding is commonly controlled with the grain to reduce conspicuous cross-grain scratches. End grain may absorb finish differently and may require particular preparation according to the finishing system. Veneered boards require especially careful sanding because the decorative face may be thin; aggressive sanding can cut through it. MDF and other engineered boards generate fine dust and require appropriate dust control and PPE according to the task and workplace controls.

#### 6. Powered sanding requires control
A powered sander must be suitable, maintained and used according to manufacturer instructions. Inspect abrasive attachment, guards/features, lead or battery condition and dust extraction before use. Keep the machine controlled and avoid lingering in one area. Allow the abrasive to cut instead of forcing excessive pressure. Isolate the machine appropriately before changing abrasives or performing adjustments.

#### 7. Manage dust
Sanding creates airborne and settled dust. Use extraction or other specified engineering controls where provided, maintain housekeeping, and wear task-appropriate PPE under the workplace risk controls. Dust must also be removed before coating because trapped dust can spoil the finish.

#### 8. Inspect between stages
Remove loose dust and examine the surface in useful light. Pencil witness marks can sometimes assist training, provided they are appropriate to the surface and completely removed. Do not advance merely because a set amount of time has passed. Advance when the previous defect/scratch pattern has been adequately removed and the surface remains geometrically correct.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
A pine shelf leaves the planer with light machining marks. The worker inspects it and chooses a suitable starting abrasive rather than automatically using a very coarse grit. A sanding block is used to preserve the face and edge geometry. The worker progresses through appropriate finer stages, inspecting after each. Final strokes are controlled with the grain. Dust is removed and side lighting confirms that no deep cross-grain scratches remain.

### Practical Activity
Prepare two identical scrap pieces. On Sample A, use a planned progressive sequence. On Sample B, intentionally skip a major intermediate stage under instructor supervision. Clean both and inspect under side lighting. Record differences in scratch visibility, time, surface flatness and likely finishing quality. Do not coat until the instructor confirms both samples are safe and suitable.

### Knowledge Check
1. Why should a worker avoid starting with an unnecessarily coarse abrasive? A) It creates deeper scratches and unnecessary material removal B) It prevents all dust C) It makes wood waterproof D) It changes softwood into hardwood. **Answer: A.**
2. What determines the correct sanding sequence? A) One universal grit chart only B) Material, initial condition, process and finish requirements C) The worker's favourite abrasive D) Colour only. **Answer: B.**
3. Why is aggressive sanding risky on veneer? A) Veneer cannot be finished B) The decorative layer may be sanded through C) Veneer has no grain D) Sandpaper cannot cut it. **Answer: B.**
4. When should the worker move to the next abrasive stage? A) Automatically every two minutes B) When the relevant previous scratches/defects have been adequately removed C) Before inspecting D) Only after applying varnish. **Answer: B.**

### Feedback / Explanation
Sanding quality comes from controlled material removal. Start no coarser than necessary, progress logically, protect geometry and inspect between stages. Veneer and engineered boards require material-specific care. There is no single correct grit sequence for every job.

### Key Takeaways
- Abrasives cut the surface.
- Coarser is not automatically better.
- Progression must remove the previous scratch pattern.
- Preserve flatness, edges and dimensions.
- Treat veneer cautiously.
- Powered sanding requires correct setup and control.
- Manage dust through workplace controls.
- Inspect before advancing.
