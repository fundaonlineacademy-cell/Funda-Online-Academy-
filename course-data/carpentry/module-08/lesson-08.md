# Carpentry Fundamentals — Module 8, Lesson 8
## Finish Defects, Inspection, Care & Protection

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

### Learning Outcomes
Learners should be able to identify common finishing defects; distinguish symptom from likely cause; inspect finished work systematically; select an appropriate corrective route rather than hiding defects; and protect finished components through handling, storage, installation and handover.

### Why This Lesson Matters
Finishing is not complete when the last coat is applied. The work must be inspected, protected and handed over in a condition that meets the specification. Diagnosing defects prevents repeated mistakes and supports professional quality control.

### Key Terms / Vocabulary
**Run** — A downward flow mark caused when a wet coating moves before setting.

**Sag** — A local downward deformation or curtain-like thick area in a wet coating.

**Dust nib** — A small raised defect caused by dust or debris trapped in or on the coating.

**Orange peel** — A textured surface resembling citrus peel, commonly associated with some spray-application problems.

**Blister** — A raised bubble-like area caused by loss of adhesion or trapped material/conditions; diagnosis is required before correction.

**Miss** — An area that did not receive the required coating coverage.

**Witness mark** — A visible sign of previous preparation, repair, sanding or application that remains after finishing.

**Handover** — Formal transfer of completed work after required inspection, cleaning, documentation and client/workplace acceptance steps.

### Main Lesson Content
#### 1. Inspect systematically
Use good lighting, including side/raking light where useful. View broad faces, edges, corners, joints, profiles and hardware areas. Compare colour, sheen, coverage and surface quality with the approved sample or specification. Touch only when the coating has reached a safe handling stage.

#### 2. Recognise common defects
Runs and sags indicate uncontrolled film flow. Dust nibs indicate contamination during coating or cure. Brush marks can result from product, tool, technique or environmental factors. Misses show inadequate coverage. Uneven sheen can result from film variation, substrate absorption, mixing or preparation. Blisters, peeling or adhesion loss require investigation rather than cosmetic touch-up.

#### 3. Diagnose cause before correction
A defect name is not a complete diagnosis. For example, peeling could involve contamination, moisture, incompatible coats, insufficient preparation or another failure. If the cause is not removed, recoating may repeat the defect. Record where and when the defect appeared, review product/process conditions and obtain technical guidance where required.

#### 4. Correct at the proper stage
Some defects can be prepared and recoated after the film reaches the required condition. Others require stripping or deeper repair. Follow product instructions for sanding, recoating and repair. Never sand a soft, uncured film merely because a defect is visible. Do not add another coat to hide a run without first correcting the surface.

#### 5. Protect finished work
Finishes can be damaged by stacking, clamps, tools, packaging, transport and installation. Use clean padded supports where appropriate. Keep hardware and abrasive objects away from finished faces. Protect corners and edges. Ensure packaging materials are compatible and used only when the finish has reached the required stage.

#### 6. Installation care
During installation, protect finished surfaces from drills, screws, adhesive, sealant and dirty hands. Use clean work surfaces. Touch-up damage only with an approved compatible system. A touch-up should not be used to hide a fitting error or structural defect.

#### 7. Cleaning and aftercare
Provide the required care instructions. Do not recommend a universal cleaner: coatings differ in chemical resistance. Use the finish manufacturer's approved cleaning and maintenance guidance. Tell the client about relevant cure restrictions where the item is handed over before full service cure, if the system permits such handover.

#### 8. Record quality outcomes
Professional workplaces may record product, colour/batch, date, defects, corrective actions and inspection acceptance. These records support traceability and learning. Repeated dust defects, for example, may reveal an environmental-control problem rather than individual worker carelessness.

### Worked Example / Demonstration
A finished cabinet side has three dust nibs and one small run. The coating is checked against the product instructions and allowed to reach the required stage. The defects are locally prepared using the approved process, the surface is cleaned, and a corrective coat is applied under improved dust control. The panel is reinspected and protected on a clean rack before assembly.

### Practical Activity
Inspect an instructor-prepared finish sample containing at least four simulated defects. Complete a **Finish Inspection & Corrective Action Report** recording defect, location, likely cause(s), evidence required, corrective route, safety/product controls and final acceptance result.

### Knowledge Check
1. Why is a defect name not always a diagnosis? A) Different underlying causes can produce similar symptoms B) Defects have no causes C) All coatings fail equally D) Inspection is unnecessary. **Answer: A.**
2. What should happen before sanding a coating defect? A) Confirm the coating has reached the appropriate condition and check repair instructions B) Sand immediately C) Add water D) Install hardware. **Answer: A.**
3. Why protect cured/curing work during transport? A) Handling and packaging can scratch, print or damage finishes B) Transport improves finish C) Dust makes it harder D) Timber becomes metal. **Answer: A.**
4. What should guide cleaning advice? A) Any household chemical B) Finish manufacturer/system guidance C) Strongest solvent available D) Timber price. **Answer: B.**

### Feedback / Explanation
Inspection must identify both the visible defect and its likely process cause. Correction should happen at the proper coating stage. Finished work remains vulnerable during handling and installation, so protection and aftercare are part of quality workmanship.

### Key Takeaways
- Inspect every face, edge and detail systematically.
- Recognise common finish defects.
- Diagnose cause before correction.
- Respect coating readiness before repair.
- Protect finished work in storage and transport.
- Prevent installation damage.
- Give product-appropriate care guidance.
- Record defects and corrective action for quality improvement.
