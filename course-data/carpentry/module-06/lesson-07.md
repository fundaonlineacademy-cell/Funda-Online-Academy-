# Carpentry Fundamentals — Module 6, Lesson 7
## Adhesives, Screws, Nails & Other Fixings

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
Learners will distinguish adhesive bonding from mechanical fixing; select fixings according to material, service condition and specification; explain preparation and installation controls; identify common fixing defects; and apply safe storage, handling and product-instruction principles.

## Why This Lesson Matters
SAQA carpentry material identifies gluing, nailing, screwing, bolting and related fixing methods as core trade knowledge. The correct fixing can secure an assembly efficiently; the wrong fixing, poor placement or incorrect adhesive can damage components or create premature failure.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Adhesive** — a bonding material used between prepared surfaces.
- **Fastener** — a mechanical item such as a screw, nail or bolt used to connect components.
- **Pilot hole** — a pre-drilled guide hole used where required for a fixing/material combination.
- **Thread engagement** — the portion of a threaded fastener effectively gripping the receiving material.
- **Countersink** — a recess prepared for an appropriate screw head.
- **Clamp pressure** — controlled force used to hold bonded components in the required position during assembly/cure.
- **Open time** — product-specific period during which an applied adhesive remains suitable for assembly.
- **Cure** — development of the adhesive’s intended properties over the specified time/conditions.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Fixing is a design choice
Adhesives and mechanical fasteners transfer loads differently. A glued long-grain joint can distribute load across a broad surface. A screw provides local mechanical engagement. A nail may be efficient for particular carpentry work. A bolt can clamp components and may be removable. Selection must follow the drawing, material, load, appearance and service environment.

### 2. Adhesive compatibility
There is no single “wood glue” suitable for every material and environment. Solid timber, plywood, MDF, laminates and treated products may require different adhesive systems. Interior and moisture-exposed conditions differ. Read the product label, technical data and safety information. Where a project specifies an adhesive class or product, do not substitute without approval.

### 3. Surface preparation
Bonding surfaces should be suitable for the adhesive: correctly fitted, reasonably clean and free of contamination that would prevent bonding. Excessive gaps should not automatically be filled with ordinary adhesive. Some products are gap-filling and others are not; use only as specified. Prepare the surface without destroying the joint dimensions.

### 4. Application and timing
Follow manufacturer instructions for spread, one- or two-sided application, mixing where relevant, assembly time, pressure and cure. Temperature and environmental conditions can influence products. Do not teach a universal “clamp for 30 minutes” rule because products differ. Plan clamps and assembly sequence before applying adhesive so the joint is not left open while tools are found.

### 5. Clamping bonded work
Clamp pressure should bring mating surfaces into the designed contact without distorting the assembly. More pressure is not always better. Excessive pressure can bow frames, crush board edges or squeeze an assembly out of position. Check square and dimensions while clamps are applied. Use pads where clamp jaws could mark finished surfaces.

### 6. Screws
Screws are available in many types for timber, boards, metal connectors and specialist applications. Use the specified type and size. Pilot holes and countersinks may be required depending on material and screw system. Maintain appropriate edge distance and alignment. Use the correct driver bit and controlled torque. Stop if the screw strips, breaks or causes splitting; diagnose the cause.

Do not assume that a longer or thicker screw is automatically stronger. It may penetrate an unwanted surface, split the component or conflict with hardware.

### 7. Nails
Nails can provide fast fixing for suitable work. Type, length, head form, coating and installation method depend on application. Place nails to avoid splitting and unintended surface damage. When using a hammer, maintain a clear striking area and suitable eye protection as required. Powered nailers require specific training and controls; never point them at a person or defeat safety mechanisms.

### 8. Bolts and other mechanical fixings
Bolts, washers, nuts, anchors and proprietary connectors may be used in carpentry assemblies. Tightening should achieve the specified connection without crushing timber or board material. Washers may distribute load where the design calls for them. Structural anchors and building fixings must follow approved details and manufacturer installation requirements; do not improvise capacities.

### 9. Corrosion and service conditions
Fixings exposed to moisture, treated timber or outdoor conditions may require specified corrosion resistance. The carpenter should follow project and product requirements rather than assuming any shiny fastener is suitable. Compatibility between metals, treatments and environments can matter.

### 10. Inspection and defects
Inspect for split timber, proud or overdriven heads, stripped screws, bent nails, loose bolts, adhesive starvation or excessive squeeze-out, misalignment and surface contamination. Check the finished assembly against dimensions and appearance requirements. Correct using approved methods and record recurring defects.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A painted indoor cabinet is specified as a combination of dowelled joints and screws. The carpenter confirms board type and screw system, drills with a verified jig, dry assembles the cabinet, then applies the specified adhesive to dowels and installs screws using controlled driving. When one screw begins to strip, the carpenter stops instead of overdriving, checks hole preparation and hardware, and replaces or repairs according to procedure.

## Practical Activity
Create a **Fixing Selection Sheet** for an indoor solid-timber frame, a plywood box and a board-product cabinet. For each, identify the specified fixing/bonding method, preparation, tools, service condition, installation checks, likely defect and information that must come from the manufacturer or drawing.

## Knowledge Check
1. Why is a universal clamp time unsafe to teach? A) adhesives have product-specific requirements B) clamps do not work C) wood never bonds D) time is irrelevant. **A**.
2. A screw begins splitting an edge. Best response? A) drive harder B) stop and diagnose selection/preparation/placement C) add glue over split D) hide it. **B**.
3. More clamp pressure is always better. True or false? **False.**
4. Structural anchor capacities should come from: A) guessing B) approved design/manufacturer data C) fastener colour D) any previous job. **B**.

## Feedback / Explanation
Fixings are systems with material and installation requirements. Product instructions control adhesives and proprietary hardware. Quality comes from correct preparation, controlled installation and inspection.

## Key Takeaways
Match fixing to material and service. Follow adhesive instructions. Plan clamping before glue application. Use correct pilot/countersink practices where required. Never assume bigger fixings are automatically better. Inspect for damage and alignment after fixing.
