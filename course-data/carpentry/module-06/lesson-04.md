# Carpentry Fundamentals — Module 6, Lesson 4
## Housing, Dado & Rebate Joints

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
Learners will distinguish housing/dado and rebate joints; explain how grooves and shoulders locate components; set out joints from drawings and reference surfaces; describe safe cutting and fitting methods; and inspect depth, width, seating, alignment and edge quality.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Housings and rebates appear in shelving, cabinet construction, backs, frames and many purpose-made components. They provide location and bearing surfaces that can improve assembly accuracy. SAQA carpentry material includes housing joints, while DBE learner material introduces rebate joints as a common corner connection.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Housing/Dado** — a channel cut across or into the face of a component to receive another component.
- **Rebate/Rabbet** — a rectangular recess along an edge or end, producing a stepped profile.
- **Shoulder** — the edge of a recess that locates the mating component.
- **Groove** — a long narrow channel; terminology varies with direction and application.
- **Depth** — distance from the original surface to the bottom of a recess.
- **Width** — distance between the two sides of a housing or groove.
- **Stopped housing** — a housing that ends before reaching an exposed edge.
- **Breakout** — unwanted tearing or chipping at an edge during cutting or machining.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Housing principle
A housing receives part of another component in a channel. In a shelf unit, the housing can locate the shelf and provide a bearing surface. The joint may be through, where the channel reaches an edge, or stopped, where the recess is concealed before an exposed edge. Exact depth and width come from the drawing and material, not a universal fraction.

### 2. Rebate principle
A rebate removes a rectangular section from an edge or end. It may receive a back panel, form a corner, locate a frame component or create a step for another part. DBE learner material describes a rebate as a rectangular cut creating an L-shaped section and notes that it may be reinforced with appropriate glue or fixings. The reinforcement used must suit the actual design.

### 3. Set-out from reference surfaces
Confirm the drawing, component identity and orientation. Select reference faces. Mark the position and limits of the recess. When a housing must fit an actual shelf, direct transfer can help control fit, but still verify dimensions. Mark waste clearly. For a stopped housing, identify the stop point so it is not accidentally cut through to the visible edge.

### 4. Width and fit
The mating component should fit according to the intended assembly. Excessive looseness creates poor location and visible gaps; excessive tightness can split the side panel or prevent full seating, especially when adhesive is added. Material thickness can vary, particularly with board products, so measure actual stock and follow production tolerances.

### 5. Depth and remaining material
Cutting deeper is not automatically stronger. A deeper housing leaves less material below the recess. The correct depth balances location and bearing with preservation of the supporting component, and it must follow the design. Where the joint is part of a structural or engineered assembly, use the specified dimensions without improvisation.

### 6. Hand-tool production
A housing can be formed with saw and chisel or other suitable hand tools. Establish clean boundaries, remove waste progressively and finish the bottom to the required level. Secure the work. Keep the chisel cutting direction controlled and hands clear. For a rebate, a rebate plane or other suitable method may be used where available and appropriate.

### 7. Machine production
Routers and workshop machines can produce repeatable housings and rebates, but setup is critical. Confirm cutter suitability, guards, depth settings, fence or guide alignment, workpiece support and extraction. Make a test cut in suitable scrap where workplace procedure permits. Never adjust a machine while it is running or bypass guarding to obtain a cut.

### 8. Preventing breakout
Breakout can occur where a cutter or saw exits the material. Grain direction, board face quality, cutter sharpness, feed method and support affect the result. Use approved techniques such as sacrificial backing, correct cutting direction or scoring where appropriate to the tool and material. The exact method should follow machine and material guidance.

### 9. Dry fitting and assembly
Clean the recess and test the mating part. Check that it reaches the bottom where designed and that shoulders seat correctly. Confirm the shelf or component is square to the supporting member. In a cabinet, check overall dimensions and diagonals before permanent assembly. Adhesive and fixings are then applied according to specification.

### 10. Common defects and diagnosis
A housing that is too narrow may come from incorrect set-out or cutter selection. Too wide may result from wandering cuts, repeated uncontrolled passes or wrong tooling. Incorrect depth may come from a bad gauge or machine setting. A shelf not square may indicate an angled housing, wrong component length or assembly distortion. Rebate defects include inconsistent width/depth, torn edges and failure of the mating component to seat.

Professional practice records the cause and prevents repetition. If a machine setup produced one incorrect housing, stop before machining the rest.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A cabinet side requires three shelf housings. The carpenter verifies actual shelf thickness, marks positions from one reference end and sets a guided router using a test piece. The first housing is measured and test-fitted before the other two are cut. During dry assembly, the shelves seat fully and remain square to the side. This sequence prevents a wrong setup from being repeated three times.

## Practical Activity
On a drawing of a small shelf unit, identify all housings and rebates. Produce a **Joint Set-out Plan** showing reference face, position, width, depth, stop point where relevant, cutting method, test-fit check and likely defect. If supervised facilities are available, make one housing and one rebate sample.

## Knowledge Check
1. What is a stopped housing? A) a broken joint B) a channel ending before an exposed edge C) a nail hole D) a surface finish. **B**.
2. Why not make every housing as deep as possible? A) depth removes supporting material B) depth changes wood species C) it prevents marking D) it makes timber longer. **A**.
3. Best control before repeating a router setup? A) test and verify first cut B) cut all parts immediately C) remove guards D) ignore actual thickness. **A**.
4. A shelf does not reach the housing bottom. First response? A) force it B) inspect for fit, debris, depth and width causes C) add paint D) shorten cabinet. **B**.

## Feedback / Explanation
Housings and rebates depend on controlled width, depth and position. Test cuts and dry fitting catch errors before permanent assembly or repeated production.

## Key Takeaways
Use the drawing and actual stock dimensions. Preserve reference surfaces. Control both width and depth. Prevent breakout through correct tooling and support. Test the first repeated setup. Dry assemble before fixing.

## Visual Learning Guidance
Provide cross-sections of through housing, stopped housing and edge rebate. Label width, depth, shoulder and remaining material. Include a cabinet-side visual showing three correctly aligned shelf housings.
