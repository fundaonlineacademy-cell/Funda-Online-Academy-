# Carpentry Fundamentals — Module 6, Lesson 5
## Mortise-and-Tenon Principles

**Estimated study time:** 45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
Learners will identify mortise, tenon, cheeks and shoulders; explain why mortise-and-tenon joints suit many frame connections; set out the joint from consistent references; describe safe cutting and fitting; and diagnose common fit, shoulder and alignment defects.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Mortise-and-tenon joinery is central to traditional and modern frame construction. DBE Civil Technology and SAQA carpentry material repeatedly include mortise-and-tenon joints, including shouldered and haunched variations. The lesson develops accuracy, patience and an understanding of how joint surfaces work together.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary
- **Mortise** — a recess or hole made to receive a tenon.
- **Tenon** — a projecting tongue formed on a component to fit a mortise.
- **Cheek** — one of the broad faces of a tenon.
- **Shoulder** — the step around a tenon that seats against the mating member.
- **Haunch** — a reduced projection used in some mortise-and-tenon forms to support alignment or resist twisting.
- **Gauge line** — a controlled marking line defining joint thickness or position.
- **Registration** — consistent positioning from reference faces/edges.
- **Drawbore** — a specialised technique not to be improvised; where specified it uses offset pinning to draw a joint together.

## Main Lesson Content
### 1. Anatomy of the joint
A mortise-and-tenon joint connects a projecting tenon to a matching mortise. The tenon cheeks engage the mortise sides while the shoulders locate the rail against the mating component. These surfaces perform different functions, so a carpenter must not focus only on whether the tenon enters the hole. Good shoulder seating and correct component alignment are equally important.

### 2. Applications and variations
The joint is widely used for rails meeting stiles or legs, doors, frames and purpose-made components. Variations include through, blind, haunched, shouldered and twin arrangements. DBE Grade 12 woodworking content includes haunched and twin mortise-and-tenon forms. Learners should recognise that dimensions and variation selection are design decisions. Do not apply a memorised tenon proportion to every job; use the approved drawing or specification.

### 3. Material and grain
The tenon is cut from the rail or connecting member and should preserve sound material around the joint. Defects, short grain or splits near the tenon can reduce reliability. The mortised member must retain sufficient material around the recess. If the drawing creates concern because of an actual defect in the stock, select another piece or escalate according to workplace procedure rather than quietly changing the joint.

### 4. Reference-face set-out
Mark reference faces and edges before setting out. Establish the shoulder position from the component length. Use a suitable gauge or marking method to define tenon cheeks and corresponding mortise position from the same reference logic. This is critical: if the mortise is gauged from one face and the tenon from an inconsistent face, the components may assemble with an offset even when each joint appears neatly cut.

Mark waste clearly and label mating parts in multi-joint assemblies. Component identification prevents a well-made tenon from being paired with the wrong mortise.

### 5. Cutting the mortise
Mortises may be made by chisel, drill-and-chisel, router, mortising machine or other approved method. Hand mortising requires secure workholding and controlled chisel use. Machine mortising requires correct setup, guarding and training. Work progressively so the mortise stays within the marked boundaries and at the specified depth.

Do not enlarge a mortise randomly to make a tenon fit. First identify where interference occurs. Excessive widening can create a loose joint and misalignment.

### 6. Cutting the tenon
Tenon shoulders and cheeks must be controlled. Sawing past the shoulder line can leave a visible gap and weaken the shoulder area. Leaving too much material creates a tight fit. A common professional method is to cut carefully with a small fitting allowance, then pare or plane only where inspection shows it is required.

Tenon thickness must suit the mortise. The tenon should engage without destructive force. Hammering a very tight tenon can split the mortised member, especially near an end.

### 7. Shoulder seating
Shoulders should seat against the mating face according to the design. If one shoulder closes while another remains open, possible causes include an out-of-square shoulder, debris, a mortise that is not aligned, a tenon cheek high spot or twisted stock. Diagnose before removing material.

A shoulder gap should not simply be filled with adhesive. Adhesive is part of the specified bonding system, not a substitute for accurate joinery.

### 8. Dry assembly and frame geometry
A frame with multiple mortise-and-tenon joints must be dry assembled. Confirm each joint, overall dimensions, diagonals, twist and orientation. Clamps should be planned before glue application. During permanent assembly, apply pressure in a way that closes shoulders without bowing the frame.

### 9. Adhesive and fixing
Use the adhesive specified for the materials and service conditions. Follow manufacturer instructions for preparation, spread, assembly time, pressure and cure. Some mortise-and-tenon assemblies may include pins, wedges or other details, but these should only be used when designed or approved. Never improvise specialised reinforcement in a structural or quality-controlled component.

### 10. Inspection and corrective practice
Inspect shoulder closure, joint fit, rail alignment, square, dimensions, surface damage and adhesive cleanup. If the tenon is loose, determine whether repair is permitted. If a mortise is misplaced, do not shift every other component to hide the error unless the drawing and approval permit the change.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A door-frame training sample has two rails meeting a stile. The carpenter marks all faces, gauges mortises and tenons from the same reference face, cuts one test pair, verifies shoulder closure and then completes the second pair. During dry assembly, one rail is offset. Investigation shows its tenon was gauged from the non-reference face. The component is remade rather than forcing the frame out of alignment.

## Practical Activity
Draw an exploded mortise-and-tenon joint and label mortise, tenon, cheek, shoulder and reference face. Then complete an inspection table covering tenon fit, mortise position, shoulder closure, rail alignment, square and corrective action. Make a supervised sample where facilities permit.

## Knowledge Check
1. What receives the tenon? A) rebate B) mortise C) dowel D) biscuit. **B**.
2. Why gauge mortise and tenon consistently from reference faces? A) to control alignment B) to change grain C) to avoid all measurement D) to increase moisture. **A**.
3. A tenon requires heavy hammering. Best response? A) force it B) diagnose the tight fit C) add a bigger hammer D) split the stile. **B**.
4. One shoulder remains open. What should happen first? A) fill with glue B) diagnose seating/alignment C) paint it D) add random screws. **B**.

## Feedback / Explanation
Mortise-and-tenon quality depends on accurate registration, controlled fit and seated shoulders. Forcing or filling conceals causes rather than correcting them.

## Key Takeaways
Know the joint anatomy. Work from common reference faces. Protect shoulder lines. Fit progressively. Dry assemble the whole frame. Follow specified adhesive/reinforcement details. Inspect geometry, not only the individual joint.
