# Carpentry — Professional Skills Short Course
## Module 1 — Carpentry Practice, Workshop Safety & Professional Standards
### Lesson 4 — Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. Explain the role and limitations of personal protective equipment in carpentry risk control.
2. Select appropriate categories of PPE for common workshop hazards using a task-specific risk assessment.
3. Inspect PPE before use and identify conditions that require replacement, adjustment or reporting.
4. Explain why correct fit, compatibility, maintenance and storage affect PPE performance.
5. Apply a simple PPE-selection process to realistic carpentry scenarios without treating PPE as a substitute for safer controls.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Carpentry exposes workers to different hazards at different stages of a job. Cutting or routing may create flying particles and noise; sanding may create airborne dust; handling rough material may expose hands to splinters; some finishing products may create chemical exposure; and moving or falling materials may create foot or head hazards depending on the workplace. PPE can reduce the consequences of exposure, but only when it is suitable for the hazard, correctly selected, properly fitted, maintained and actually worn.

A professional carpenter therefore does not begin with the question, “What PPE do I always wear?” The better question is, “What hazards remain after the work has been made as safe as reasonably practicable, and what additional personal protection is required for this task?” South African occupational-health guidance reinforces this principle: PPE should not replace elimination, substitution, engineering or administrative controls where those measures are reasonably practicable. PPE is an additional protective layer when other controls cannot remove or adequately reduce the remaining exposure.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary

**Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)** — Equipment or clothing worn by a person to reduce exposure to a workplace hazard. Examples can include eye, hearing, respiratory, foot, head and suitable body protection depending on the task.

**Residual Risk** — The risk that remains after other control measures have been applied. PPE is commonly considered as additional protection against this remaining risk.

**Safety Eyewear** — Eye protection designed for specified hazards such as impact or particles. Ordinary prescription spectacles or sunglasses should not automatically be assumed to provide occupational eye protection.

**Hearing Protection** — Devices such as suitable earplugs or earmuffs used to reduce harmful noise exposure when required as part of the workplace noise-control programme.

**Respiratory Protective Equipment (RPE)** — A category of PPE intended to reduce inhalation exposure to specified airborne contaminants. Correct type, fit, use and maintenance are essential; a generic dust mask is not suitable for every airborne hazard.

**Fit** — How correctly PPE matches and seals or sits on the individual user. Poor fit can reduce protection and may interfere with safe work.

**Compatibility** — The ability of different items of PPE to be worn together without one item preventing another from functioning correctly.

**Pre-use Inspection** — A check made before PPE is worn to identify damage, contamination, deterioration, missing parts, poor adjustment or other conditions that could reduce protection.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. PPE is one layer in a larger safety system
A common beginner mistake is to think that wearing PPE makes a hazardous task safe. It does not. PPE normally places a protective barrier on the worker while the hazard may still be present. For example, safety glasses may reduce the chance of an eye injury from a flying chip, but they do not prevent a poorly secured workpiece from moving. Hearing protection may reduce the sound reaching the ear, but it does not remove excessive noise at its source. Respiratory protection may be required for a particular airborne exposure, but it does not replace effective dust extraction or ventilation where those controls are practicable.

This is why PPE selection must follow hazard identification and risk control. Before choosing PPE, ask whether the hazard can be eliminated, whether a safer material or process can be substituted, whether guarding/extraction or another engineering control can reduce exposure, and whether procedures, training, access control or scheduling can reduce risk. Only then should the remaining personal-protection requirement be determined. In some jobs, PPE is still essential even when stronger controls are operating.

### 2. Match protection to the actual task
There is no single “carpenter PPE kit” that is automatically correct for every operation. The required protection depends on the task, tool, material, environment and workplace risk assessment.

Eye protection may be needed where chips, dust, fragments or splashes could reach the eyes. The protection must be appropriate to the identified hazard. A face shield may sometimes provide useful face protection, but where eye protection is required it should not automatically be treated as a substitute for suitable safety eyewear unless the equipment and risk assessment specifically support that use.

Hearing protection may be required for noisy woodworking operations. However, selection should form part of a proper noise-control approach rather than guessing from how loud a machine “feels.” Excessive attenuation can also create communication or warning-signal problems, so suitable selection matters.

Respiratory protection requires particular care. Wood dust, coating vapours and other airborne contaminants are not all the same hazard. Different filters or respirator types may be required, and tight-fitting respiratory protection depends on achieving an effective fit. The learner should therefore never assume that one disposable mask protects against every dust, vapour or chemical. Workplace risk assessment, product safety information, exposure requirements and competent guidance determine the correct protection.

Foot protection may be required where there is a risk from falling objects, sharp objects, slippery conditions or other workplace hazards. Head protection may be required in environments where overhead or falling-object hazards exist. Clothing should be suitable for the work and should not introduce entanglement hazards around machinery. Jewellery, loose sleeves, unsecured long hair and other items that can be caught by moving equipment require control according to the task and workplace procedure.

### 3. Gloves require task-specific judgement
Gloves are useful for many handling tasks, but “always wear gloves” is unsafe advice in a woodworking environment. Suitable gloves may protect against splinters, rough surfaces or certain chemical exposures when the glove material is compatible with the substance. However, gloves can create an entanglement hazard near rotating or moving machinery. The correct decision depends on the equipment, manufacturer's instructions, risk assessment and workplace procedure.

For chemical products, glove selection cannot be based only on thickness or appearance. A glove material that resists one chemical may perform poorly against another. Product labels and safety data sheets should be consulted, together with workplace requirements. Contaminated gloves must be removed and managed in a way that prevents skin contact and the spread of contamination.

### 4. Fit and compatibility are part of protection
PPE that is uncomfortable, loose, badly adjusted or incompatible with other equipment may not perform as intended. Safety eyewear should sit securely and provide the required coverage without obstructing vision. Hearing protection must be worn correctly. Tight-fitting respiratory protection requires an appropriate seal and fit. Footwear should fit securely and suit the working surface and hazard.

Compatibility becomes important when several items are worn at once. Earmuffs, eye protection, a hard hat and respiratory equipment may physically interfere with one another. A spectacle arm or another item can affect the seal of some tight-fitting respirators or hearing protectors. The complete combination should therefore be considered, not each item in isolation.

### 5. Inspect before use
PPE can deteriorate. Scratched or cracked eye protection can affect vision or protection. Worn straps may prevent correct adjustment. Damaged hearing protectors may not perform properly. Respiratory equipment can be contaminated, incorrectly assembled or fitted with unsuitable, damaged or expired components depending on the system. Protective footwear can be damaged or lose tread.

A simple pre-use inspection asks: Is this the correct item for the task? Is it clean enough to use safely? Is anything cracked, torn, distorted, heavily worn, missing or contaminated? Does it fit correctly? Are replaceable components suitable and within any manufacturer-defined service requirements? Does it work correctly with the other PPE being worn? If the answer is uncertain, the worker should not simply continue and hope for protection; the issue should be reported and corrected.

### 6. Cleaning, maintenance and storage matter
Reusable PPE should be cared for according to manufacturer and workplace instructions. Incorrect cleaning chemicals can damage materials. Throwing safety glasses into a toolbox with sharp objects can scratch them. Storing respiratory equipment in a dusty or contaminated area can defeat the purpose of cleaning it. Sharing contaminated PPE without appropriate hygiene controls can expose another worker.

Storage should keep equipment protected from damage, contamination and conditions that degrade it. Defective PPE should be repaired only where the manufacturer or authorised system permits repair; otherwise it should be replaced. The important principle is that PPE must remain capable of providing the protection for which it was selected.

### 7. PPE does not increase competence
A final professional principle is that PPE does not authorise a person to perform a task for which they are not trained or competent. Wearing goggles, earmuffs and safety footwear does not make an inexperienced learner competent to operate a woodworking machine. Training, supervision, guarding, safe procedures and correct tool selection remain necessary. PPE supports a safe system of work; it does not replace that system.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
**Scenario:** A learner is preparing to sand several timber components with a powered sander in a workshop.

A weak approach would be: “Put on a mask and start sanding.” A professional approach is systematic.

1. Identify the hazards: airborne wood dust, particles reaching the eyes, noise, electrical/tool hazards, vibration and an unsecured workpiece.
2. Apply stronger controls first: use suitable dust extraction where provided, inspect the sander and extraction connection, secure the workpiece, keep the area clear and follow the tool manufacturer's instructions.
3. Determine residual exposure: dust or particles may remain despite extraction; the operation may also require hearing protection depending on the assessed noise exposure.
4. Select suitable PPE: appropriate eye protection and any respiratory/hearing protection specified by the risk assessment and workplace requirements.
5. Inspect and fit the PPE before starting.
6. Stop if the extraction fails, PPE is defective, visibility becomes impaired or another unsafe condition develops. PPE is not a reason to continue through a failed engineering control.

The important lesson is the decision process, not memorising a fixed list.

## Practical Activity — PPE Selection Matrix
Choose four carpentry activities, for example: manually handling rough timber, sanding timber, applying a finishing product, and working in an area where overhead work is taking place.

For each activity, create a five-column table in your learner notes:
- Task
- Main hazard(s)
- Controls before PPE
- Possible PPE required after risk assessment
- Pre-use checks

Then add one sentence explaining why PPE alone would not adequately control each task. If you have access to real PPE, inspect it without undertaking hazardous work. Record the manufacturer markings/instructions you can identify, its condition, adjustment points and any obvious incompatibility with other equipment. Do not test PPE by deliberately exposing yourself to a hazard.

## Knowledge Check

**1. Why should PPE generally not be the first control considered for a workshop hazard?**
A. PPE is only used by supervisors.
B. PPE may reduce personal exposure while leaving the hazard present.
C. PPE is never required in carpentry.
D. PPE makes tools less accurate.

**Correct answer: B.** PPE is important, but it usually acts on the worker's exposure rather than removing the hazard itself. Higher-order controls should be considered first where reasonably practicable.

**2. A learner has one type of disposable mask and wants to use it for every sanding and finishing task. What is the best response?**
A. Any mask protects against every airborne hazard.
B. Two masks worn together always solve the problem.
C. Respiratory protection must be selected for the specific airborne hazard and work conditions.
D. Respiratory protection is unnecessary if the job is short.

**Correct answer: C.** Wood dust and chemical vapours, for example, can require different controls and respiratory protection. Selection must be hazard-specific.

**3. Why must multiple PPE items be checked for compatibility?**
A. Different PPE can interfere with fit, seals, vision, hearing protection or movement.
B. All PPE must be the same colour.
C. Workers may only wear one item at a time.
D. Compatibility affects only appearance.

**Correct answer: A.** PPE must function as a system. One item can reduce another item's performance if the combination is poorly selected or fitted.

## Feedback / Explanation
If you selected B, C and A, you have identified the core principle of this lesson: PPE is selected after understanding the hazard and the controls already in place. Effective PPE use depends on suitability, correct fit, compatibility, inspection and maintenance. If any answer was incorrect, revisit the sections on hierarchy of controls, respiratory protection and compatibility before continuing.

## Key Takeaways
- PPE is an additional protective layer, not a substitute for eliminating or controlling hazards at source where reasonably practicable.
- PPE must be selected for the specific task and hazard rather than from a universal carpentry checklist.
- Eye, hearing, respiratory, hand, foot, head and body protection each have different purposes and limitations.
- Gloves require special judgement around moving machinery because they may create entanglement risk.
- Correct fit and compatibility between PPE items affect protection.
- PPE must be inspected before use and maintained, cleaned and stored according to applicable instructions.
- Wearing PPE does not make an untrained person competent to perform hazardous work.

## Accuracy Note
This lesson teaches general carpentry safety principles and South African workplace context. PPE requirements for a real task must be determined from the actual risk assessment, applicable occupational-health and safety requirements, manufacturer instructions, product safety data and workplace procedures. Product-specific respirator filters, glove materials, hearing-protection ratings and other technical selections should not be guessed from this lesson alone.
