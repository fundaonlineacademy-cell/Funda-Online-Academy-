# Carpentry — Professional Skills Short Course
## Module 1 — Carpentry Practice, Workshop Safety & Professional Standards
### Lesson 3 — Workshop Hazards & Risk Awareness

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. Identify common hazard categories in a carpentry workshop or work area.
2. Explain the difference between a hazard and a risk.
3. Carry out a simple pre-task risk-thinking process before beginning carpentry work.
4. Select sensible controls using the hierarchy of controls rather than relying only on PPE.
5. Recognise situations that require work to stop and the hazard to be reported or escalated.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Carpentry combines sharp tools, rotating machinery, electricity, timber, boards, dust, noise, manual handling, finishing products and changing work conditions. A workshop can look familiar while still containing serious hazards. Professional carpenters therefore do not wait for an accident before thinking about safety. They observe the work area, understand the task, identify what could cause harm, consider who may be exposed and make sure appropriate controls are in place before work begins.

In South Africa, occupational health and safety law places responsibilities on employers and employees and requires workplace hazards to be addressed as far as reasonably practicable. For a learner, the important practical habit is simple: **identify the hazard, understand the risk, control it, and do not continue when the work cannot be carried out safely within your competence and authority.**

## Key Terms / Vocabulary

**Hazard** — Anything with the potential to cause injury, illness, damage or other harm. Examples include an unguarded moving blade, excessive wood dust, a damaged electrical lead or material blocking a walkway.

**Risk** — The combination of how likely harm is to occur and how serious the consequences could be. The same hazard can create different levels of risk depending on exposure, conditions and controls.

**Risk assessment** — A systematic process of identifying hazards, considering who may be harmed and how, evaluating the risks, selecting controls, recording or communicating findings where required, and reviewing the assessment when conditions change.

**Control measure** — An action or system used to eliminate a hazard or reduce the associated risk. Examples include removing the hazard, using safer equipment, installing guards or extraction, changing work procedures and using suitable PPE.

**Hierarchy of controls** — A way of prioritising risk controls, generally favouring elimination and controls at the source before administrative measures and personal protective equipment.

**Exposure** — Contact with or being subjected to a hazard. Exposure can be immediate, such as contact with a moving cutter, or repeated over time, such as exposure to excessive dust or noise.

**Near miss** — An unplanned event that did not cause injury or damage but had the potential to do so. Near misses are warning signs that should be reported and investigated according to workplace procedure.

**Dynamic risk awareness** — Continually observing the job and recognising when conditions have changed after work has started, such as a new person entering the work area, material becoming unstable or equipment behaving abnormally.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Hazard and risk are not the same thing
A hazard is the source or situation that can cause harm. Risk describes the chance and potential seriousness of that harm under the actual working conditions. This distinction matters because safe work requires more than making a list of dangerous objects.

Consider a portable circular saw. Its moving blade is a hazard because contact can cause severe injury. The level of risk depends on factors such as whether the saw is suitable and maintained, whether its guards operate correctly, whether the operator is trained, whether the material is properly supported, whether the work area is controlled and whether the correct procedure is followed. A familiar tool does not become harmless simply because it has been used many times.

The same principle applies to dust. Timber and board machining can generate airborne dust. The hazard exists even when there is no dramatic event. Risk can increase when dust-generating work is frequent, extraction is inadequate, housekeeping allows dust to accumulate or workers are exposed without appropriate controls. Safety therefore includes both obvious accident hazards and less visible health hazards.

### 2. Common hazard categories in carpentry
A professional hazard scan should be broad. **Mechanical hazards** include moving blades, cutters, rotating parts, pinch points, kickback and unexpected movement of workpieces. **Sharp-edge hazards** include chisels, hand saws, knives, splintered timber and exposed fasteners. **Electrical hazards** can arise from damaged cords, plugs, unsuitable connections, wet conditions or faulty equipment.

**Airborne and chemical hazards** can include wood dust, board dust, vapours or substances associated with adhesives, coatings and cleaning products. Product-specific precautions should come from labels, safety data sheets, manufacturer instructions and workplace procedures rather than assumptions. **Noise and vibration** may arise from machinery and power tools. **Manual-handling and ergonomic hazards** include heavy, long or awkward boards, poor lifting technique, repetitive work and unsuitable working height.

The environment creates hazards too. Offcuts, cables, packaging and tools can obstruct walkways. Poor lighting can make marking or machine work less reliable. Unstable stacks can fall. Fire risk may increase where combustible materials, dust or certain finishing products are poorly controlled. Visitors or untrained persons entering a machine operating zone can also change the risk of an otherwise planned task.

Hazards frequently interact. For example, poor housekeeping may force an operator into an awkward stance near equipment. A poorly supported long board may move unexpectedly while being cut. Rushing to meet a deadline can encourage a worker to skip a pre-use check. Effective risk awareness therefore examines the whole task, not one object in isolation.

### 3. A practical pre-task risk-thinking process
Before beginning a task, pause long enough to answer five questions.

**First: What am I about to do?** Be specific. “Cut timber” is less useful than “crosscut these prepared pieces to the dimensions on the cut list using the approved method.” Clear task definition helps reveal the actual hazards.

**Second: What could cause harm?** Look at the tool or machine, material, electricity, dust, noise, work position, other people, access routes and surrounding conditions. Check whether the material contains unexpected fasteners or defects that could affect the operation.

**Third: Who could be harmed and how?** The answer may include the operator, a colleague holding or moving material, a cleaner, a visitor or another person passing nearby. Do not assume that only the person holding the tool is exposed.

**Fourth: What controls are already required, and are they working?** This can include guards, extraction, suitable workholding, barriers, correct operating procedure, training, supervision and PPE. A control that is missing, defeated, damaged or unsuitable should not be counted as protection.

**Fifth: Has anything changed?** A risk check is not valid forever. Different material, a changed setup, a damaged accessory, poor lighting, an unfamiliar task or a new person entering the area can require the work to be reassessed.

### 4. Use the hierarchy of controls
A weak safety culture jumps immediately to PPE. A stronger approach first asks whether the hazard can be removed or reduced at its source. The hierarchy of controls provides a useful order of thinking.

**Elimination** means removing the hazard where reasonably practicable. For example, clearing an unnecessary obstruction from a walkway eliminates the trip hazard rather than merely warning people about it. **Substitution** means replacing something with a safer alternative where appropriate and authorised. **Engineering controls** physically separate people from hazards or control the hazard at source, such as effective machine guarding or suitable dust extraction. **Administrative controls** include procedures, training, restricted access, signage, inspection systems and work scheduling. **PPE** provides personal protection appropriate to the remaining hazard and task.

These layers can work together. A dust-generating operation may require source extraction, controlled work methods, housekeeping and appropriate respiratory protection depending on the actual assessment and applicable requirements. PPE should not be used to justify leaving a preventable hazard uncontrolled.

### 5. Dynamic awareness while the job is underway
Conditions can change after a safe start. Listen and observe. A machine that develops unusual vibration, smell or sound may require immediate shutdown and reporting according to procedure. Material may shift in its supports. A clamp may loosen. A cable may move into a traffic route. Dust extraction may stop working. A person may enter a restricted area.

Professional behaviour is to respond to the change, not to continue because the task is nearly finished. Stop when necessary, make the area safe within your authority, isolate equipment where the procedure requires and you are authorised to do so, and report the condition to the responsible person. Never remove guards, bypass safety devices or improvise a repair merely to complete a job.

Near misses deserve the same professional attention. If an offcut falls beside someone, a board almost tips from a rack or a workpiece unexpectedly moves without causing injury, the absence of injury does not mean the system was safe. Reporting the event can reveal a weakness before the next occurrence causes harm.

### 6. Risk awareness does not mean guessing technical limits
Learners should recognise the boundary between observation and specialist judgement. You can identify that a damaged cable, unstable stack, missing guard or uncontrolled dust condition needs attention without inventing a technical solution. Exposure limits, electrical repairs, machine modifications, specialist respiratory protection, structural decisions and other regulated or technical matters may require competent persons, approved procedures, manufacturer information or formal assessment.

The professional rule is: **do not convert uncertainty into confidence.** When the hazard cannot be safely controlled with the information, competence and authority available, stop and escalate.

## Worked Example / Demonstration

**Scenario:** A learner is preparing to cut several pieces of timber at a workstation. An extension lead crosses the normal walking route, offcuts are lying near the operator's feet, another learner is stacking boards close to the work area, and the dust extraction system is not running.

A poor response would be to put on safety glasses and begin cutting. PPE has not corrected the unsafe environment.

A better risk-thinking process is:
1. Define the task and operating zone.
2. Identify the trip obstruction, poor housekeeping, nearby person/material movement and unavailable dust control as hazards or changed conditions.
3. Stop the task before starting the cutting operation.
4. Remove or safely reroute the walkway obstruction according to workshop arrangements, clear the working area, coordinate with the other learner and restore the required dust-control system or report the fault.
5. Complete the normal tool/machine and material pre-use checks.
6. Use the required work method and PPE only after the work area and controls are satisfactory.

The important lesson is that safety is a system. Wearing PPE does not compensate for a badly organised task.

## Practical Activity — Workshop Hazard Scan
Choose a real workshop, training area, workplace photograph, or a room arranged as a simulated work area. Do not operate machinery for this activity.

Create a table with the headings: **Task/Area | Hazard | Who may be harmed | Possible harm | Existing control | Additional action needed | Escalate?**

Identify at least six hazards covering at least four different categories, such as mechanical, electrical, housekeeping, dust/chemical, manual handling, fire or people/traffic. For each hazard, state a sensible control priority. Mark any item that would require a competent person, supervisor or authorised workplace procedure rather than learner improvisation.

Keep the completed hazard scan as portfolio evidence.

## Knowledge Check

**1. Which statement best describes a hazard?**
A. The percentage chance that an accident will happen
B. Anything with the potential to cause harm
C. Only a machine with a moving blade
D. An injury that has already occurred

**Correct answer: B.** A hazard is a source or situation with the potential to cause harm. Risk concerns likelihood and consequence under the actual conditions.

**2. A walkway is blocked by loose offcuts. Which control is generally stronger than placing a warning sign beside them?**
A. Remove the offcuts and restore the clear walkway
B. Wear hearing protection
C. Walk faster through the area
D. Ignore them if nobody has fallen yet

**Correct answer: A.** Removing the obstruction eliminates the immediate trip hazard. A warning is an administrative measure and does not remove the obstruction.

**3. A power tool begins making an unfamiliar vibration during use. What is the best general response?**
A. Continue until the current job is finished
B. Hold the tool more tightly and increase speed
C. Stop safely and follow the workplace procedure for inspection/reporting
D. Remove a guard to see the moving parts more clearly

**Correct answer: C.** Unexpected behaviour can indicate a fault or unsafe condition. The task should not continue until the issue has been addressed through the appropriate procedure.

## Key Takeaways
- A hazard has the potential to cause harm; risk considers the likelihood and possible severity of that harm under actual conditions.
- Carpentry hazards include mechanical, sharp-tool, electrical, dust/chemical, noise, manual-handling, fire, housekeeping and people-related hazards.
- Risk awareness begins before the task and continues while conditions change.
- Prefer eliminating or controlling hazards at source before relying on administrative controls and PPE alone.
- A missing, damaged or defeated control is not protection.
- Near misses provide useful warning information and should be handled according to workplace procedure.
- Stop and escalate when safe control requires competence, authority or technical information you do not have.

## Visual Learning Guidance
**Recommended visual:** An annotated carpentry workshop scene showing a guarded machine, extraction, clear pedestrian route, correctly stored timber, an obstructed walkway, a damaged lead example, an unstable stack and an unauthorised person entering a work zone.

**Caption:** *Scan the whole task environment. Hazards can come from machinery, material, electricity, dust, housekeeping and the movement of other people—not only from the tool in your hands.*
