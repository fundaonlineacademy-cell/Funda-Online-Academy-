# Carpentry — Professional Skills Short Course

## Module 1: Carpentry Practice, Workshop Safety & Professional Standards
### Lesson 7 of 8: Emergency, Fire & Incident Procedures

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. Identify common emergency situations that may arise in a carpentry workshop or work area.
2. Explain the correct priorities when discovering a fire, injury, dangerous equipment condition or other emergency.
3. Describe the purpose of alarms, evacuation routes, assembly points, first-aid arrangements and incident reporting.
4. Distinguish between actions that an ordinary worker may safely take and situations that require evacuation, emergency services or a competent authorised person.
5. Explain why incidents and near misses should be reported, preserved and reviewed according to workplace procedures and applicable requirements.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Carpentry combines timber, electrical equipment, cutting tools, dust, coatings, adhesives and sometimes flammable products. A small event can therefore become serious if workers react slowly or incorrectly. Professional carpenters do not wait for an emergency before deciding what to do. They learn the workplace alarm, escape routes, assembly point, emergency contacts, first-aid arrangements and reporting procedure before work begins.

Emergency knowledge protects people first. It can also reduce property damage, prevent a second injury, help emergency responders and preserve reliable information for an investigation. In South Africa, occupational-health-and-safety requirements include duties concerning safe workplaces, fire precautions and reporting of specified serious incidents. Workplace procedures may impose additional requirements. Learners must therefore follow the emergency arrangements applicable to the actual workplace rather than assuming that one procedure fits every site.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary

**Emergency** — An unexpected situation requiring immediate action to protect people, property or the environment from serious harm.

**Evacuation** — The organised movement of people away from a dangerous area to a safer location, normally by designated escape routes.

**Means of egress** — A route or exit provided so people can leave a workplace safely during an emergency.

**Assembly point** — A designated safe location where evacuated persons gather so that accountability and further instructions can be managed.

**Fire triangle** — A basic model showing that heat, fuel and oxygen support combustion. Removing an essential element can stop combustion, although real fire behaviour and firefighting decisions require appropriate training and equipment.

**Incident** — An unplanned work-related event that causes, or in some cases has the potential to cause, injury, illness, damage or dangerous loss of control.

**Near miss** — An unplanned event that did not cause injury or damage on that occasion but could reasonably have done so under slightly different circumstances.

**Competent person** — A person who has the appropriate knowledge, training, experience and authority required for the particular task or responsibility.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Prepare before an emergency happens
The safest emergency response begins before anything goes wrong. When entering an unfamiliar workshop or site, a worker should learn how an alarm is raised, where emergency exits are located, where the assembly point is, who the responsible supervisors or emergency personnel are and how emergency services are contacted. Workers should also know where first-aid resources and firefighting equipment are located without assuming they are authorised or trained to use every item.

Escape routes must not become storage areas. Timber, boards, toolboxes, cables, waste and unfinished work can obstruct movement exactly when people need to leave quickly. South Africa's 2025 General Safety Regulations include fire-precaution and means-of-egress requirements dealing with clear, rapidly usable exits and suitable firefighting equipment. For a learner, the practical principle is straightforward: never create an obstruction that could delay evacuation, and report an obstructed or unusable escape route immediately.

Emergency planning should also consider people who may need assistance. A workplace's own emergency plan determines roles and arrangements. A learner should not invent an evacuation role or physically attempt a rescue that creates an additional casualty. Follow the plan and instructions of authorised emergency personnel.

### 2. Recognise common carpentry emergencies
A carpentry emergency can include fire or smoke, serious cuts, crushing injuries, electrical shock, uncontrolled machinery, a chemical spill, structural collapse, a person becoming unconscious, or another event presenting immediate danger. Warning signs may be obvious, such as visible flames, but sometimes they are subtle: an unusual burning smell, sparking equipment, smoke from a motor, a damaged electrical lead, a machine that will not stop normally, or someone showing signs of serious illness.

The first decision is not 'How do I fix this?' It is 'What immediate danger exists, and how do I prevent more people from being harmed?' Stop work where it is safe to do so, warn others, raise the alarm using the workplace method and move away from danger when evacuation is required. Do not re-enter a dangerous area simply to retrieve tools, a phone or personal belongings.

### 3. Fire response and evacuation
Timber and many workshop materials can provide fuel for a fire. Fine combustible dust and some finishing products introduce additional hazards that require workplace-specific controls. Fire prevention therefore starts with housekeeping, controlled storage, safe electrical/tool practices and correct handling of products.

If a fire or suspected fire occurs, follow the site's alarm and evacuation procedure. The safest action for an untrained person is generally to raise the alarm, alert people at risk and evacuate by the designated route. Fire extinguishers are not permission to fight a fire. Their safe selection and use depend on the fire, equipment, environment and the person's training. A learner should never delay evacuation or place themselves between a fire and their escape route in an attempt to save property.

At the assembly point, follow the workplace accountability process. Do not leave the assembly area without informing the responsible person where the plan requires accountability. Missing-person information may affect emergency response decisions.

### 4. Injury and first response
When someone is injured, first ensure that approaching them will not expose you to the same hazard. A person injured by machinery, electricity, fire, falling material or chemicals may still be in a dangerous environment. Raise the alarm and obtain appropriate assistance. If machinery is involved, follow authorised emergency isolation arrangements rather than reaching into moving equipment.

First aid should be provided by a suitably trained person within the scope of their training. Untrained workers can still make an important contribution by calling for help, keeping the area clear, directing responders and providing accurate information. Do not give treatment beyond your competence simply because you want to help.

### 5. Preserve the scene where required
After a serious incident, people may instinctively begin cleaning or moving objects. This can destroy evidence needed to determine what happened. Section 24 of South Africa's Occupational Health and Safety Act identifies categories of incidents that must be reported to an inspector and also restricts disturbance of the scene in certain severe cases. The Act allows necessary action to prevent another incident, rescue people or remove injured or deceased persons.

A learner is not expected to make every legal classification. The professional rule is to report serious incidents immediately to the responsible supervisor and follow authorised instructions. Do not rearrange guards, tools, controls or materials to make the scene look better. Accurate evidence helps investigators distinguish root causes from assumptions.

### 6. Report incidents and near misses
Not every event reaches the legal threshold for external reporting, but workplace reporting is still important. A near miss may reveal a failed control before someone is injured. For example, a board falling from poor storage but missing a worker by centimetres is valuable warning evidence. Reporting it allows the storage method to be corrected.

A useful internal report records factual information: date and time, location, activity being performed, people involved or witnesses, equipment/materials involved, what was observed, immediate actions taken and who was notified. Avoid blame, exaggeration or guessing. Investigation should establish causes and corrective actions using evidence.

### 7. Learn from the event
An emergency procedure is not complete when work restarts. Organisations should learn from incidents, drills and near misses. Questions may include: Did the alarm work? Were exits clear? Did everyone know the assembly point? Was emergency equipment accessible? Did workers understand their roles? Did an earlier hazard report go unresolved?

Corrective action should address the underlying cause where practicable. If a blocked exit contributed to delayed evacuation, simply telling workers to 'be more careful' is weaker than changing storage arrangements, responsibilities and inspection routines so the exit remains clear.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A learner is sanding timber when they smell burning from a nearby extension connection and see light smoke. Another worker says, 'Finish the board first; it is probably nothing.'

A professional response is to stop work safely and treat the warning as potentially serious. The learner should warn others and follow the workshop procedure for reporting the electrical/fire hazard and raising an alarm if required. If evacuation is instructed or there is immediate danger, the learner leaves by the designated route and goes to the assembly point. They do not handle damaged electrical equipment unless trained and authorised, do not throw water on an electrical source, and do not return for tools.

Afterwards, the event should be reported and the defective equipment kept out of service until appropriately assessed. The lesson is that early reporting can prevent a warning sign from developing into a larger emergency.

## Practical Activity — Create an Emergency Readiness Map
For your learner portfolio, draw a simple plan of a real or imagined carpentry workshop. Mark:
- normal work areas;
- at least two possible exit/escape directions where the actual layout allows;
- the designated or proposed assembly point;
- alarm/reporting point or method;
- first-aid location;
- firefighting-equipment locations (without assuming you are authorised to use them);
- two areas where stored material could become an evacuation obstruction;
- emergency contact/reporting information that would need to be displayed.

Then write five actions you would take after discovering smoke from an electrical tool. If you are using a real workplace, verify your map against the workplace's authorised emergency plan rather than changing emergency arrangements yourself.

## Knowledge Check

### Question 1
You hear the fire alarm while working. Your phone is on a bench in another room. What is the best action?

A. Collect the phone before leaving.
B. Follow the designated evacuation procedure immediately.
C. Wait until you see flames.
D. Continue working until a supervisor reaches you personally.

**Answer: B.** An alarm requires the response specified by the workplace emergency procedure. Retrieving property or waiting for visible flames can delay safe evacuation.

### Question 2
Why should a near miss be reported even when nobody was injured?

A. Only so someone can be blamed.
B. Because every near miss must automatically be reported to external emergency services.
C. Because it can reveal a hazard or failed control before a more serious event occurs.
D. Near misses should not be reported.

**Answer: C.** Near-miss information supports prevention. Internal reporting requirements and external statutory reporting are not necessarily the same.

### Question 3
A serious machinery incident has occurred. Why should the scene not be casually cleaned or rearranged?

A. The workshop must always remain closed permanently.
B. Evidence may be needed for investigation and, for certain serious incidents, legal requirements restrict disturbance of the scene.
C. Tools may never be used again after any incident.
D. Only photographs matter.

**Answer: B.** Evidence can help establish what happened. Necessary rescue and prevention actions take priority, while subsequent handling follows authorised procedures and applicable requirements.

### Question 4
What should an untrained worker do when a fire extinguisher is available during a developing fire?

A. Always attempt to extinguish the fire.
B. Ignore the alarm because an extinguisher exists.
C. Follow the emergency procedure and do not attempt firefighting beyond their training, authority or safe escape conditions.
D. Move closer to identify the exact flame colour.

**Answer: C.** Equipment availability does not make firefighting safe for every person or situation. Life safety and evacuation take priority.

## Visual Learning Guidance
**Recommended visual:** An annotated carpentry-workshop emergency diagram showing work zones, two escape directions, unobstructed exits, alarm point, first-aid location, firefighting equipment and assembly point.

**Caption:** *Emergency routes and equipment must remain visible and accessible. Learn the authorised workplace plan before an emergency occurs; never use an escape route as a storage area.*

## Key Takeaways
- Learn the workplace emergency plan before starting work.
- Raise the alarm and protect people before property or production.
- Keep escape routes and exits unobstructed.
- Firefighting and first aid must remain within the person's training and authority.
- Serious incidents may have statutory reporting and scene-preservation requirements.
- Report near misses because they provide opportunities to correct hazards before someone is harmed.
- Record facts accurately and use incident learning to strengthen controls.

This lesson teaches general emergency principles and does not replace a workplace emergency plan, fire-service instruction, first-aid training or legal advice.
