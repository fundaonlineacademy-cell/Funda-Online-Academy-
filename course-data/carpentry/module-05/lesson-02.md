# Carpentry — Professional Skills Short Course

## Module 5: Timber, Boards & Material Selection
### Lesson 2: Softwoods, Hardwoods & Common Applications

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. Distinguish softwoods from hardwoods using botanical origin rather than assuming that the names describe hardness.
2. Describe practical characteristics that can influence the selection of solid timber for carpentry work.
3. Identify common applications of selected softwoods and hardwoods used in woodworking and construction contexts.
4. Compare timber options against a job brief, service environment, appearance, workability, availability and cost.
5. Explain why a carpenter should verify the actual grade, condition, treatment and specification of timber rather than selecting material by species name alone.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A carpenter does not choose timber simply because one species is familiar or because a board looks attractive. Material choice affects machining, joint quality, appearance, durability, cost, waste, finishing and whether the completed item is suitable for its intended use. A learner who understands the difference between softwood and hardwood—and also understands the limitations of that classification—can make better material decisions and communicate more professionally with supervisors, suppliers and clients.

South African Department of Basic Education woodworking material classifies solid wood into hardwoods and softwoods and specifically warns that hardwood is not always hard and softwood is not always soft. The same learning material gives examples such as South African pine as a softwood and Saligna and Meranti as hardwoods, with different carpentry, construction, furniture, flooring, joinery and other applications.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary

**Softwood** — Solid wood obtained botanically from coniferous trees. The word describes the tree group, not a guaranteed level of physical softness.

**Hardwood** — Solid wood obtained botanically from broadleaf trees. The classification does not mean that every hardwood is harder, stronger or more durable than every softwood.

**Species** — A particular biological type of tree. Different timber species can have different colour, grain, density, workability, movement, durability and finishing behaviour.

**Workability** — How readily a material can be cut, planed, drilled, shaped, joined and finished using appropriate tools and methods.

**Density** — The amount of mass contained in a given volume of material. In carpentry it can influence handling, machining and some performance characteristics, but density alone does not determine whether timber is suitable for a job.

**Durability** — The ability of timber to resist deterioration under the service conditions for which it is used. Durability depends on species characteristics, exposure, detailing, treatment and other factors.

**Grade** — A classification used to indicate that timber meets defined requirements for a particular purpose or quality category. The carpenter must use the grade or specification required by the job rather than assuming all boards of one species are equivalent.

**Service conditions** — The environmental and use conditions the completed timber will experience, such as indoor or outdoor exposure, moisture, wear, loading and contact with other materials.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Softwood and hardwood are botanical groups
The first rule is to avoid interpreting the words *softwood* and *hardwood* too literally. Softwoods generally come from coniferous trees, while hardwoods come from broadleaf trees. This classification is useful because the groups often have recognisable growth structures and commercial patterns, but it is not a simple strength scale.

A carpenter therefore should not say, “This is hardwood, so it must be stronger,” or, “This is softwood, so it cannot be used for serious construction.” The correct question is whether the particular timber, in its actual grade and condition, satisfies the requirements of the job.

### 2. Softwoods in carpentry
Softwoods are widely used in building, carpentry and joinery. South African pine is a familiar example in local training material. DBE material lists applications including carpentry, building-site construction, shelving, joinery, furniture and other timber products.

Softwood can be attractive for many projects because suitable stock may be readily available and comparatively economical, and many softwoods can be machined efficiently with correctly maintained tools. However, the carpenter must still inspect every piece. Knots, splits, distortion, resinous areas, damaged edges, poor storage or inappropriate moisture condition can affect the result.

Where timber is intended for structural, exterior, treated or otherwise controlled work, the required grade, treatment and project specification must be followed. A generic statement such as “pine is fine” is not enough to approve material for every use.

### 3. Hardwoods in carpentry
Hardwoods include a wide range of species with very different characteristics. DBE woodworking material gives Saligna and Meranti as examples and associates them with uses such as carpentry, flooring, cabinetmaking, furniture, mouldings, panelling and joinery.

Some hardwoods may be selected for appearance, wear resistance, finishing qualities or particular joinery applications. Others may be relatively easy to work. Because the group is diverse, the word *hardwood* by itself does not tell the carpenter how the material will cut, whether it is durable outdoors, whether it will accept a particular finish, or whether it is suitable for a structural component.

The professional approach is species-specific and job-specific: identify the material, understand the required performance, inspect the actual stock and follow the drawing, specification, supplier information and workplace procedure.

### 4. Compare material properties with the job
Material selection begins with the job brief. A carpenter should consider questions such as:

- What is the component required to do?
- Is it structural, decorative, protective or a combination?
- Will it be indoors, outdoors or exposed to changing moisture?
- What dimensions and tolerances are required?
- Will the surface be painted, stained, clear-coated or concealed?
- What machining and joining operations are required?
- Is a particular species, grade or treatment specified?
- What material is locally available within the approved budget?

For example, timber for a painted indoor utility shelf may be selected differently from timber for visible furniture, exterior joinery or a load-bearing component. A professional carpenter does not substitute material simply because another board has approximately the same dimensions.

### 5. Appearance is only one selection factor
Colour and grain pattern can be important, especially for exposed furniture and joinery, but appearance cannot replace technical suitability. Two boards of the same species may look different because of natural variation, growth pattern, cut orientation, defects or previous storage. When matching visible components, the carpenter may need to consider grain direction, colour consistency and how adjoining pieces will look after finishing.

For concealed components, appearance may be less important than straightness, dimensional stability, grade, condition and cost. The job specification determines the priority.

### 6. Workability affects process and quality
Different timbers behave differently during sawing, planing, routing, drilling, fastening, sanding and finishing. Grain direction, knots, density and other characteristics can influence cutting resistance, tear-out, splitting and surface quality.

The response should not be to force the tool. The carpenter should check the material, cutting direction, tool condition, accessory selection, workholding and approved process. Tool manufacturers' instructions and workplace procedures remain important. Material knowledge helps the carpenter anticipate behaviour, but it does not justify unsafe improvisation.

### 7. Species name does not replace inspection
Even if the correct species has been supplied, the actual piece may still be unsuitable. Inspect for obvious defects, distortion, damage, contamination, unsuitable dimensions and other conditions relevant to the job. Confirm grade and treatment where these are specified. If identification, grading or treatment status is uncertain, isolate the material from the job and obtain clarification rather than guessing.

This is especially important when material performance has safety, structural or durability consequences.

### 8. Material selection is also a cost and waste decision
Using an unnecessarily expensive or scarce timber can increase project cost without improving the result. Using unsuitable cheap timber can cause rework, failure and even greater waste. Good selection balances fitness for purpose, specification, quality, availability, responsible use and cost.

A carpenter should also plan how boards will be allocated to components. Clear, straight sections may be reserved for critical visible or accurately machined pieces, while acceptable sections with less important appearance may be used where the specification permits. Defective or prohibited material must never be hidden inside the project merely to avoid waste.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A workshop must make two items: a painted indoor storage shelf and a visible clear-finished small cabinet.

The learner first checks the drawings and material specification. For the utility shelf, approved South African pine may be suitable where the specification allows it. The learner inspects the stock for straightness, serious defects, damage and usable dimensions before planning cuts.

For the cabinet, the job specifies an approved hardwood because the exposed appearance and finishing requirement are different. The learner does not simply choose the darkest board. Pieces are compared for grain, colour, condition, machining requirements and component location. The most visually compatible boards are allocated to adjoining visible parts.

The lesson from the example is that neither “softwood” nor “hardwood” is enough information by itself. Selection follows the job requirement and the actual material condition.

## Practical Activity
### Timber Selection Comparison Card
Under supervision, examine at least one available softwood sample and one hardwood sample. If physical samples are not available, use instructor-approved photographs or supplier samples.

For each sample record:
1. Known or supplied species/type.
2. Hardwood or softwood classification.
3. Visible grain characteristics.
4. Visible defects or irregularities.
5. Likely workability considerations.
6. One suitable application and why it may be suitable.
7. One application for which you would require more information before approving the material.
8. What specification, grade, treatment or service-condition information you would verify before use.

Finish with a short comparison explaining why botanical classification alone cannot determine the best timber for a job. Keep the completed card as portfolio evidence.

## Knowledge Check

### Question 1
Which statement about hardwood and softwood is most accurate?

A. Every hardwood is harder than every softwood.  
B. Softwood cannot be used in construction.  
C. The terms primarily describe botanical groups and do not guarantee hardness.  
D. Hardwood is always the more professional choice.

**Correct answer: C**

**Feedback:** Hardwood generally comes from broadleaf trees and softwood from conifers. The classification should not be treated as a universal hardness or quality ranking.

### Question 2
A drawing specifies a particular timber grade for a component, but the carpenter has an attractive board of the same species with unknown grade. What is the best action?

A. Use it because the species is correct.  
B. Use it if the colour matches.  
C. Confirm that the board meets the required grade before using it.  
D. Sand it until the grade becomes acceptable.

**Correct answer: C**

**Feedback:** Species identification does not prove compliance with a required grade or specification.

### Question 3
Why might a carpenter allocate selected boards differently within the same project?

A. To hide unacceptable defects.  
B. Because visible, critical and concealed components can have different specification and appearance priorities.  
C. Because measurements are unnecessary when selecting timber.  
D. To make all components from different species.

**Correct answer: B**

**Feedback:** Material planning can improve quality and reduce waste, provided every component still meets its specification.

### Question 4
Which is the best material-selection process?

A. Choose the cheapest timber available.  
B. Choose hardwood for every project.  
C. Match the actual timber's species, grade, condition and properties to the job specification and service conditions.  
D. Select only by colour and grain.

**Correct answer: C**

**Feedback:** Professional selection considers the complete requirement rather than relying on one characteristic.

## Feedback / Explanation for the Knowledge Check
The central decision rule is **identify → specify → inspect → compare → select → verify**. Begin with the job requirement, identify the timber correctly, inspect the actual stock and verify controlled requirements such as grade or treatment. Hardwood and softwood are useful categories, but neither category guarantees suitability.

## Key Takeaways
1. Hardwood and softwood are botanical classifications, not universal hardness rankings.
2. South African pine is a common softwood example; Saligna and Meranti are hardwood examples used in DBE woodworking material.
3. Different species can vary in appearance, workability, movement, durability and other properties.
4. Material selection must start with the job brief and specification.
5. Species name alone does not prove grade, treatment, condition or fitness for purpose.
6. Visible appearance matters for some work but cannot replace technical suitability.
7. Careful stock allocation can improve quality and reduce avoidable waste.
8. When a controlled material requirement is uncertain, verify it rather than guessing.
