# Carpentry — Professional Skills Short Course

## Module 5: Timber, Boards & Material Selection
### Lesson 7: Timber Storage, Handling & Conditioning

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. Explain why correct storage and handling protect timber quality, safety and project cost.
2. Identify practical requirements for stable, organised timber and board storage.
3. Explain how support, airflow, moisture exposure and storage conditions can influence timber condition.
4. Apply safe principles when receiving, moving, stacking and retrieving timber and sheet materials.
5. Explain the purpose of conditioning material before accurate manufacture or installation where required.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Good material can become poor material before a carpenter ever cuts it. Timber that is left directly on a damp floor, supported unevenly, exposed to rain, mixed carelessly with unsuitable stock or damaged during handling may warp, stain, split, swell or become difficult to identify. Large boards can also create serious manual-handling and storage hazards if they are unstable or moved without planning.

Professional carpentry therefore includes material care from the moment stock is received until the finished component is made. Correct storage helps preserve dimensions, surfaces, edges and material identification. It reduces avoidable waste and makes the workplace easier to manage. Historical South African occupational standards for carpentry and wood products explicitly include the selection, stacking and storage of materials, while timber-storage standards emphasise designated storage areas, stable stacking, safe work conditions and minimising material damage.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary

**Stack** — An organised group of timber or boards placed together for storage. A safe stack must be stable and arranged so that material can be identified and retrieved without uncontrolled movement.

**Sticker** — A narrow spacer placed between layers of timber where a storage or drying method requires separation and airflow. Stickers must be positioned consistently according to the applicable storage or drying procedure.

**Conditioning** — Allowing material time in or near its intended working environment so that its condition can become more suitable for accurate manufacture or installation. The required method and duration depend on the material, supplier instructions, project specification and environment.

**Airflow** — Movement of air around stored material. Controlled airflow can be important where timber is being stored or conditioned, but the required arrangement depends on the material and purpose.

**Warp** — Unwanted distortion such as bow, cup, spring/crook or twist. Poor support, moisture change and unsuitable storage can contribute to distortion.

**Stock rotation** — An organised method of managing stored material so that stock is identifiable and older or designated material is used according to workplace procedure rather than forgotten in storage.

**Designated storage area** — A workplace location approved for storing a particular material. It should support safe access, identification, protection and handling.

**Manual handling** — Moving, lifting, carrying, lowering, pushing or positioning material by human effort. Long timber and large sheet goods may require more than one person or suitable handling equipment according to the workplace risk assessment and procedure.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Storage is part of material quality control
A carpenter should not think of storage as simply finding an empty corner. Storage is a quality-control activity. Before material is placed in a rack, stack or designated area, the worker should know what it is, what condition it is in and what project or stock category it belongs to.

When timber arrives, obvious damage should be identified before it disappears into the stack. Check, as appropriate, for distortion, splits, damaged edges, contamination, unexpected moisture exposure, incorrect dimensions, incorrect material, missing identification and other visible problems. Manufactured boards should also be checked for damaged corners, swollen edges, surface damage or delamination where relevant.

Recording or reporting damage at receipt is better than discovering it after components have been marked out.

### 2. Keep material supported and stable
Stored timber needs appropriate support. Long pieces placed on inadequate or uneven supports can sag or distort. A stack built on an unstable base can shift or collapse. Material should therefore be stored in the workplace's designated system—such as racks, bearers, shelves or approved stacks—and the support arrangement should suit the material.

The base should be stable. Supports should be positioned so that the load is carried without creating unnecessary distortion. Where layers are separated by stickers, the stickers should be aligned and spaced according to the relevant procedure so that the load is transferred consistently through the stack.

Never create a stack that depends on luck to remain upright. Do not lean long, heavy material in an improvised position where it can slide or fall. Do not climb unstable stacks to retrieve stock. If safe access requires handling equipment or assistance, use the authorised method.

### 3. Protect timber from unsuitable moisture exposure
Lesson 3 explained that wood is hygroscopic and can exchange moisture with the surrounding environment. Storage conditions therefore matter.

Timber should normally be protected from unintended wetting, ground moisture and conditions that conflict with the material specification. Placing material directly on a damp floor can expose it to moisture and contamination. Rainwater, plumbing leaks or condensation can affect timber and some manufactured boards severely.

However, 'keep it dry' is not a complete technical specification. Different products and projects can have different storage requirements. Treated timber, kiln-dried joinery timber, structural timber, veneered board and moisture-resistant board are not automatically stored in exactly the same way. Follow supplier instructions, product information, project requirements and workplace procedures.

### 4. Airflow and stickered storage
Where timber is being dried, conditioned or stored in a way that requires airflow, separation between layers may be necessary. Stickers can create air spaces between boards. Their position matters: inconsistent or poorly aligned stickers can concentrate loads and contribute to distortion.

A learner should not invent sticker spacing or drying schedules. Kiln drying and controlled seasoning are specialised processes. The carpenter's responsibility is to understand the principle and follow the approved method.

If timber is already properly conditioned for a project, careless restacking can undo part of that quality control. Maintain the required support and protection until the material is used.

### 5. Store manufactured boards deliberately
Sheet materials present different challenges from narrow solid timber. A full sheet can be heavy, awkward and vulnerable to edge or surface damage. The correct storage orientation depends on the product and approved storage system.

Boards stored flat require a sufficiently flat and supportive base. Boards stored in an approved vertical or near-vertical rack must be restrained and supported so that they cannot fall or bend excessively. Do not simply lean sheets against a wall because it is convenient.

Protect finished or decorative faces from scratching. Keep board types and thicknesses identifiable. If the product has manufacturer-specific storage requirements, those instructions take priority over a generic rule.

### 6. Safe handling begins before lifting
Before moving material, plan the route. Ask:
- Where is the material going?
- Is the route clear?
- Is the load too long, heavy or awkward for one person?
- Are there doorways, stairs, machinery, people or fragile objects in the route?
- Is suitable handling equipment required?
- Where will the material be placed when the move is complete?

Long timber can strike people or objects when the carrier turns. Large boards can block the carrier's view and catch wind outdoors. Sharp or damaged edges can cause injury. Good handling therefore requires communication and controlled movement.

Use team lifting or authorised mechanical assistance where required by the workplace assessment and procedure. A learner should not guess a universal maximum manual-lifting weight. Safe handling depends on the load, posture, frequency, route, individual capability and workplace controls.

### 7. Retrieve stock without destabilising the remainder
Storage remains safe only if material is removed safely. Pulling a board from underneath a heavy stack, removing a restraint too early or leaving unsupported pieces after retrieval can create a new hazard.

Select the required material deliberately. Confirm the species or board type, grade where relevant, dimensions, quantity and condition before moving it to production. Re-secure or reorganise the remaining stock so that the storage area remains stable and orderly.

Good stock organisation also reduces unnecessary handling. If material can be identified quickly, workers do not need to move several heavy pieces simply to find one suitable board.

### 8. Conditioning before accurate work
Material sometimes needs time to adjust to the environment in which it will be manufactured or installed. This is particularly important where dimensional stability and appearance matter.

Conditioning does not mean leaving timber anywhere for an arbitrary number of days. The correct approach depends on the product, its starting condition, intended service environment, supplier guidance and job specification. Where moisture content is important, a competent person may use an appropriate moisture-measuring method and compare the result with the specified requirement.

The professional principle is: **verify rather than assume**. If material has just arrived from very different storage conditions and the job requires close-fitting components, investigate whether conditioning is required before final machining.

### 9. Storage supports cost control and sustainability
Damaged material is expensive twice: the business pays for the material and then pays again through replacement, labour, delay or rework. Correct storage therefore supports profitability and environmental responsibility.

Organise offcuts and reusable material according to workplace policy. Do not mix valuable reusable stock with waste. At the same time, do not keep unusable scrap indefinitely where it blocks access or creates a fire, trip or housekeeping problem.

A well-managed store should make it easy to answer: What material do we have? What condition is it in? Where is it? Is it suitable for the next job?

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A workshop receives several lengths of prepared timber for cabinet frames and a delivery of sheet board for cabinet panels. The timber will not be machined until later in the week.

A poor approach would be to place the timber directly on the workshop floor and lean the boards against the nearest wall. This exposes the stock to contamination, uneven support, impact damage and unstable storage.

A professional approach is to:
1. Compare the delivery with the order or cutting requirements.
2. Inspect the stock for obvious damage and identify discrepancies.
3. Separate and label different materials and dimensions.
4. Move the material using a planned route and suitable assistance.
5. Place timber on the designated supported storage system.
6. Store sheet material in the approved board-storage system with faces and edges protected.
7. Protect all stock from unsuitable moisture exposure.
8. Maintain clear access around the storage area.
9. Before final machining, verify whether the material requires conditioning or moisture confirmation for the project.

The important lesson is that storage is connected directly to the accuracy and quality of the final cabinet.

## Knowledge Check

**1. Why should timber be stored on suitable, stable supports?**
A. To make every piece darker in colour
B. To reduce uncontrolled distortion and maintain a stable stack
C. To remove the need for inspection
D. To guarantee all timber has identical moisture content

**Correct answer: B.**
**Feedback:** Proper support protects both material condition and stack stability. It does not guarantee identical moisture content.

**2. A large sheet is awkward for one learner to carry and blocks the learner's view. What is the best response?**
A. Carry it faster so the task finishes sooner
B. Drag it by one corner
C. Plan the route and use suitable assistance or authorised handling equipment
D. Cut it into pieces before checking the cutting list

**Correct answer: C.**
**Feedback:** Handling should be planned around the load and environment. Assistance or equipment may be required.

**3. Why might timber need conditioning before final machining?**
A. To allow it to adjust appropriately to relevant environmental conditions where the project requires it
B. To change hardwood into softwood
C. To remove every natural defect
D. To eliminate the need for accurate measurement

**Correct answer: A.**
**Feedback:** Conditioning can support dimensional stability. The required method depends on the material and project.

**4. What should a carpenter do if a board manufacturer's storage instruction differs from a generic workshop habit?**
A. Ignore the manufacturer
B. Use whichever method requires less space
C. Follow the applicable product instructions and workplace/project requirements
D. Store every board vertically

**Correct answer: C.**
**Feedback:** Product-specific and approved requirements take priority over unsupported generic assumptions.

## Feedback / Explanation for Knowledge Check
The central decision pattern is:

**identify → inspect → plan handling → support → protect → organise → condition/verify where required → retrieve safely**.

Storage is successful when it protects people, preserves material condition and allows stock to be identified and used efficiently.

## Key Takeaways
1. Timber storage is part of professional quality control, not merely housekeeping.
2. Stable support helps protect both workers and material condition.
3. Timber and boards should be protected from unintended moisture exposure and contamination.
4. Stickered storage and airflow must follow the relevant storage or drying procedure.
5. Sheet materials require deliberate support and safe handling because they can be heavy and awkward.
6. Manual handling should be planned; there is no single universal safe lifting weight for every situation.
7. Conditioning may be necessary before accurate manufacture, but its requirements must come from the material, project or workplace specification.
8. Good storage reduces damage, rework, waste and unnecessary cost.
