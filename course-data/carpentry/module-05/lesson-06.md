# Carpentry — Professional Skills Short Course

## Module 5: Timber, Boards & Material Selection
### Lesson 6: Selecting Materials for Purpose & Service Conditions

**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. Explain why carpentry materials must be selected for the intended purpose rather than by appearance or price alone.
2. Identify important service conditions that influence timber and board selection.
3. Compare candidate materials against a job specification, required quality, exposure, dimensions, finish and fixing method.
4. Recognise when a proposed material substitution requires clarification or approval.
5. Apply a structured material-selection process that supports quality, safety, economy and minimum waste.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A carpenter may work accurately and still produce an unacceptable product if the wrong material is selected. A board that performs well in a protected interior may not be suitable for a damp or exposed location. A visually attractive piece of timber may contain defects in a critical area. A cheaper material can become expensive when it causes excessive waste, difficult machining, poor fixing, rework or premature failure.

Professional material selection therefore begins with the job requirement. The carpenter must understand what the component has to do, where it will be used, what conditions it will experience, how it will be manufactured and finished, and what the approved specification requires. South African vocational carpentry standards have consistently linked material selection to specifications, material properties, quality, quantity, grade and cost-effective use. Where a specification is unclear or a substitute is proposed, the learner should not silently redesign the job.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary

**Service conditions** — The environmental and use conditions that a material or finished component is expected to experience, such as indoor or outdoor location, moisture exposure, wear, loading and temperature changes.

**Specification** — The approved information that defines what is required for a job or component. It may include drawings, dimensions, material type, grade, finish, quantity and other requirements.

**Suitability** — The degree to which a material's properties and condition make it appropriate for a particular purpose.

**Durability** — The ability of a material or product to remain fit for its intended purpose under the relevant conditions over time. Durability depends on the material, design, protection, exposure and maintenance; it should not be assumed from a timber name alone.

**Grade** — A classification used to indicate that material meets defined quality or performance criteria. Where a specified grade is required, the carpenter must use the specified grade rather than make an informal visual substitution.

**Compatibility** — The ability of materials, fixings, adhesives, coatings, tools or processes to be used together appropriately without creating unacceptable performance or quality problems.

**Substitution** — Replacing a specified material with a different material. A substitution must be authorised where the drawing, specification, client requirement or workplace procedure requires approval.

**Whole-job cost** — The practical cost of using a material after considering purchase price together with waste, labour, machining, finishing, replacement and rework implications.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Start with the purpose, not the material rack
Material selection should begin by asking what the finished component must achieve. A carpenter selecting material for a decorative indoor panel has a different problem from one selecting material for a structural frame, an exterior component, a wet-area fitting or a frequently handled counter.

Before selecting stock, identify the component, its dimensions, its function, its location and the approved material requirement. Read the drawing, cutting list, job card or other authorised instruction. If the specification identifies a particular material or grade, that requirement is the starting point. Do not replace it simply because another material is cheaper, easier to machine or currently available.

A useful first question is: **What must this component do, and under what conditions must it continue to do it?**

### 2. Identify the service conditions
Service conditions can change whether a material is suitable. Consider whether the component will be used indoors, outdoors or in a partly protected location. Consider the likelihood of moisture, repeated wetting, high humidity, direct weather, strong sunlight, abrasion, impact, frequent cleaning, heat sources or other relevant exposure.

Also consider the way the product will be used. A display panel may mainly need dimensional stability and an acceptable surface. A shelf may also need adequate stiffness for the specified span and load. A door component must remain functional and compatible with its fittings. A work surface may require resistance to repeated wear and a finish system suitable for its intended use.

Do not invent performance ratings. If the job requires a certified structural grade, a specific board classification, preservative treatment, fire performance, moisture resistance or other technical rating, verify the requirement from the approved specification, manufacturer information or competent workplace instruction.

### 3. Match material properties to the job
Once the service conditions are understood, compare material properties with the requirement. Relevant considerations may include:

- strength and stiffness where the component carries or transfers load;
- density and workability where machining and handling are important;
- grain direction and natural timber movement;
- resistance to the expected moisture or exposure conditions;
- ability to hold the specified fixings;
- compatibility with adhesives and finishes;
- surface quality and visual requirements;
- board edge characteristics;
- available dimensions and thicknesses;
- required grade and defect limits.

No single property should automatically decide the selection. A dense material is not automatically the best material. A smooth board is not automatically suitable for every cabinet or installation. A naturally attractive timber is not automatically acceptable for an exposed application. Selection is a comparison between the complete job requirement and the complete material condition.

### 4. Inspect the actual material, not only its label
Even when the correct material type has been supplied, the carpenter must inspect the actual stock. Check dimensions, straightness, surface condition, moisture-related signs, knots and other defects, damaged edges or faces, delamination or swelling in manufactured boards, contamination and any required grade or identification marks.

Material quality should be considered together with component position. A local defect may be acceptable in one non-critical offcut but unacceptable where a joint, fixing, visible face or highly stressed section will occur. The carpenter must work within the specification and workplace quality requirements rather than inventing personal defect limits.

### 5. Consider manufacturing and finishing
Material selection continues beyond the raw board or timber. Ask how the material will be cut, shaped, joined, fixed and finished. A material that cannot safely or effectively be processed using the approved equipment and method requires a different plan or clarification.

Consider whether the selected stock can produce the required finished dimensions while allowing for the approved preparation process. Do not invent machining allowances where none are specified. Check whether the intended fixing method is appropriate to the material and edge condition. Check whether the required coating or finish is compatible with the material and its service environment according to the product manufacturer's instructions.

Manufactured boards require particular attention to their product classification and intended use. Two boards that look similar may have different properties. Product names such as plywood, MDF, particleboard or OSB describe broad families, not a universal performance level. Use the actual product specification when exposure or performance matters.

### 6. Balance quality, economy and waste
Cost-effective selection does not mean buying the cheapest material. The carpenter should consider stock sizes, cutting-list quantities, defect position, reusable offcuts, machining requirements and the likelihood of rework. A slightly higher purchase price may be more economical if the material provides better yield or meets the specification without excessive correction.

Plan component layout so useful material is not unnecessarily discarded. At the same time, never reduce waste by placing an unacceptable defect in a critical component or by ignoring required grain direction, grade, dimensions or surface quality. Waste reduction must operate inside the quality and safety requirements of the job.

### 7. Control substitutions professionally
Sometimes the specified material is unavailable, unusually expensive or difficult to source. This does not automatically authorise substitution. A different species or board can change dimensions, weight, movement, machining, fixing, finishing, appearance, durability and cost.

A professional response is to identify the shortage, gather accurate information about the proposed alternative and refer the change to the authorised person where approval is required. Record the approved change according to workplace procedure. If a drawing or cutting list is revised, ensure the current revision is used before material is cut.

A useful rule is: **identify → compare → verify → approve → record → use**.

### 8. Use a repeatable material-selection workflow
For routine carpentry, use this sequence:

1. Read the approved job information.
2. Identify each component and its function.
3. Identify service conditions and exposure.
4. Confirm specified material, grade, dimensions and quantity.
5. Compare relevant material properties with the requirement.
6. Inspect the actual stock for quality and defects.
7. Check compatibility with machining, fixing, joining and finishing.
8. Plan stock use and component layout to reduce unnecessary waste.
9. Clarify discrepancies or proposed substitutions before cutting.
10. Record material selection or changes where the workplace system requires it.

This process turns material selection from guesswork into a quality-control decision.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A learner receives an approved cutting list for a small indoor freestanding storage unit. The specification identifies a particular 18 mm board product for the panels and solid timber of a stated size for the edging. The workshop has another cheaper board in stock that looks similar.

A poor approach would be to use the cheaper board because the colour and thickness appear close.

The professional approach is to check the approved specification and compare the available board's documented product type, thickness, surface, intended application, fixing requirements and finish compatibility. The learner also checks the sheets for damaged corners, swelling, surface defects and sufficient usable area for the cutting list. If the cheaper board is not the specified product, the learner does not substitute it without the required approval.

The learner then lays out the required components to use the sheet efficiently while protecting visible faces and avoiding damaged areas. The selection is based on purpose, specification, actual condition and efficient use—not appearance alone.

## Practical Activity — Material Selection Decision Record
Choose a simple approved carpentry project, drawing or instructor-provided scenario. Prepare a one-page decision record containing:

| Item | Record |
|---|---|
| Component | Name and purpose |
| Location | Indoor / outdoor / protected / other stated condition |
| Expected service conditions | Moisture, wear, load, appearance or other relevant factors |
| Specified material | Exact information provided by the job document |
| Required grade/classification | Record only if stated |
| Required dimensions/quantity | From drawing or cutting list |
| Material properties that matter | Explain why |
| Actual stock inspection | Defects, damage, dimensions, faces/edges, identification |
| Manufacturing considerations | Cutting, joining, fixing and finishing |
| Waste/yield consideration | How the stock can be used efficiently |
| Substitution required? | Yes / No |
| Approval or clarification | Record what would be required before changing specification |

Add a short paragraph explaining why your selected material is suitable. If essential information is missing, write **“Clarification required”** rather than inventing a requirement.

**Portfolio evidence:** Keep the completed decision record with the relevant drawing, scenario or cutting-list reference.

## Knowledge Check

### Question 1
What should normally be the first basis for selecting material for a carpentry component?

A. The cheapest material in the store
B. The approved job requirement and intended purpose
C. The material with the darkest colour
D. The material that is easiest to cut

**Correct answer: B**

**Feedback:** Material selection begins with the specification, component function and intended service conditions. Price and workability matter, but they cannot replace the job requirement.

### Question 2
A specified board is unavailable and another board of the same thickness looks similar. What is the best response?

A. Substitute it immediately because thickness is the same
B. Use it only for the visible parts
C. Compare its documented suitability and obtain required approval before substitution
D. Reduce the dimensions so the substitute becomes suitable

**Correct answer: C**

**Feedback:** Similar appearance or thickness does not prove equivalent performance. A substitution may affect machining, fixing, finishing, durability, movement and compliance with the specification.

### Question 3
Which statement best describes cost-effective material selection?

A. Always choose the lowest purchase price
B. Ignore defects if this reduces waste
C. Consider specification, usable yield, processing, quality and rework as well as purchase price
D. Use every offcut even when it does not meet the component requirement

**Correct answer: C**

**Feedback:** Whole-job economy considers whether the material meets the specification and how efficiently it can be converted into acceptable components without unnecessary waste or rework.

### Question 4
A carpenter notices that a piece of correctly specified timber has a serious defect exactly where an important joint must be cut. What is the best action?

A. Ignore the defect because the species is correct
B. Inspect and reposition or reject the piece according to the specification and workplace quality requirements
C. Fill the defect after assembly without checking
D. Increase clamping pressure to compensate

**Correct answer: B**

**Feedback:** Correct material type does not remove the need to inspect actual stock. Defect position can affect suitability for a particular component or joint.

## Feedback / Explanation for the Knowledge Check
The four questions follow the same professional decision chain: **requirement → service conditions → material properties → actual stock condition → manufacturing compatibility → economy → approval**. If any essential requirement is unknown, the correct professional response is to verify or escalate it rather than guess.

## Key Takeaways
- Select materials for the job they must perform, not appearance or price alone.
- Begin with the approved specification, drawing, cutting list or job instruction.
- Service conditions such as moisture, weather, wear and loading influence suitability.
- Material type, grade, dimensions, defects and actual stock condition all matter.
- Check compatibility with cutting, joining, fixing and finishing methods.
- A similar-looking material is not automatically an acceptable substitute.
- Reduce waste through planning, but never by ignoring quality, grade, safety or specification requirements.
- Record and obtain approval for substitutions where required.
