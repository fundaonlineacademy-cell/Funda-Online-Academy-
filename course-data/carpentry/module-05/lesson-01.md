# Carpentry — Professional Skills Short Course

## Module 5: Timber, Boards & Material Selection
### Lesson 1: Wood Structure, Grain & Basic Properties

**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. Explain why wood structure and grain influence carpentry decisions and finished quality.
2. Identify basic visible wood features including growth rings, grain direction, heartwood, sapwood and knots.
3. Explain how grain direction can influence cutting, planing, sanding, joining and finishing.
4. Describe basic wood properties relevant to material selection without assuming that every species behaves the same way.
5. Inspect a piece of timber systematically before marking, machining or assembly and record relevant observations.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Timber is not a perfectly uniform manufactured material. It grew as part of a tree, and its structure records that growth. Two boards of the same nominal size can differ in grain direction, colour, density, knots, moisture condition, straightness and machining behaviour. A carpenter who treats every piece as identical may experience tear-out, splitting, inaccurate joints, poor appearance, unnecessary waste or an unsuitable material choice.

Professional material selection therefore begins before the first cut. The carpenter studies the job specification and then studies the material. South African vocational woodworking material has historically required learners to understand wood structure, grain characteristics, timber properties and their influence on product quality. This lesson develops that practical foundation; later lessons will distinguish softwoods and hardwoods, timber movement, defects, manufactured boards and service-condition selection.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary

**Wood grain** — The visible and structural direction or arrangement of wood fibres and other tissues. Grain direction can influence appearance and the way a cutting tool interacts with the timber.

**Growth ring** — A visible band of wood produced during a period of tree growth. Growth-ring appearance varies between species and growing conditions.

**Heartwood** — The inner, generally older wood of a tree stem. Its colour and properties may differ from the outer sapwood, depending on species.

**Sapwood** — The younger outer wood of the stem that, while the tree is living, participates in movement and storage of water and nutrients.

**Knot** — A portion of a branch base incorporated into the wood as the tree grows. Knots vary greatly in size, condition and effect on appearance or performance.

**Figure** — The visual pattern seen on a wood surface as a result of grain, growth structure, colour and the way the board was cut.

**Density** — Mass per unit volume. In practical carpentry, differences in density can be associated with differences in weight and working behaviour, but species-specific performance must not be guessed from density alone.

**End grain** — The surface exposed when timber is cut across the general direction of its fibres. End grain behaves differently from long-grain surfaces during cutting, sanding, gluing and finishing.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Wood is a natural structural material

A timber board began as part of a living tree. Its cells and fibres developed in organised patterns that supported the tree and transported or stored substances during growth. When a log is converted into boards, those structures become visible as grain, growth-ring patterns, colour differences and other features. This is why timber should not be treated as if it were a completely homogeneous material.

For the carpenter, the important principle is practical: **look at the material before deciding how to work it**. The drawing may tell you the required component dimensions, but the board itself tells you where knots are located, how the grain runs, whether the piece is straight enough for the intended component, and whether an attractive face should be positioned where it will be visible.

Material selection should always remain connected to the specification. Attractive grain does not automatically make a board suitable for a structural or exterior application, and a visually plain board is not automatically inferior. Species, grade, moisture condition, treatment, service environment and the job specification may all matter. Never substitute visual judgement for a requirement stated in an approved drawing, schedule or material specification.

### 2. Reading the visible structure of timber

Start by examining the faces, edges and ends of the board. The end of a piece often shows growth rings clearly. The faces may show straight, curved, interlocking or irregular-looking grain patterns depending on the species and how the log was sawn.

Heartwood and sapwood may also be distinguishable by colour, although this is not equally obvious in every species. A carpenter should learn the terms without assuming that colour alone proves the species, strength, durability or treatment status of a board. Identification should use reliable material information, supplier grading or workplace procedures where those characteristics are important.

Knots are another natural feature. A knot records where a branch was connected to the tree. Some knots may be sound and acceptable for a particular product; others may be loose, cracked, large, poorly positioned or unacceptable under the specification. The correct decision depends on the intended component and acceptance criteria. Do not create a universal rule such as “all knots are bad” or “small knots are always acceptable.”

### 3. Grain direction affects tool behaviour

Grain direction is especially important when planing, chiselling, routing or performing other cutting operations. A cutting edge that meets fibres in an unfavourable direction can lift them ahead of the cut, producing tear-out rather than a clean surface. This does not mean there is one permanent direction that works for every face or every piece. Grain can reverse or change direction, particularly around knots or figured areas.

Before machining, inspect the grain and begin conservatively. Where appropriate, make a controlled trial on waste material or a non-critical area. If the surface begins tearing, stop and reconsider the direction, tool sharpness, cutting depth, support and method rather than forcing the tool.

Grain direction also matters when marking and cutting. A component with difficult grain near a narrow edge or joint may require more careful positioning. End grain usually requires different handling from long grain because the tool is cutting across fibre ends. It can splinter at unsupported exits, absorb some finishes differently, and require deliberate preparation.

### 4. Wood properties must be considered together

Carpenters often describe timber using properties such as hardness, density, strength, stiffness, workability, stability, appearance and durability. These terms are useful, but they should not be reduced to simplistic rules.

For example, a denser timber may feel heavier and may resist indentation differently from a lighter timber, but density alone does not tell the carpenter everything about structural suitability, exterior durability, movement, machining or finishing. Similarly, “hardwood” and “softwood” are botanical groupings and do not guarantee that every hardwood is harder than every softwood. That distinction is developed in Lesson 2.

The professional approach is to match verified material properties to the job requirements. Ask: What must this component do? Is appearance important? Will it carry load? Is it exposed to moisture or weather? Will it be machined into fine joints? What finish is specified? Are there grade, treatment or manufacturer requirements? The answer may involve several properties at once.

### 5. Grain and structure influence quality

Material characteristics can affect dimensional accuracy and surface quality. Grain around a knot can change direction suddenly. A board with unsuitable structure for a delicate moulded profile may tear during machining. A component selected without considering visible defects may fail inspection after valuable labour has already been spent on it.

This is why quality control begins at material selection rather than only at final inspection. Examine the board, compare it with the specification, position components intelligently and identify doubtful areas before machining. This reduces rework and waste.

When several components must match visually, grain and colour may also be considered during layout. For example, two cabinet doors placed next to one another may look more professional when the material is selected and oriented deliberately. However, visual matching must not override structural, dimensional or specification requirements.

### 6. A systematic pre-work timber inspection

Before marking a component, use a repeatable inspection sequence:

1. **Confirm the specification.** Identify the required material, grade if specified, dimensions, quantity and intended use.
2. **Confirm the material identity.** Use reliable labels, stock records or supplier information where required; do not guess a species from colour alone.
3. **Inspect faces, edges and ends.** Look for grain direction, knots, checks, splits, distortion, damage, contamination and other visible concerns.
4. **Consider component orientation.** Decide which face and edge should become references and which surface should be visible in the finished work.
5. **Consider grain around the machining area.** Pay particular attention to joint positions, narrow sections, edges and decorative surfaces.
6. **Check dimensions and available allowance.** Make sure the stock is large enough for the required finished component and processing sequence.
7. **Escalate uncertainty.** If the specification, grade, treatment, defect acceptability or material identity is uncertain, confirm it with the authorised person rather than making an unsupported assumption.
8. **Mark only after the material is accepted for the task.** This avoids investing work in a piece that should have been rejected or reassigned.

### 7. Use observations to reduce waste

Material efficiency is not simply about using the smallest possible piece. It means using material intelligently while still meeting specification and quality requirements. A board may contain an unacceptable defect in one region but useful material elsewhere. A competent cutting plan can sometimes position components around unsuitable areas while respecting grain, dimensions, machining allowances and required quality.

Never cut around a defect in a way that produces a component that no longer meets the specification. If a material decision could affect safety, structural performance, grade or customer requirements, it must be confirmed through the appropriate workplace process.

### 8. Build the habit of material-based decision making

Experienced carpenters continually read timber as they work. They notice changes in sound, cutting resistance, grain direction and surface quality. Learners should develop the same habit without pretending to know what has not been verified. Observation is valuable; unsupported assumptions are not.

The strongest workflow is: **specification → material identification → visual inspection → grain and defect assessment → component orientation → marking → controlled processing → quality check**. This links material knowledge directly to professional workmanship.

## Worked Example / Demonstration

A carpenter must prepare a visible 700 mm rail for a small timber display frame. Two pieces of stock are available and both are large enough.

- **Board A** has a visually clean central area, but a large knot and disturbed grain lie close to the proposed joint position at one end.
- **Board B** has less dramatic figure, but its proposed rail area is straight, visually consistent and free of obvious defects that conflict with the job requirements.

A poor decision would be to select Board A automatically because its central grain looks more attractive. A better process is to compare both boards with the specification, inspect the joint zones, consider the finished visible face and choose the piece that best satisfies the complete requirement.

If Board B is selected, the carpenter identifies a reference face and edge, marks the component orientation, confirms the cutting allowance and checks grain direction before machining. Board A is not automatically discarded; its usable areas can be assessed for other components if permitted by the cutting plan and specification.

**Professional principle:** Material selection is a quality decision, not merely a size decision.

## Practical Activity — Timber Observation & Selection Record

Obtain at least three safe, unprocessed timber samples supplied or approved by the facilitator. Do not cut or machine them for this activity.

For each sample, record:
- sample identifier;
- visible grain direction or pattern;
- growth-ring visibility at the end, if observable;
- visible heartwood/sapwood difference, if observable — write “not clearly distinguishable” rather than guessing;
- knots or other visible natural features;
- visible damage or defects requiring further assessment;
- the face you would initially choose as a reference or presentation face and why;
- one machining or quality consideration suggested by your observation;
- information you would still need before approving the material for a specific job.

Then choose a hypothetical component such as a shelf, rail or frame member. Compare the samples and write a short recommendation. Your recommendation must refer to the **job requirement and the observed material**, not only to appearance.

**Portfolio evidence:** Keep the completed Timber Observation & Selection Record with labelled sketches or approved photographs.

## Knowledge Check

### Question 1
Why should a carpenter inspect grain direction before planing or routing a visible surface?

A. Grain determines the selling price of every timber species
B. Unfavourable grain can contribute to fibre lifting and tear-out during cutting
C. Grain proves the moisture content of the board
D. Grain direction makes pre-use tool checks unnecessary

**Correct answer: B**

**Feedback:** Cutting tools interact with the direction and arrangement of fibres. Grain changes can affect surface quality, so the carpenter should inspect and respond rather than force the operation.

### Question 2
Which statement about knots is most professional?

A. Every knot makes timber unusable
B. Every small knot is acceptable
C. Knot acceptability depends on its condition, location, component purpose and specification
D. Knots affect appearance only and never machining

**Correct answer: C**

**Feedback:** Knots vary. Their effect must be judged against the actual component and acceptance requirements rather than a universal rule.

### Question 3
A board looks like a particular timber species because of its colour. What should the learner do when species identity is important to the job?

A. Approve it from colour alone
B. Use reliable labels, stock information or authorised confirmation
C. Sand it until the species becomes obvious
D. Assume all boards of similar colour have the same properties

**Correct answer: B**

**Feedback:** Colour varies and is not sufficient proof of material identity. Verification is necessary where species or grade matters.

### Question 4
What is the best sequence before marking a critical component?

A. Mark first, inspect later, then read the drawing
B. Choose the most attractive board and ignore the specification
C. Confirm specification, identify and inspect material, consider grain/defects and orientation, then mark
D. Cut the board into small pieces before deciding its purpose

**Correct answer: C**

**Feedback:** Professional quality begins with specification and material inspection before value-adding work is performed.

## Feedback / Explanation for Knowledge Check

The four questions test the central decision-making chain of this lesson. Wood structure is useful only when it improves a real carpentry decision. The learner should be able to observe grain and natural features, understand how they can influence processing, and still rely on the specification or verified material information when a property cannot be determined safely by sight.

A carpenter should neither ignore natural variation nor exaggerate what can be concluded from it. The correct approach combines observation, technical information, job requirements and controlled workmanship.

## Key Takeaways

- Timber is a natural material whose internal structure creates visible grain and variation.
- Growth rings, heartwood, sapwood, knots and end grain are useful features to recognise.
- Grain direction can influence cutting behaviour and finished surface quality.
- Knots and other features must be evaluated against their condition, position and the job specification.
- Appearance alone does not prove species, grade, strength, durability or suitability.
- Wood properties such as density, hardness, workability, stability and durability must be considered in context.
- Material inspection should happen before marking and machining, not only during final quality control.
- Intelligent component orientation can improve quality and reduce avoidable waste.
- When a material requirement cannot be verified, ask or escalate rather than guess.

**Caption:** “Select material for the whole job requirement: dimensions, grain, defect position, machining, appearance and specification all matter.”
