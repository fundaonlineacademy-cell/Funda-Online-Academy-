# Carpentry — Professional Skills Short Course

## Module 5: Timber, Boards & Material Selection
### Lesson 5: Manufactured Boards: Plywood, MDF, Particleboard & OSB

**Estimated study time:** 45–50 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. Explain what a manufactured or composite wood-based board is and why boards are used in carpentry and furniture work.
2. Distinguish the basic construction and common characteristics of plywood, MDF, particleboard and OSB.
3. Select a board by considering the job specification, service conditions, edge requirements, fixing method, finish and manufacturer information.
4. Identify common board-handling, machining, dust and moisture-related risks and apply appropriate controls.
5. Inspect manufactured boards before use and recognise conditions that require rejection, clarification or specialist guidance.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Modern carpentry is not limited to solid timber. Cupboards, shelving, furniture, interior fittings, partitions, templates, panels and many other products can include manufactured boards. A carpenter therefore needs to recognise different board types and understand that they do not all behave in the same way.

Selecting a board only because it is inexpensive, smooth or available can cause failure. A board may machine well but have unsuitable moisture resistance; another may be structurally useful but require a different finishing approach. Edge quality, fixing performance, dust generation, sheet weight, exposure conditions and the manufacturer's product classification all affect the decision. Professional material selection begins with the specification and intended service, not with assumptions.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary

**Manufactured board** — A sheet or panel product made by combining wood veneers, fibres, particles, strands or other wood-based elements using a manufacturing process. Different board families have different structures and performance characteristics.

**Veneer** — A thin layer or sheet of wood. Plywood is manufactured by bonding veneers in layers, normally with the grain direction of adjacent layers arranged across one another.

**Plywood** — A manufactured board made from bonded layers of wood veneer. Its layered construction gives it useful dimensional and structural characteristics, but the exact grade and suitability depend on the product specification.

**MDF (medium-density fibreboard)** — A relatively uniform board manufactured from refined wood fibres bonded into a panel. It is widely used where a smooth, consistent surface and machinable profile are useful.

**Particleboard** — A panel manufactured from wood particles or chips bonded and pressed together. It is widely used in furniture and interior panel applications, often with decorative surface finishes.

**OSB (oriented strand board)** — A structural-type panel manufactured from wood strands arranged in oriented layers and bonded into a sheet. Product grade and intended application must be confirmed before use.

**Face** — The broad surface of a board. Some boards have different face grades, factory coatings, laminates or decorative surfaces that influence which side is exposed.

**Edge** — The narrow side of a board revealed after cutting. Edge structure affects appearance, finishing, edge-banding and the way some fixings perform.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Manufactured boards are engineered materials

Solid timber is cut from a tree and retains the natural grain, growth features and variation of that tree. Manufactured boards are produced by reorganising wood into veneers, fibres, particles or strands and forming these into panels. This allows manufacturers to create large, consistent sheet sizes and material characteristics suited to particular applications.

This does not mean every manufactured board is interchangeable. The internal structure of a board strongly influences how it cuts, holds an edge, accepts a fixing, reacts to moisture, produces dust and receives a finish. A professional carpenter identifies the exact board and reads the job or product requirements before deciding how to use it.

South African DBE woodworking material includes plywood, chipboard, Supawood/MDF and other manufactured boards as important woodworking materials. Current occupational woodworking curricula likewise require learners to identify characteristics of manufactured boards and use board materials in practical work. SAQA occupational material also recognises timber, board and composite-board components in furniture machining and woodworking practice.

### 2. Plywood: layered veneers

Plywood is made by bonding thin wood veneers into a panel. Adjacent layers are generally arranged with grain directions crossing one another. This cross-layered construction helps produce a panel that behaves differently from a single solid board and can provide useful strength and dimensional stability.

A carpenter should inspect plywood for face quality, damaged corners, edge separation, visible voids where relevant, warping and signs of moisture damage. The number of visible layers alone does not tell the carpenter whether a sheet is suitable for a particular exposure condition. Terms such as interior, exterior or marine-related classifications must be verified from the actual product specification and supplier or manufacturer documentation rather than guessed from appearance.

Plywood can be useful for furniture, panels, templates, construction components and other work, depending on its specified grade. Its exposed layered edge may be intentionally displayed, covered or finished according to the design.

### 3. MDF: a fine-fibre board

MDF is made from fine wood fibres that are formed and bonded into a dense, comparatively uniform panel. Because it has no natural solid-wood grain direction in the conventional sense, its surface is consistent and it can be useful for painted furniture, cabinet components, moulded profiles, templates and interior joinery where the specified product is appropriate.

Its smooth face can provide a good substrate for certain finishing systems, but freshly machined edges may absorb finish differently from the face and may require an appropriate preparation system. MDF machining can also generate large quantities of fine dust. Dust extraction, suitable respiratory protection where required by the risk assessment, good housekeeping and the applicable machine/tool controls are therefore important.

A learner must not assume that ordinary MDF is suitable for wet or exterior exposure. Moisture-resistant or specialised MDF products exist, but their suitability must be confirmed from the specific product classification and intended-use information.

### 4. Particleboard: bonded wood particles

Particleboard, often informally called chipboard, is manufactured from wood particles bonded and pressed into panels. It is widely used for cabinets, furniture and shelving, particularly when supplied with a decorative laminate or melamine-type surface.

The faces can be smooth and suitable for factory-applied finishes, while the cut edge exposes the particle structure. Edge treatment may therefore form part of the product design. Fixing performance can differ from solid timber and from other boards. Screw type, pilot preparation, distance from edges, insertion method and any specialist fittings must follow the design and product guidance. Repeatedly removing and reinserting a fixing into a damaged hole can reduce holding quality.

Water or excessive moisture can cause severe deterioration in products not designed for that exposure. A swollen or crumbling panel should not simply be forced back into an assembly; its condition and required corrective action must be assessed.

### 5. OSB: oriented strands

OSB is produced from relatively long wood strands arranged in oriented layers and bonded into a panel. The visible strand pattern makes it easy to distinguish from fine-fibre MDF and many particleboards. OSB is commonly associated with building and structural panel applications, but its exact permitted use depends on the board's specified grade, thickness, span/application requirements and the relevant design or manufacturer's instructions.

A carpenter must never assume that any sheet labelled OSB can replace plywood or another structural panel without checking the specification. Structural decisions require the correct product and design information.

### 6. Select by performance, not by name alone

When choosing a manufactured board, ask:

- What does the drawing, cutting list or job specification require?
- Is the component structural, decorative or both?
- Will it be indoors, outdoors or exposed to intermittent moisture?
- What face quality is required?
- Will edges remain visible, be painted or receive edge-banding/lipping?
- What machining is required: straight cutting, drilling, routing, profiling or jointing?
- What fixings or adhesives are specified?
- What finish will be applied?
- What board thickness and product grade are required?
- What does the manufacturer state about use, handling and installation?

Cost matters, but cheapest purchase price is not the same as lowest job cost. An unsuitable board can create excessive waste, poor finish, fixing failure, rework or replacement.

### 7. Sheet handling, cutting and dust control

Full sheets can be large, heavy and awkward. Handling must be planned according to the board dimensions, mass, workplace manual-handling procedure and available assistance or equipment. Never attempt an unsafe lift merely to save time.

Before machining, support the sheet so that it remains stable and the cut cannot trap the blade or create an uncontrolled offcut. Confirm that the cutting tool and blade are suitable for the board and follow manufacturer instructions. Laminated or veneered faces may require additional care to reduce breakout or surface damage.

Machining wood-based boards produces dust. Source extraction should be used where provided and appropriate. The work area should be kept clean, and PPE should supplement—not replace—effective dust-control and safe-machine measures.

### 8. Inspect before committing material

Before marking or cutting, check the board against the specification. Confirm type, thickness, dimensions, face/finish, quantity and any grade or product identification required. Then inspect for damage such as:

- warped sheets;
- swollen edges;
- delamination or separation;
- broken corners;
- deep surface scratches;
- damaged decorative faces;
- contamination;
- incorrect thickness or product type.

If identification is uncertain, stop and clarify. A similar-looking board is not automatically an acceptable substitute.

## Worked Example / Demonstration

A learner is preparing components for an indoor painted storage cabinet. The approved cutting list specifies MDF of a stated thickness for the cabinet panels. In the material rack, the learner sees MDF, laminated particleboard and plywood.

A poor approach would be to choose whichever sheet is closest because all three are flat boards.

The professional sequence is:
1. Read the cutting list and confirm the specified board and thickness.
2. Identify the MDF from its product label and material characteristics.
3. Inspect the sheet for swelling, surface damage, warping and damaged edges.
4. Plan the component layout to reduce waste without changing specified dimensions.
5. Identify finished faces and any areas where edge preparation will matter.
6. Plan safe sheet handling and stable support.
7. Confirm the cutting equipment, extraction and blade/tooling are suitable.
8. Cut according to the approved dimensions and inspect the components before proceeding.

If only particleboard were available, the learner would not substitute it without approval. The correct action would be to report the material shortage or discrepancy.

## Knowledge Check

### Question 1
What is the most important first step when selecting between plywood, MDF, particleboard and OSB for a job?

A. Select the cheapest board
B. Select the heaviest board
C. Check the job specification and intended service conditions
D. Select whichever board has the smoothest face

**Correct answer: C**

**Feedback:** Material selection must begin with the approved requirement and service conditions. Price and appearance alone cannot establish suitability.

### Question 2
Which description best identifies plywood?

A. Fine fibres formed into a uniform panel
B. Thin wood veneers bonded in layers
C. Loose sawdust used without bonding
D. One solid piece of timber cut directly from a log

**Correct answer: B**

**Feedback:** Plywood is manufactured from bonded veneers, normally arranged so adjacent grain directions cross one another.

### Question 3
A sheet of ordinary board has swollen badly after water exposure. What is the best response?

A. Force it into the assembly because the dimensions were originally correct
B. Paint over the swelling and continue
C. Assess the damage and specification, and replace or escalate if the material is no longer acceptable
D. Sand away any amount necessary without checking dimensions

**Correct answer: C**

**Feedback:** Swelling can alter dimensions, surface quality and material integrity. Corrective action must be based on acceptance requirements, not concealment or force.

### Question 4
Why should a carpenter avoid assuming that every OSB or plywood sheet is suitable for the same application?

A. All boards have identical performance
B. Product grade, thickness, exposure classification and intended use can differ
C. Board labels are only decorative
D. Only board colour affects performance

**Correct answer: B**

**Feedback:** Board family names describe broad material types. Actual suitability depends on the specified product, grade, dimensions, exposure and application requirements.

## Feedback / Explanation for the Knowledge Check

A professional carpenter uses the sequence **specification → identify material → inspect → confirm service conditions → plan machining/handling → process safely → inspect result**. Manufactured boards are engineered for different purposes. The correct decision is therefore based on verified requirements rather than appearance, habit or price alone.

## Key Takeaways

1. Manufactured boards are engineered sheet materials made from veneers, fibres, particles or strands.
2. Plywood, MDF, particleboard and OSB have different internal structures and therefore different working characteristics.
3. Board family names alone do not prove suitability for a particular structural, exterior, moisture-exposed or specialised application.
4. The drawing, cutting list, product grade and manufacturer information take priority over assumptions.
5. Face and edge characteristics affect machining, fixing and finishing decisions.
6. Large sheets require planned handling and stable support during machining.
7. Board machining can generate significant dust, so effective extraction, housekeeping and appropriate PPE are important.
8. Damaged, swollen, delaminated or incorrectly identified material must be assessed before use.
9. Material substitution requires approval when the specification calls for a particular board.
10. Professional selection balances quality, service conditions, safety, waste, cost and required performance.
