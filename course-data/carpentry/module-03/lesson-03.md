# Carpentry — Professional Skills Short Course

## Module 3: Power Tools & Safe Operating Practice
### Lesson 3: Jigsaws & Reciprocating Saws

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. Explain the main differences between a jigsaw and a reciprocating saw and select the more suitable tool for a task.
2. Select an appropriate blade by considering the material, type of cut, required finish and manufacturer guidance.
3. Carry out a systematic pre-use inspection and prepare the workpiece and work area before cutting.
4. Describe safe, controlled techniques for straight, curved, internal and demolition-style cutting where appropriate.
5. Recognise unsafe conditions, common cutting faults and situations that require the tool to be stopped and reported.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A carpenter does not choose a powered saw simply because it can cut the material. The tool must suit the shape of the cut, the required accuracy, the workpiece, the working environment and the operator's competence. Jigsaws are commonly useful for curved cuts, openings and controlled cuts where a narrow reciprocating blade is advantageous. Reciprocating saws are generally more suitable for rougher cutting, alteration, removal and demolition-type work where access and speed may matter more than a fine finished edge.

Poor tool selection can damage material, break blades, create excessive vibration, reduce accuracy and expose the operator or nearby people to unnecessary risk. Professional practice therefore combines correct selection, inspection, workholding, blade choice, controlled operation and final quality checking.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary

**Jigsaw** — A portable power saw that uses a narrow blade moving rapidly up and down. It is commonly used for straight cuts, curved cuts and cut-outs in suitable materials.

**Reciprocating saw** — A portable power saw with a blade that moves backwards and forwards. It is commonly used for rough cutting, alteration and removal work where a fine finished edge is not the main requirement.

**Reciprocating action** — Repeated back-and-forth or up-and-down movement of a blade that produces the cutting action.

**Sole plate / base plate** — The flat part of a jigsaw that rests against the workpiece and helps support and guide the tool during a cut.

**Blade selection** — Choosing a blade that is compatible with the tool and suitable for the material, cut and finish required.

**Internal cut-out** — An opening cut inside a workpiece rather than beginning from an outside edge. The starting method must be appropriate to the tool, material and manufacturer instructions.

**Orbital action** — A feature on some jigsaws that changes the blade movement to increase cutting action. Settings and suitability vary by tool and material, so manufacturer instructions must be followed.

**Blade deflection** — Bending or wandering of a blade away from the intended cutting path, which can produce an inaccurate or non-square cut.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Select the saw according to the task

The jigsaw and reciprocating saw both use reciprocating blades, but they should not be treated as interchangeable tools. A jigsaw has a base plate that normally remains in contact with the workpiece. Its relatively narrow blade makes it useful for curves, shaped openings and many controlled cuts in timber and board products. The quality achievable depends on the blade, material, tool setup, support and operator technique.

A reciprocating saw normally uses a longer projecting blade and is often selected for rougher work such as cutting out damaged material, shortening components during alteration work or working where access is difficult. It can be extremely useful, but it is generally not the first choice when a clean furniture-quality edge or precise joinery cut is required.

Before selecting either tool, ask: What material am I cutting? Is the cut straight, curved or internal? How accurate must the finished edge be? Is there enough access? What is behind or below the cut? Can the workpiece be supported safely? Does the operator have the required training and authorisation?

### 2. Choose the correct blade

The blade is part of the cutting system. A blade must be compatible with the particular saw and suitable for the material and intended operation. Blades differ in length, tooth form, tooth spacing, material and intended application. A blade intended for one material or purpose should not automatically be used for another.

For jigsaw work, the required finish and curve radius also influence blade selection. A narrow blade may negotiate tighter curves than a broad blade, but blade stiffness, material thickness and cutting technique affect accuracy. A badly worn, bent, cracked, blunt or incorrectly secured blade should not be used.

For reciprocating saws, blade length deserves special attention. The operator must consider the material thickness and what lies beyond the workpiece. A blade that can strike a hidden pipe, electrical service, wall surface, person or other object creates a serious hazard. Never begin a blind alteration cut until the area has been assessed and hidden-service risks have been addressed through the workplace's approved procedure.

### 3. Perform the pre-use inspection

Before connecting power or fitting a battery, inspect the tool. Check the housing, handles, switches, base plate or shoe, blade clamp, guards or protective features, cable and plug where applicable, battery interface and other visible components. Confirm that adjustments lock correctly and that the correct blade is securely installed according to manufacturer instructions.

On a jigsaw, inspect the sole plate and blade area. A damaged or loose sole plate can affect both control and accuracy. On a reciprocating saw, inspect the shoe and blade mounting system. Do not use a tool with a loose blade, damaged cable, cracked housing, missing safety component or other defect that could affect safe operation. Remove it from service and report it according to workplace procedure.

South African DBE woodworking guidance reinforces several core principles: use the correct blade for the specific work, keep the power cord away from the blade, avoid forcing a jigsaw blade, check that the blade is correctly secured and not bent, and disconnect power when replacing the blade or making relevant adjustments.

### 4. Prepare and secure the workpiece

Good cutting begins before the trigger is pressed. Mark the cut clearly and identify the required side and waste side where relevant. Support the material so that it remains stable while the cut progresses. The support arrangement must not place clamps, benches or supports directly in the blade path.

For a jigsaw, the base plate should normally remain properly supported against the workpiece during the cut. If the workpiece vibrates or bounces, accuracy decreases and blade damage becomes more likely. Thin sheet materials may require additional support appropriate to the material and operation.

When using a reciprocating saw, the work must also be stable. The operator should anticipate what happens when the cut completes. A released component must not fall onto a person, trap the blade, strike another object or destabilise the remaining structure. Structural, electrical, plumbing or other specialist risks must be escalated rather than guessed.

### 5. Use the jigsaw with controlled technique

Start from a stable position with the blade clear of unintended contact unless the manufacturer's procedure for the particular operation states otherwise. Hold the tool securely and allow it to reach its intended operating condition before feeding it into the work as appropriate. Keep the base plate in controlled contact with the material and guide the saw without excessive sideways pressure.

Do not force the saw to cut faster than the blade and material allow. Excessive feed pressure can cause blade deflection, overheating, rough edges or breakage. When cutting a curve, guide the tool gradually. If the required curve is too tight for the blade and setup, stop and reassess rather than twisting the blade aggressively.

For an internal cut-out, use an approved starting method. Depending on the material and task, this may involve drilling a suitable starter hole and inserting the jigsaw blade through it before starting. Plunge-cutting should not be improvised; it should only be performed where the tool, blade, material, operator competence and manufacturer instructions specifically support it.

### 6. Use the reciprocating saw for suitable work

A reciprocating saw can produce strong vibration and rapid cutting action. Establish a stable stance and secure grip. Where the design permits, keep the shoe firmly controlled against the work to reduce uncontrolled movement and improve stability. Keep hands and other body parts away from the blade path and consider the full blade travel, including what is behind the material.

Do not use the tool as a substitute for careful investigation. Alteration and demolition work can conceal nails, screws, electrical cables, pipes and other services. A suitable blade may be capable of cutting several materials, but that capability does not make a blind cut safe.

As the cut nears completion, anticipate movement of the offcut and remaining structure. Maintain control until the blade has stopped before setting the tool down or moving into the blade area.

### 7. Recognise common faults and unsafe conditions

A jigsaw cut that wanders may result from excessive feed pressure, unsuitable blade selection, a bent or blunt blade, poor support, inaccurate marking or blade deflection. A rough edge may be influenced by blade type, cutting direction, material surface, speed or technique. The professional response is to diagnose the cause rather than repeatedly forcing the tool through the same problem.

Stop using either saw if there is abnormal vibration, an unusual sound, burning smell, smoke, unexpected overheating, repeated blade loosening, damaged insulation, sparking inconsistent with normal tool operation, loss of control or any other abnormal condition. Disconnect or isolate the energy source using the approved procedure and report the defect. Do not perform an unauthorised repair.

### 8. Complete the cut professionally

After cutting, allow the blade to stop fully before placing the tool down or touching the cutting area. Disconnect power or remove the battery as required before blade changes, cleaning or other activities specified by the manufacturer.

Inspect the work. Check the cut against the marked line, required dimensions, squareness where applicable, curve quality, edge condition and specification. A powered saw is not successful merely because it passed through the material. The finished component must still meet the job requirement.

Clean and store the tool and blades according to workplace and manufacturer procedures. Protect blades from damage, keep the tool dry and organised, and report consumables or defects requiring attention before the next user receives the equipment.

## Worked Example / Demonstration

A learner must create a rectangular service opening in a plywood panel. The drawing gives the opening dimensions and requires the visible face to remain as clean as reasonably practicable.

A professional sequence would be:
1. Confirm the drawing, dimensions and orientation of the panel.
2. Inspect the panel and identify the visible face.
3. Mark the opening accurately using verified reference lines.
4. Select a jigsaw because the operation requires a controlled internal cut rather than rough demolition cutting.
5. Select a compatible blade suitable for the plywood and required finish, following manufacturer guidance.
6. Inspect the jigsaw, cable or battery, sole plate, blade clamp and blade condition.
7. Secure and support the panel so that the blade path is clear.
8. Create an appropriate starter opening using an approved method, such as a drilled starter hole where suitable.
9. Insert the blade safely, establish the cut and guide the jigsaw along the marked lines without excessive force.
10. Slow down and control direction at corners rather than twisting the blade aggressively.
11. Allow the blade to stop fully, isolate the tool as required and inspect the opening for dimensions, line accuracy and edge damage.

The key lesson is that accuracy and safety are created by the whole process, not by the cutting stage alone.

## Knowledge Check

### Question 1
Which tool is generally the better choice for a controlled curved cut in a timber board?

A. Reciprocating saw
B. Jigsaw
C. Claw hammer
D. Bench vice

**Correct answer: B — Jigsaw.**

**Feedback:** The jigsaw's narrow reciprocating blade and supported base make it suitable for many controlled curved cuts when the correct blade and technique are used.

### Question 2
A jigsaw blade repeatedly bends away from the marked line. What is the best response?

A. Push harder so the blade cannot wander
B. Twist the saw sideways to force it back
C. Stop and check blade condition, selection, support and feed technique
D. Remove the sole plate

**Correct answer: C — Stop and diagnose the cause.**

**Feedback:** Forcing or twisting a blade can worsen deflection and increase the risk of blade damage or loss of control.

### Question 3
Why must the operator investigate what lies behind material before making an alteration cut with a reciprocating saw?

A. The blade may extend beyond the material and strike hidden services or objects
B. It makes the saw quieter
C. It changes the battery voltage
D. It automatically improves the surface finish

**Correct answer: A — The blade may strike hidden hazards.**

**Feedback:** Reciprocating saw blades can project beyond the workpiece. Blind cutting can therefore expose hidden electrical, plumbing, structural or other hazards.

### Question 4
What should happen before changing a jigsaw blade?

A. Hold the trigger so the clamp remains open
B. Disconnect or isolate the power source according to the manufacturer's procedure
C. Increase the speed setting
D. Rest the moving blade against scrap timber

**Correct answer: B — Disconnect or isolate the energy source.**

**Feedback:** Blade changes and relevant adjustments must not be performed while the tool can start unexpectedly.

## Knowledge Check Feedback Summary
A safe operator thinks in a sequence: **select the correct tool → select the correct blade → inspect → mark and support → check the full blade path → cut under control → stop for abnormal conditions → inspect the result → isolate, clean and store safely.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Jigsaws and reciprocating saws use reciprocating blades but serve different types of carpentry work.
2. A jigsaw is commonly suited to controlled curves and cut-outs; a reciprocating saw is commonly suited to rough alteration and removal work.
3. Correct blade selection is essential for safety, accuracy and blade life.
4. Inspect the tool and blade before every use and remove defective equipment from service.
5. Secure and support the workpiece and keep the complete blade path clear.
6. Never force or aggressively twist a jigsaw blade to correct a poor cut.
7. Investigate hidden-service and behind-the-work hazards before alteration or demolition cuts.
8. Isolate the energy source before blade changes and relevant adjustments.
9. The completed cut must be checked against the specification, not merely accepted because the saw passed through the material.
