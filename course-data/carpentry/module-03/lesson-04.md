# Carpentry — Professional Skills Short Course

## Module 3: Power Tools & Safe Operating Practice
### Lesson 4: Drills, Drivers & Boring Operations

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

1. Distinguish between drilling, driving and boring operations used in carpentry.
2. Select a suitable drill, driver, bit or accessory for a specified task.
3. Carry out a systematic pre-use check and prepare the workpiece and work area safely.
4. Apply controlled techniques for drilling holes and driving screws accurately.
5. Identify common drilling and driving faults, explain likely causes, and choose appropriate corrective actions.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Drills and drivers are among the most frequently used portable power tools in carpentry. They are used for tasks ranging from pilot holes and clearance holes to fixing hardware, assembling timber components and creating larger-diameter openings. Because these tools are so common, learners can easily become overconfident and treat them as low-risk. In practice, an incorrect bit, loose accessory, unsecured workpiece, poor body position or hidden obstruction can damage the workpiece, damage the tool or injure the operator.

Professional carpentry requires more than simply making a hole or driving a screw. The hole must be in the correct position, at the correct angle and, where specified, at the correct depth. The fastener must be driven without stripping the head, splitting the timber, damaging the surrounding surface or over-tightening the joint. Safe and accurate drilling is therefore part of quality workmanship, not a separate activity.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary

**Drill** — A powered tool that rotates a drill bit or other compatible accessory to create holes or perform related operations.

**Driver** — A powered tool intended mainly to turn screws or other compatible fasteners. Some drill-drivers combine drilling and driving functions in one tool.

**Chuck** — The mechanism that holds a drill bit, driver bit or compatible accessory in the tool. It must grip the accessory securely before use.

**Pilot hole** — A smaller hole drilled before inserting a screw. A suitable pilot hole can reduce splitting, improve alignment and reduce driving resistance where the material and specification require it.

**Clearance hole** — A hole large enough for the screw shank to pass through one component without the threads gripping that component, where the joint design calls for this.

**Countersink** — A conical recess formed at the entrance of a hole so that a countersunk screw head can sit flush or below the surface when the specification requires it.

**Torque** — Turning force. Many drill-drivers have adjustable torque settings to help control how strongly a screw is driven.

**Boring** — Producing a hole, often larger or more accurately controlled than a basic drilling operation, using a suitable boring bit or accessory.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Drilling, driving and boring are different operations

Although one portable drill-driver may perform several functions, the operator must still distinguish the task being performed. **Drilling** removes material to create a hole. **Driving** turns a screw or compatible fastener into the material. **Boring** is a broader term used for making holes, especially where the hole is larger, deeper, cleaner or more precisely controlled using a specialised accessory.

The correct setup depends on the task. A twist drill may be suitable for one application, while a brad-point bit, spade bit, auger-type bit, hole saw or other woodworking accessory may be more appropriate for another. Driver bits must match the screw-head profile and size. The operator should never force an unsuitable accessory to perform a job for which it was not intended.

Manufacturer instructions, workplace procedures and the job specification must take priority when choosing accessories. Learners should not invent bit-speed limits, maximum hole sizes or torque values. These vary according to tool design, accessory type, material and manufacturer.

### 2. Select the tool and accessory before starting

A professional selection sequence begins with the job requirement. Ask:

- What material is being drilled or fastened?
- What diameter, depth and position are required?
- Is the hole a pilot, clearance, countersunk, through-hole or blind hole?
- Is the fastener type and head profile known?
- Does the task require a drill, drill-driver, impact driver or another approved tool?
- Is the accessory compatible with the tool and the material?

For example, a learner assembling two timber rails with screws should not simply choose the largest available driver. The learner should first confirm the specified fastener, determine whether pilot or clearance preparation is required, choose the matching driver bit, and select the correct tool mode and control setting according to the tool instructions and workplace practice.

### 3. Carry out a systematic pre-use check

Before fitting an accessory or connecting power, inspect the tool. Check the housing, handle, trigger, battery interface or electrical lead, plug where applicable, chuck and visible fasteners. Look for cracks, looseness, damaged insulation, missing parts, abnormal wear or signs of unauthorised repair. A defective tool must be removed from use and reported according to workplace procedure.

Inspect the bit or driver accessory as well. A drill bit should not be badly bent, cracked or damaged. A driver bit with a badly worn tip can slip or cam out of the screw head, leading to poor control and damaged fasteners. A hole saw or other accessory must be complete and assembled correctly according to its instructions.

When using a keyed chuck, the chuck key must be removed before the tool is started. South African Department of Basic Education woodworking material specifically reinforces this point, together with securing the workpiece and allowing the machine to come to a complete stop before putting it down.

### 4. Secure the workpiece and inspect the drilling path

The workpiece must be stable. Small pieces should normally be secured with an appropriate clamp or vice rather than held in the hand. Larger workpieces must be supported so that they cannot spin, shift, drop or trap the bit unexpectedly.

The operator must also consider what lies behind or beneath the drilling point. On site, there may be hidden electrical wiring, plumbing, reinforcing elements or other services. In a workshop, drilling through a workpiece can damage a bench, clamp or support block if the exit path is not planned. Never drill blindly where hidden services may be present. Follow the workplace's approved detection, isolation and authorisation procedures.

A backing piece can be useful where a clean exit is required or where breakthrough could damage the supporting surface. It also helps support the fibres at the back of the workpiece in some operations.

### 5. Mark accurately before drilling

Accuracy begins before the trigger is pressed. Use the correct reference face or edge, measure from known references, and mark the hole centre clearly. If several holes must align with another component, verify the layout before drilling.

Where the operation requires the drill to be perpendicular to the surface, the learner must control the tool in more than one visual plane. Looking square from only one direction can still result in an angled hole. A drilling guide, jig or drill press may be more appropriate where high repeatability or precise alignment is required, provided the learner is trained and authorised to use it.

### 6. Fit the bit correctly and prepare the tool

Disconnect the tool from its energy source where required by the manufacturer's instructions before fitting or changing accessories. For a corded tool, this commonly means unplugging it. For a cordless tool, follow the manufacturer's safe accessory-change procedure, which may involve removing the battery or using another specified control state.

Insert the accessory to the correct depth and tighten it securely. Do not leave a bit loosely gripped in the chuck. Check that the accessory runs true and that no chuck key or adjustment tool remains fitted.

Select the appropriate operating mode before starting. A drill setting, driver setting, hammer setting or speed range may be available, but these are tool-specific. The learner must use the settings described by the manufacturer for the task. A hammer function intended for masonry, for example, should not be used simply because it appears to make the tool more powerful.

### 7. Use controlled drilling technique

Adopt a stable stance, secure the workpiece and hold the tool with a controlled grip. Position the bit on the marked point before applying power. Start the hole under control and maintain alignment as the bit enters the material.

Do not force the tool. Excessive pressure can overheat the bit, reduce control, cause the bit to bind or damage the workpiece. If the tool labours abnormally, the bit stops progressing, the material begins to burn or the tool produces unusual vibration, smell or sound, stop safely and investigate. The correct response may involve checking sharpness, bit selection, speed setting, chip clearance, material condition or tool condition.

For deeper holes, chip build-up can increase resistance. Appropriate withdrawal or clearing technique may be required depending on the accessory and manufacturer's instructions. The operator must wait for rotation to stop before touching or clearing the accessory.

When a through-hole is nearly complete, control should increase rather than decrease. Sudden breakthrough can pull the tool forward or cause splintering at the exit face. Good support, correct bit selection and a suitable backing method can improve the result.

### 8. Use controlled screw-driving technique

Driving screws requires correct alignment between the driver bit and the screw head. The bit should match the profile and size of the fastener. A mismatched or worn bit can damage the screw head and reduce control.

Keep the driver aligned with the screw axis. Apply enough forward pressure to maintain engagement without forcing the fastener. Stop when the specified seating condition is reached. A screw that must finish flush should not be driven deeply below the surface merely because the tool can do so. Over-driving can crush timber fibres, weaken the fixing area, damage hardware or make later removal difficult.

Where the material is prone to splitting, a suitable pilot hole may be necessary. A clearance hole or countersink may also be required depending on joint design and fastener type. These are not universal steps for every screw; the correct preparation depends on the material, screw and specification.

### 9. Recognise common faults and correct them properly

Common drilling and driving faults include:

- **Hole wandering:** often linked to poor marking, unstable starting technique or unsuitable bit selection.
- **Angled hole:** caused by poor alignment or inadequate guidance.
- **Splintered exit:** may result from unsupported breakthrough, unsuitable bit or excessive pressure.
- **Burning:** can indicate a dull bit, excessive speed or pressure, poor chip clearance or incorrect technique.
- **Bit binding:** may occur if the workpiece shifts, the bit is forced, chips jam the cut or the material closes on the bit.
- **Stripped screw head:** often caused by poor bit fit, poor alignment, excessive torque or continued driving after proper seating.
- **Split timber:** may result from fastener position, inadequate edge distance, missing pilot preparation where required, or over-tightening.
- **Over-driven fastener:** caused by poor torque control, excessive trigger control or failure to stop at the specified seating point.

The professional response is to stop and diagnose. Continuing to force the operation usually increases damage.

### 10. Finish the task safely and inspect the result

After the operation, release the trigger and allow the tool and accessory to come to a complete stop before setting the tool down. Disconnect or isolate it appropriately before changing accessories, clearing trapped material or performing maintenance.

Inspect the finished hole or fixing against the job requirement. Check position, angle, depth where relevant, diameter, surface damage, fastener seating and component alignment. A good drilling or fastening operation should support the next stage of work without creating hidden defects.

## Worked Example / Demonstration

**Scenario:** A learner must fasten a small timber cleat to a timber panel using screws specified on the job sheet.

A professional sequence would be:

1. Confirm the cleat position and mark from an agreed reference edge.
2. Confirm the specified screw type and select the matching driver bit.
3. Determine from the job method and material whether pilot, clearance or countersink preparation is required.
4. Inspect the drill-driver, chuck, bit, battery or lead, controls and visible condition.
5. Secure the panel and cleat so they cannot shift.
6. Check the drilling path and ensure no clamp or bench component is directly behind the hole.
7. Drill the required preparation holes with the tool held square to the work.
8. Change to the correct driver bit using the approved accessory-change procedure.
9. Drive each screw in alignment and stop at the specified seating position.
10. Inspect the cleat for correct position, secure fixing, undamaged surface and absence of splitting.

If a screw begins to cam out, the learner should stop rather than forcing it. The likely causes should be checked: bit size, bit wear, alignment, torque setting, pilot preparation or fastener damage.

## Knowledge Check

### Question 1
A learner must drill a small timber component. What is the safest approach?

A. Hold the component in one hand and drill with the other.
B. Secure the component with an appropriate vice or clamp before drilling.
C. Increase speed so the hole is completed before the component can move.
D. Place the component on a smooth bench without restraint.

**Correct answer: B**

**Feedback:** Small workpieces can spin or move suddenly when a bit catches. Secure workholding improves both safety and accuracy.

### Question 2
Why must the chuck key be removed before starting a drill that uses a keyed chuck?

A. It reduces battery use.
B. It improves drilling speed.
C. The key could be thrown from the rotating chuck and cause injury.
D. It makes the bit sharper.

**Correct answer: C**

**Feedback:** A chuck key left in position can become a projectile when the tool starts. It must be removed before switching on.

### Question 3
A screw head begins to strip while being driven. What should the operator do first?

A. Keep driving until the screw is below the timber surface.
B. Stop and check bit fit, alignment, torque and fastener condition.
C. Strike the driver with a hammer.
D. Change immediately to the highest torque setting.

**Correct answer: B**

**Feedback:** Stripping is a warning that the fastening system is not working correctly. Stop and diagnose rather than forcing the operation.

### Question 4
Why should the operator check what lies behind the drilling point?

A. Only to estimate drilling time.
B. To make the drill louder.
C. To prevent damage to hidden services, clamps, benches or other materials.
D. Because every hole must be drilled completely through the workpiece.

**Correct answer: C**

**Feedback:** The drilling path must be planned. Hidden services or objects behind the material can create serious hazards or cause damage.

## Feedback / Explanation for the Knowledge Check
The four questions reinforce a professional sequence: secure the workpiece, prepare the tool correctly, stop when a fault develops, and inspect the full drilling path before starting. These steps help the learner move from casual tool use to controlled workplace practice.

## Key Takeaways

- Drilling, driving and boring are related but different operations.
- Select the tool and accessory according to the material, hole or fixing requirement and manufacturer guidance.
- Inspect the tool, chuck, accessory and power source before use.
- Secure small workpieces rather than holding them by hand while drilling.
- Remove the chuck key before starting a keyed-chuck drill.
- Check what lies behind or beneath the drilling path.
- Maintain alignment and controlled pressure; do not force the tool.
- Match driver bits correctly to screw heads and stop if cam-out or stripping begins.
- Pilot, clearance and countersink preparation must follow the job requirement rather than habit.
- Allow the tool to stop fully before setting it down, changing accessories or clearing material.
- Final inspection is part of the operation: check position, angle, damage, seating and overall quality.
