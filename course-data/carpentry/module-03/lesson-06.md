# Carpentry — Professional Skills Short Course

## Module 3: Power Tools & Safe Operating Practice
### Lesson 6: Sanders, Abrasives & Dust Control

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:
1. Distinguish common powered sanding methods and select an appropriate sander and abrasive for a task.
2. Explain abrasive grit progression and how poor sanding technique can damage a workpiece.
3. Prepare, inspect and operate a portable sander using safe workholding and controlled technique.
4. Explain why wood dust must be controlled at source and why PPE is not a substitute for effective dust controls.
5. Inspect a sanded surface and identify common sanding defects and appropriate corrective action.

## Why This Lesson Matters
Sanding is often treated as a simple finishing operation, but it directly affects appearance, coating performance, dimensional accuracy, worker health and customer acceptance. A poorly selected abrasive can leave deep scratches that remain visible after finishing. Excessive pressure can round edges, create hollows, damage veneer or overheat the abrasive. Uncontrolled sanding also produces fine airborne wood dust.

South African occupational-health guidance recognises wood dust as an exposure hazard. The Hazardous Chemical Agents Regulations guidance notes that sanding and assembly operations can generate fine wood dust. Professional carpentry therefore requires both surface-quality control and effective exposure control. The correct approach is to prevent or reduce dust generation and capture dust as close to the source as reasonably practicable, while using suitable PPE where required by the risk assessment and workplace procedure.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary

**Abrasive** — A material containing hard particles that cut or wear away a surface through friction. Sandpaper, sanding discs and sanding belts are common abrasive products used in carpentry.

**Grit** — A grading used to describe abrasive particle size. Lower grit numbers generally represent coarser abrasives, while higher numbers generally represent finer abrasives; the exact abrasive system and manufacturer information should be checked where precision matters.

**Orbital sander** — A powered sander in which the sanding pad moves in a small orbital pattern. It is commonly used for surface preparation and finishing work.

**Random-orbit sander** — A sander that combines orbital movement with rotation to reduce obvious repetitive scratch patterns when correctly used.

**Belt sander** — A powered sander using a continuous abrasive belt. It can remove material rapidly and therefore requires particularly careful control to avoid hollows, rounded edges and excessive stock removal.

**Dust extraction** — A system that captures dust generated by an operation, preferably close to where it is produced, and conveys it to suitable collection or filtration equipment.

**Scratch pattern** — The visible marks left on a surface by abrasive particles. The pattern should become progressively finer as sanding progresses.

**Loading** — The accumulation of dust, resin or removed material in the abrasive surface, reducing cutting efficiency and potentially increasing heat or scratching.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Sanding is controlled cutting, not simply rubbing a surface
An abrasive removes many tiny particles from the workpiece. The carpenter should therefore think about sanding in the same disciplined way as other cutting operations: choose the correct tool, choose a suitable abrasive, secure the work, control the operation and inspect the result.

The objective is not always to make timber feel as smooth as possible. The required finish depends on the specification and the next operation. A surface being prepared for a particular coating may require a different final abrasive from a surface that will remain unfinished. Manufacturer instructions for coatings, boards, veneers and abrasive products should be followed rather than assuming that one sanding sequence suits every material.

Sanding can also change geometry. Aggressive sanding on one area can create a hollow. Repeated sanding across a corner can round an arris that was supposed to remain sharp. Thin veneers and decorative surfaces can be sanded through. A professional carpenter therefore removes only the material necessary to achieve the required surface condition.

### 2. Select the sander according to the task
Different sanders behave differently.

An **orbital or finishing sander** is useful for controlled surface preparation and relatively fine work. A **random-orbit sander** is widely used for general preparation and finishing because its movement can help reduce obvious directional marks when the correct abrasive and technique are used. A **belt sander** removes material much more rapidly and is useful for appropriate heavier preparation tasks, but it can damage a component quickly if held in one place or tilted. Detail or specialised sanders may be appropriate for corners or profiles where the manufacturer designed them for that purpose.

Selection must consider the material, surface size, required removal rate, access, finish quality and dust-control arrangement. A sander should not be selected merely because it is the nearest powered tool.

### 3. Understand abrasive progression
Coarse abrasives remove material rapidly but leave deeper scratches. Finer abrasives remove smaller amounts and refine the scratch pattern. A typical professional approach is to begin only as coarse as the actual surface condition requires and then progress through appropriate finer stages.

Skipping too far between abrasive grades may leave deep scratches that the finer abrasive struggles to remove efficiently. Staying too long with a very coarse abrasive may remove unnecessary material. Starting excessively fine on a rough or damaged surface may waste time, clog abrasive and tempt the operator to press harder.

There is no universal grit sequence for every timber, board, veneer or coating system. The carpenter should consider the material, required finish and product instructions. Before moving to a finer stage, inspect the surface under good lighting and confirm that the previous unwanted marks have been removed uniformly.

### 4. Inspect the sander and abrasive before use
Before connecting power or fitting a battery, inspect the sander according to the manufacturer's instructions and workplace procedure. Check the body, handles, switch, pad or platen, dust port, guards or covers where fitted, power lead and plug on corded equipment, or battery interface on cordless equipment.

Inspect the abrasive for tears, severe loading, incorrect size, damaged backing or poor attachment. A belt must be the correct type and size for the machine and fitted in the direction required by the product design. A disc or sheet must be securely attached and must not interfere with dust-extraction openings where the system depends on aligned holes.

Do not continue using equipment that has abnormal vibration, damaged electrical components, a defective switch, loose parts or other conditions that could make operation unsafe. Remove it from use and report it according to workplace procedure.

### 5. Secure the work and protect the work area
The workpiece must be stable. Small or lightweight components can move under a powered sander, creating both a safety problem and a quality defect. Use a suitable bench, vice, clamp, stop or other approved workholding arrangement while keeping clamps outside the sanding path.

Plan cable and hose routing before starting. A cord, extraction hose or loose material should not pull the sander sideways, cross a hazardous traffic route or enter the operating area. Ensure adequate lighting so scratch patterns, edge condition and surface defects can be seen.

The Department of Basic Education's woodworking assessment material reinforces safe handling and care of orbital sanders and specifically asks learners to understand cord positioning and regular removal of sanding dust. These are practical reminders that housekeeping and tool control form part of the sanding operation, not an afterthought.

### 6. Use controlled sanding technique
Place the tool according to the manufacturer's operating instructions. Maintain a stable stance and balanced grip. Allow the sander and abrasive to do the cutting rather than forcing the machine downward. Excessive pressure can reduce effective abrasive action, increase heat, overload the machine, create uneven removal and shorten abrasive life.

Move the sander in a controlled, systematic pattern so the entire required area receives consistent treatment. Avoid leaving an aggressive sander stationary on the surface. Keep the pad or platen properly supported; tilting can concentrate cutting on an edge and create grooves or rounded areas.

Pay special attention near corners, edges, veneer and already-correct dimensions. Stop periodically, allow moving parts to stop as required, and inspect the work. Do not use a running machine to brush or clear dust by hand.

### 7. Control wood dust at the source
Wood dust is not merely a housekeeping nuisance. South Africa's Hazardous Chemical Agents regulatory guidance explains that wood-working operations generate airborne dust and specifically notes that sanding can generate fine dust. Fine dust can remain airborne and be inhaled, and certain wood species can present additional sensitisation or other health concerns.

The preferred control strategy is to prevent or reduce exposure using stronger controls before relying on PPE alone. Where the equipment is designed for it, use effective on-tool extraction or other suitable local extraction connected and maintained according to manufacturer and workplace requirements. Keep extraction openings clear and empty or service collection systems safely as required.

Do not assume that a disposable dust bag automatically provides adequate exposure control for every task. The workplace risk assessment must consider the material being sanded, duration and intensity of exposure, extraction effectiveness, other people nearby and any additional substances such as old coatings, adhesives or treated materials. Suitable respiratory protection may be required, but its selection, fit, use and maintenance must follow the risk assessment and workplace respiratory-protection programme.

Dust should also be removed safely from surfaces and the work area. Avoid practices that simply redistribute fine dust into the air. Follow the workplace's approved vacuuming, extraction or cleaning method.

### 8. Inspect quality before moving to the next operation
After sanding, inspect the surface visually and by appropriate touch where safe. Use good lighting, including light across the surface where useful, to reveal scratches, hollows and uneven preparation.

Common defects include deep scratches remaining from an earlier abrasive, swirl or repetitive marks, rounded edges, uneven surfaces, clogged-abrasive marks, sanding through veneer, and dust trapped in pores or corners. The correct response is diagnosis, not automatic additional sanding.

Ask: Is the abrasive too coarse? Was a grade skipped? Was too much pressure used? Was the machine tilted? Is the abrasive loaded or damaged? Was the surface contaminated? Is the defect actually below the surface and therefore unsuitable for correction by further sanding?

Once the surface meets the specification, remove dust using the approved method and protect the component from contamination before finishing or assembly.

## Worked Example / Demonstration
A learner must prepare a solid-timber shelf for a clear finish. The shelf has light machining marks but no deep damage.

The learner first checks the finishing specification and selects a suitable random-orbit sander with compatible extraction. The workpiece is supported flat and clamped where the clamps cannot interfere with the sanding path. The learner inspects the machine, pad, cable, extraction connection and abrasive.

Instead of beginning with the coarsest abrasive available, the learner chooses a grade appropriate to the actual machining marks. The sander is moved systematically with light, controlled pressure. After the first stage, dust is safely removed and the surface is inspected under good lighting. The learner confirms that the original marks have been removed before progressing to the next suitable finer abrasive.

Near the edges, pressure is reduced and the machine is kept properly supported to avoid rounding the shelf. After the final specified stage, the learner stops, disconnects or makes the tool safe as required, cleans the surface using the approved dust-control method and checks for remaining scratches before sending the shelf to finishing.

The professional result comes from controlled progression and inspection, not from sanding for the longest possible time.

## Knowledge Check

**1. Why is it usually poor practice to begin every sanding job with the coarsest abrasive available?**
A. Coarse abrasive cannot remove timber.
B. It may create unnecessarily deep scratches and remove excessive material.
C. Fine abrasive is always faster.
D. Coarse abrasive can only be used by machine manufacturers.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Feedback:** Begin only as coarse as the actual surface condition requires. Unnecessary coarse sanding increases material removal and creates deeper scratches that later stages must remove.

**2. A belt sander is creating a hollow in one area of a board. What is the best immediate response?**
A. Press harder so the rest of the board matches the hollow.
B. Continue until the hollow disappears.
C. Stop and reassess technique, support, abrasive choice and the amount of material being removed.
D. Wet the abrasive while the machine is running.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Feedback:** Further uncontrolled sanding can worsen the defect. Diagnose the cause before removing more material.

**3. Why is dust extraction important during powered sanding?**
A. Sanding dust is only a visual problem.
B. Fine wood dust can become airborne, so exposure should be controlled as close to the source as practicable.
C. Extraction eliminates every need for risk assessment.
D. Extraction allows damaged abrasive to be used safely.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Feedback:** Effective source control reduces airborne dust. PPE may still be required depending on the risk assessment, but it should not be treated as a substitute for stronger controls.

**4. Deep scratches remain after the learner has moved to a much finer abrasive. What is a likely cause?**
A. The learner may have progressed before removing the previous scratch pattern or skipped too large a grit step.
B. The timber must always be discarded.
C. The finer abrasive automatically creates deeper scratches.
D. The workpiece was clamped securely.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Feedback:** Each stage should remove the significant scratch pattern from the previous stage before progressing. Inspect between stages instead of relying only on elapsed sanding time.

## Knowledge Check Feedback — Professional Sequence
A reliable sanding sequence is:

**Inspect surface → define required result → select sander → select abrasive → inspect tool → secure work → connect effective dust control → sand with controlled pressure and movement → inspect → progress appropriately → clean safely → final quality check.**

## Key Takeaways
- Sanding removes material and can change dimensions and geometry.
- Select the sander according to the material, access, removal rate and required finish.
- Start only as coarse as necessary and progress through suitable abrasive stages.
- Inspect the machine, abrasive, workholding and dust-control arrangement before use.
- Excessive pressure and stationary sanding can damage the workpiece and overload the process.
- Fine wood dust generated during sanding is an occupational-health concern and should be controlled at source.
- PPE supports, but does not replace, effective engineering and workplace controls.
- Inspect between sanding stages and diagnose defects before removing more material.
- A professional sanded surface is one that meets the specification and is ready for the next operation, not simply one that feels smooth.
