# Carpentry — Professional Skills Short Course

## Module 3: Power Tools & Safe Operating Practice
### Lesson 5: Routers & Edge/Forming Operations

**Estimated study time:** 40–45 minutes

## Learning Outcomes
By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

1. explain the main carpentry uses of a portable router and distinguish common routing operations;
2. select an appropriate router bit and routing method for the required task;
3. complete a systematic pre-use inspection of the router, bit, workpiece and work area;
4. describe safe, controlled procedures for edge profiling, rebates, grooves and guided routing;
5. recognise common routing defects, unsafe conditions and situations that require stopping, reporting or competent supervision.

## Why This Lesson Matters
A router is one of the most versatile portable power tools used in carpentry. With the correct bit, setup and guiding method, it can form decorative edges, cut grooves and rebates, trim edges, prepare joints and produce repeated shapes. The same versatility also means the tool demands careful planning. A router bit rotates at very high speed, and poor bit selection, loose workholding, incorrect feed, unsupported material or unauthorised adjustment can quickly create an unsafe situation or damage the workpiece.

Professional routing is therefore not simply about switching on a machine and following a pencil line. The operator must understand the required profile, confirm the condition and compatibility of the router and bit, secure the work, establish a stable reference or guide, control the cutting sequence and inspect the result. South African DBE woodworking guidance reinforces key principles such as wearing eye protection, disconnecting the power supply before replacing router bits, avoiding adjustments while the bit is rotating, keeping the bit clear of the workpiece at startup, and firmly securing the workpiece.

## Key Terms / Vocabulary

**Router** — A portable power tool that rotates a shaped cutting bit at high speed to remove timber or board material for profiling, grooving, trimming, rebating and related operations.

**Router bit** — The replaceable cutting accessory fitted into the router collet. Bits are manufactured in many shapes and sizes for different operations and must be compatible with the router and intended material.

**Collet** — The clamping component that grips the router-bit shank. The collet must be clean, undamaged and correctly tightened according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

**Rebate** — A rectangular step or recess cut along the edge or face of a workpiece, often used to receive another component, panel or back.

**Groove** — A channel cut into the face or edge of a workpiece, generally to receive another component or for a functional or decorative purpose.

**Profile** — The shaped form produced along an edge, such as a round-over, chamfer or other moulded contour.

**Guide** — A fence, bearing, template or straightedge used to control the path of the router and improve repeatability and accuracy.

**Feed direction** — The direction in which the router is moved relative to the rotation of the bit. The correct direction depends on the tool, operation and manufacturer guidance and is critical for control.

## Main Lesson Content

### 1. Understand the operation before selecting the tool setup

A router should be selected and prepared only after the required result is understood. The operator must first answer: What shape or recess is required? Where must it be positioned? How deep or wide should it be? Which face or edge is the reference? What material is being routed? Is the job a one-off operation or a repeated cut that would benefit from a guide or template?

Common carpentry routing operations include edge profiling, rebates, grooves, trimming, template-guided shaping and joint preparation. Different operations place different demands on the bit, guide, depth setting and workholding. A decorative edge profile is not prepared in the same way as a straight groove in the face of a board. Similarly, trimming a laminate or board edge with a bearing-guided bit requires different control from routing a rebate against a fence.

The learner should not improvise with a bit that merely looks similar to the desired shape. The bit must be intended for the task, material and router. Workplace procedures, manufacturer instructions and competent supervision take priority over general guidance.

### 2. Select and inspect the router bit carefully

Router bits vary in profile, cutting diameter, shank size, cutting-edge design and intended use. Common examples include straight bits, rebate bits, flush-trim bits, round-over bits and chamfer bits. Some bits use a guide bearing; others depend on a fence, template or the router base for guidance.

Before fitting a bit, the router must be disconnected from its power source or otherwise made safe according to the manufacturer’s procedure. On a battery-powered router, this normally includes removing the battery where the design allows. The operator should inspect the bit for chips, cracks, obvious damage, contamination or abnormal wear. A damaged bit must not be used. The shank and collet should be clean and in sound condition so that the bit can be held securely.

The bit must be fitted and tightened exactly as required by the tool manufacturer. It should not be inserted or clamped using an improvised method. The operator should also confirm that the bit is compatible with the router’s collet and operating limits. Do not invent speed settings or maximum diameters; these are manufacturer-specific.

### 3. Inspect the router and prepare the work area

A pre-use router inspection should include the housing, base, handles, switches, depth controls, power lead or battery interface, collet area, guards or protective features, and any fitted fence or guide. Loose parts, cracked components, exposed conductors, damaged plugs, abnormal movement or unreliable controls require the tool to be removed from use and reported according to workplace procedure.

The work area should be well lit, stable and free from unnecessary obstructions. Leads should be routed so they cannot be cut or create a trip hazard. Dust extraction should be connected and used where provided and required. Fine woodworking dust can affect health and visibility, so dust control and suitable respiratory protection must follow the workplace risk assessment and manufacturer guidance.

Eye protection is essential because routing can eject chips and debris. Hearing protection may also be required because routers can generate significant noise. Loose clothing, jewellery and unsecured hair should be kept away from moving parts.

### 4. Secure the workpiece and establish a reliable guide

The workpiece must be firmly secured before the router is started. A board that shifts under the tool can cause the bit to move away from the intended path, damage the profile and reduce operator control. Clamps should be positioned so they do not interfere with the router base, handles, guide or intended travel.

For a straight cut, a straightedge or fence may be clamped as a guide. For repeated shapes, a template may be appropriate. Bearing-guided bits can follow a workpiece edge or template, but the bearing and reference edge still need to be clean, continuous and suitable for guiding.

Before cutting, the operator should perform a dry run with the router switched off. This means moving the tool through the intended travel path to check whether the base will remain supported, whether clamps will interfere, whether the guide is reachable throughout the cut and whether the operator can maintain a balanced stance. This simple check can prevent many routing errors.

### 5. Set cutting depth conservatively and plan the sequence

Routing quality improves when the operator controls how much material is removed in a pass. Deep or heavy cuts can overload the bit and router, increase heat, reduce surface quality and make the tool more difficult to control. For many operations, several controlled passes are preferable to forcing a large cut in one movement.

The exact depth and number of passes depend on the router, bit, material and manufacturer guidance. The learner should therefore avoid memorising arbitrary universal figures. Instead, use the principle of progressive material removal: set the tool carefully, verify the setup, make a controlled pass, inspect the result and adjust only when the tool is fully stopped and made safe.

No adjustment should be made while the bit is rotating. The operator must wait until all motion has stopped before setting the router down or changing depth, guide position or bit.

### 6. Start and guide the router under full control

The router should normally be held securely with both hands where the tool design provides two handles. Before starting, the bit should be clear of the workpiece unless the manufacturer specifies another procedure. The operator should establish a stable stance, ensure the base is properly supported and confirm the guide is engaged.

Once started, allow the tool to reach its operating condition before entering the cut. Feed the router smoothly rather than forcing it. Excessive force can reduce control and surface quality and may overload the tool. Moving too slowly may create excessive heat or burn marks. The correct feed should sound and feel controlled rather than strained.

Feed direction is important because the rotating cutter creates a reaction force against the router. The correct direction depends on whether the operation is along an external edge, an internal opening or another guided path. Learners should therefore follow the tool manufacturer’s instructions and workplace training rather than applying a guessed rule in every situation. If the router unexpectedly pulls, chatters, climbs or becomes difficult to control, stop safely and reassess the setup, feed direction, workholding, bit and cut depth.

### 7. Protect edge quality and avoid breakout

Wood fibres can break away at corners or on unsupported edges. When routing around an external profile, a careless sequence may cause the cutter to tear fibres as it exits an edge. Professional operators plan the direction and sequence of cuts to protect vulnerable corners and may use light preliminary passes or supporting material where appropriate.

The workpiece should also be checked for loose knots, embedded metal, screws, nails or other foreign objects that could damage the bit or create a hazard. Never deliberately route into unknown fixings or hidden services. If the cutting path may contain electrical wiring, plumbing, structural fasteners or other concealed hazards, the work must stop until the condition is verified using an authorised method.

### 8. Inspect the routed result and respond to defects

After the router is switched off, allow the bit to stop completely before putting the tool down. Disconnect or isolate the tool before changing the setup. Then inspect the work for correct dimension, depth, profile consistency, straightness, clean edges and signs of burning, chatter or tear-out.

Common defects include wandering cuts, uneven depth, burn marks, chipped edges, excessive tear-out, ridges between passes and poorly defined profiles. These defects are not automatically solved by making another cut. The operator should identify the cause first. Possible causes include a loose guide, unstable workpiece, dull or unsuitable bit, excessive cut depth, poor feed control, inadequate base support or incorrect reference setup.

A professional carpenter corrects the cause before continuing. Repeated uncontrolled passes can remove too much material and turn a repairable problem into scrap.

## Worked Example / Demonstration

A learner must form a straight rebate along the rear edge of a timber side panel so that a backing panel can fit neatly into it.

First, the learner checks the drawing and confirms the rebate position, width and depth. The correct router and suitable straight or rebate bit are selected. With the router disconnected from its power source, the learner checks the bit and collet, fits the accessory correctly and verifies the router base and controls.

The workpiece is placed on a stable bench and clamped securely. A straight guide is positioned using the verified reference edge. Before switching on, the learner performs a dry run and confirms that the router base will remain supported and that no clamps interfere with the path.

The learner starts with a conservative cut, keeps the router under two-handed control and follows the guide smoothly. The cut is completed without forcing the tool. After the bit has fully stopped, the learner disconnects the router, checks rebate depth and straightness, and only then decides whether a further controlled pass is required.

If the first pass shows an uneven depth, the learner does not simply cut deeper. The workholding, guide position, router base support and depth setting are checked first.

## Knowledge Check

### Question 1
Why should the router normally be disconnected from its power source before changing a router bit?

A. To make the timber softer
B. To prevent unintended startup during the bit change
C. To make the router run faster later
D. To reduce the width of the cut

**Correct answer: B**

**Feedback:** Disconnecting or otherwise making the router safe prevents unintended startup while the operator is working close to the cutting accessory.

### Question 2
A workpiece moves slightly when the router contacts it. What is the best response?

A. Push the router harder to keep it on the line
B. Continue because the guide will correct the problem
C. Stop safely and improve the workholding before continuing
D. Hold the workpiece with one hand while routing with the other

**Correct answer: C**

**Feedback:** Secure workholding is essential. A moving workpiece reduces control, accuracy and safety.

### Question 3
A deep routing operation produces heavy vibration and burning. What is the most appropriate first action?

A. Increase feed force
B. Stop and reassess cut depth, bit condition, setup and feed
C. Remove the guide
D. Spray water on the bit

**Correct answer: B**

**Feedback:** Abnormal vibration and burning indicate a problem. The operator should stop safely and diagnose the cause instead of forcing the cut.

### Question 4
What should happen before the router is placed on the bench after a cut?

A. The operator should wait until the bit has stopped completely
B. The depth should be adjusted immediately while the bit slows down
C. The bit should touch the bench so it stops faster
D. The guide should be removed while the tool is still running

**Correct answer: A**

**Feedback:** The cutter must be allowed to stop completely before the router is put down or adjustments are made.

## Feedback / Explanation for Knowledge Check
A safe routing sequence can be remembered as:

**Plan → Select → Disconnect → Inspect → Fit → Secure → Guide → Dry-run → Start clear → Cut under control → Stop fully → Disconnect → Inspect quality.**

If the router behaves unexpectedly, do not compensate with extra force. Stop, make the tool safe and identify the cause.

## Key Takeaways

1. Routers are versatile but require precise setup and controlled operation.
2. Select the router bit for the specific profile, groove, rebate or trimming task.
3. Disconnect or make the router safe before fitting bits or making adjustments.
4. Inspect the tool, bit, collet, guide, workpiece and work area before use.
5. Secure the workpiece firmly and perform a dry run before cutting.
6. Use controlled, progressive cuts rather than forcing excessive material removal.
7. Follow manufacturer and workplace guidance for feed direction and operating settings.
8. Allow the bit to stop completely before putting the router down or adjusting the setup.
9. Diagnose routing defects before removing more material.
